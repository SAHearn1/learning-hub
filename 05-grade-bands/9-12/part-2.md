# 9-12 Regulate Phase: Proactive Self-Regulation

## Overview

> **Core Question**: "Can I return to my window of tolerance?"

At the 9-12 level, the Regulate phase reaches its most advanced form: proactive self-regulation. Students have progressed through the full developmental arc -- from co-regulation (K-2), to prompted self-regulation (3-5), to independent self-regulation (6-8), and now to proactive regulation where they anticipate triggers, deploy preventive strategies, and support peers in their regulation journeys. The educator serves as a regulation consultant, not a regulation director.

This phase draws on Dan Siegel's window of tolerance model and Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory, applied through the lens of adolescent neuroscience and the unique stressors of high school life.

## Developmental Context for Proactive Regulation

### Why Proactive Regulation Matters for Ages 14-18

- **Academic intensity**: AP/honors coursework, standardized testing (SAT, ACT), and college applications create sustained stress that requires ongoing regulation -- not just crisis intervention.
- **Executive function maturation**: The prefrontal cortex is developing rapidly but remains under construction until the mid-twenties. Students can plan regulation strategies but may still be overwhelmed by limbic hijack.
- **Emotional complexity**: Adolescents experience emotions with adult intensity but without fully developed adult coping mechanisms. Romantic relationships, social hierarchies, identity exploration, and family dynamics all contribute to dysregulation risk.
- **Transition to adulthood**: The regulation skills developed in this phase directly transfer to college, career, and life success.

## The Four Stages of Self-Regulation Mastery

By grades 9-12, students should be operating at Stages 3 and 4 of the self-regulation continuum.

| Stage | Description | Grade Band Focus | 9-12 Expectation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1: Co-Regulation | Adult identifies dysregulation and initiates strategies | K-2 | Foundational only |
| 2: Prompted Self-Regulation | Adult helps student notice; student selects from offered strategies | 3-5 | Review as needed |
| 3: Independent Self-Regulation | Student recognizes dysregulation and initiates strategies independently | 6-8 / 9-12 | Consistent demonstration |
| 4: Proactive Regulation | Student anticipates challenges and engages strategies preventively | 9-12 | Target mastery |

## Physiological Awareness and Biofeedback

### Body Literacy Curriculum

Students in grades 9-12 develop sophisticated awareness of their physiological regulation signals through a **Body Literacy** approach.

| System | Dysregulation Signal | Proactive Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | Elevated heart rate, chest tightness, flushing | Box breathing (4-4-4-4), cold water on wrists, paced exhale breathing |
| **Muscular** | Jaw clenching, shoulder tension, fist tightening | Progressive muscle relaxation, garden digging, stretching |
| **Digestive** | Stomach churning, nausea, loss of appetite | Herbal tea from garden (chamomile, mint), mindful eating, grounding |
| **Respiratory** | Shallow breathing, breath holding, sighing | Diaphragmatic breathing, outdoor air, garden aromatherapy |
| **Cognitive** | Racing thoughts, mental fog, intrusive memories | Thought labeling, grounding in present via 5-4-3-2-1, journaling |
| **Behavioral** | Avoidance, irritability, withdrawal, restlessness | Movement break, peer check-in, mentor role activation |

### Biofeedback Integration (Optional Technology Enhancement)

Where available, students may use simple biofeedback tools to develop physiological awareness:

- Heart rate variability (HRV) monitors during mindfulness practice
- Breathing pace apps with visual feedback
- Mood and regulation tracking apps with pattern analysis
- Garden-based physiological experiments (measuring heart rate before and after garden work)

## Proactive Regulation Strategies

### The Regulation Forecast

Students learn to conduct a **Regulation Forecast** at the start of each day or before challenging activities.

**Regulation Forecast Protocol:**
1. **Scan the day**: What classes, social situations, or events might be challenging?
2. **Rate the risk**: On a 1-5 scale, how likely is dysregulation today?
3. **Pre-load strategies**: What regulation tools will I have ready?
4. **Identify supports**: Who can I turn to if I need co-regulation?
5. **Set a check-in time**: When will I pause and reassess during the day?

### Anticipatory Regulation Menu

| Anticipated Stressor | Proactive Strategy | Garden Connection |
|---|---|---|
| High-stakes test or presentation | Morning mindfulness, review of past successes, power posing | Tend a plant before the test -- nurturing something calms the nervous system |
| Conflict with a peer or family member | Journaling before class, identifying emotions, planning a response (not a reaction) | Weeding as a metaphor -- remove what does not serve growth |
| Emotionally charged content (literature, history) | Pre-reading with emotional preparation, identifying personal triggers, grounding anchors ready | Sitting in the garden's quiet space to build emotional reserves |
| Cumulative academic pressure | Time management review, prioritization, self-compassion practice | Observing seasonal cycles -- growth takes time; not everything blooms at once |
| Trauma anniversary or personal crisis | Connecting with school counselor proactively, adjusting expectations, increasing grounding frequency | Planting something new -- a tangible act of hope and forward movement |

## Educator Language Models

### Validating Without Rescuing
- "I can see this is a hard day. What's your plan for managing it?"
- "You seem like you're carrying a lot right now. What strategy from your toolkit feels right?"
- "I trust you to handle this, and I'm here as backup if you need me."

### Coaching Proactive Regulation
- "Tomorrow is the AP exam. What's your regulation plan for the morning?"
- "I noticed you were starting to tense up during that reading. What did you do to stay in your window?"
- "You anticipated that group discussion would be tough and prepared. That's exactly what proactive regulation looks like."

### Normalizing the Need for Regulation
- "Even experienced adults use regulation strategies before high-pressure situations. This is not a weakness -- it is a skill."
- "The most successful people I know have a regulation practice. Athletes, surgeons, musicians -- they all ground before performing."
- "Your brain is doing exactly what brains do under stress. The difference is that you know how to respond to it."

## Peer Mentoring in Regulation

### Regulation Partners

Students in grades 9-12 may serve as **Regulation Partners** for each other and for younger students.

**Peer Regulation Partner Guidelines:**
1. **Listen first**: Do not immediately suggest strategies. Ask: "What's going on?" and "What do you need?"
2. **Offer, don't impose**: "Would it help if we took a walk to the garden?" not "You need to go breathe."
3. **Model calm**: Your own regulation is the most powerful co-regulation tool. Be a non-anxious presence.
4. **Know your limits**: You are a peer, not a therapist. If a situation exceeds your ability, involve an adult.
5. **Debrief afterward**: Check in with yourself after supporting a peer. Helping others regulate can be draining.

### Mentoring Younger Students in Regulation

| Younger Grade Band | Mentor's Regulation Role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| K-2 | Calm presence during co-regulation activities | Sit with a kindergartner and do breathing exercises together during garden time |
| 3-5 | Guide student to notice and name their feelings | "I notice your shoulders are up by your ears. What does that tell you about how you're feeling?" |
| 6-8 | Support independent strategy selection | "You know your regulation tools. Which one feels right for you right now?" |

## Mindfulness Practice

### Structured Mindfulness Curriculum for 9-12

Mindfulness is integrated into the Regulate phase as a daily practice, not just a crisis intervention.

| Week Focus | Practice | Duration | Garden Integration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Breath Awareness** | Focused attention on breath; counting exhales | 3-5 min | Practice in the garden; synchronize breath with wind patterns |
| **Body Scan** | Progressive attention through body regions | 5-7 min | Lie on garden ground; feel connection to earth |
| **Thought Observation** | Label thoughts as "planning," "worrying," "remembering" without judgment | 3-5 min | Watch garden insects move without interfering -- observe without reacting |
| **Emotional Awareness** | Name emotions without suppressing or amplifying | 3-5 min | Journal emotions while sitting among plants; notice parallels to growth cycles |
| **Compassion Practice** | Self-compassion and extending compassion to others | 5-7 min | Water a struggling plant with care; extend the same care inward |
| **Open Awareness** | Expansive attention to all sensory input without focus on any single element | 5-10 min | Sit in garden center with eyes closed; let all sounds, smells, and sensations arrive |

## Stress Management for Academic Demands

### The Academic Stress Regulation Toolkit

| Stressor | Dysregulation Risk | Regulation Strategy | Evidence Base |
|---|---|---|---|
| Test anxiety | Hyperarousal: racing heart, blanking, panic | Pre-test grounding ritual, cognitive reframing ("This is excitement, not fear"), box breathing | Jamieson et al. (2010) -- arousal reappraisal |
| Procrastination cycle | Avoidance leading to crisis | Break tasks into 15-minute garden-and-study intervals, accountability partner | Pomodoro Technique; self-determination theory |
| Perfectionism | Chronic hyperarousal, fear of error | Growth mindset journaling, Restore phase integration, self-compassion practice | Dweck (2006); Neff (2011) |
| Overwhelm from multiple demands | Shutdown, withdrawal | Priority matrix in journal, "one next step" focus, garden time for reset | Covey time management; executive function research |
| Social comparison | Shame, inadequacy, withdrawal | Gratitude practice, focus on personal growth trajectory, mentor role (giving shifts focus from self) | Emmons & McCullough (2003) |

## Sample Regulate Phase Session

### Scenario: Student Arrives Agitated Before a Chemistry Exam

| Step | Time | Action | Language |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Recognition | 0:00 | Student enters visibly tense; self-rates at 35 | Student: "I'm at a 35. I'm freaking out about the test." |
| 2. Validation | 0:15 | Educator acknowledges without minimizing | "That makes sense. Chemistry exams are high stakes. What's your plan?" |
| 3. Strategy Selection | 0:30 | Student chooses from personal toolkit | "I'm going to do my breathing and then look at my study guide for two minutes." |
| 4. Execution | 0:45 | Student performs 2 minutes of box breathing at desk | Educator maintains calm presence, does own grounding |
| 5. Reassessment | 2:45 | Student re-rates | "I'm at a 55 now. Better. I think I can do this." |
| 6. Bridge to Root | 3:00 | Brief intention setting | "Okay. My plan is to read each question carefully and use DECOMPOSE if I get stuck." |
| 7. Transition | 3:30 | Student signals readiness | "I'm ready." Educator: "You've got this. Trust your preparation." |

## Assessment Indicators

### Proficiency Indicators

- **Emerging**: Student recognizes dysregulation only after it escalates; relies on adult intervention; limited strategy repertoire
- **Developing**: Student identifies dysregulation independently but sometimes after a delay; uses 2-3 strategies with moderate effectiveness
- **Proficient**: Student identifies dysregulation early and selects effective strategies; returns to window of tolerance within 3-5 minutes; supports peers
- **Distinguished**: Student anticipates dysregulation and engages proactive strategies; maintains regulation through high-stress events; effectively mentors others in regulation skills

### Portfolio Evidence
- Regulation Forecast logs (weekly)
- Mindfulness practice journal (ongoing)
- Peer mentoring reflections (monthly)
- Self-regulation growth narrative (quarterly -- student-authored analysis of their regulation development)

## Transition Out of Regulate

At the 9-12 level, the transition out of Regulate is student-directed:

- **To Root**: Student returns to a brief grounding check after regulation stabilizes (self-rating returns above 60)
- **To Reflect**: If regulation was brief and student feels fully ready, they may transition directly to learning
- **Student language**: "I'm back in my window. Let's go." or "I need to stay here a bit longer." or "I think I need to talk to someone."
- **Educator monitoring**: Even when students self-direct, the educator observes for masked dysregulation and checks in non-intrusively if the stated readiness does not match observed behavior
