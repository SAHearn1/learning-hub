# 9-12 Grade Band Part 4: Social-Emotional Learning & Regulation

## Overview

Social-emotional learning in grades 9-12 addresses the most complex developmental terrain in the K-12 spectrum. High school students are forming adult identities, navigating increasingly consequential relationships, preparing for post-secondary transitions, and developing the capacity for abstract moral reasoning. For students in the RootWork program—many of whom have experienced significant adversity—the garden enterprise provides a structured context for practicing adult-level social-emotional skills: professional communication, ethical leadership, financial stewardship, community accountability, and sustained commitment to long-term goals.

The SEL framework for 9-12 moves beyond skill-building toward identity integration. Students are not simply learning regulation strategies or conflict resolution techniques—they are developing a coherent sense of who they are, what they value, and how they want to contribute to their communities. The garden serves as both metaphor and practice ground for this integration: tending a garden over time requires patience, responsibility, adaptability, and care—the same qualities that support healthy adult functioning.

For students with trauma histories, the 9-12 years are a critical window. Many are aging out of child welfare systems, transitioning to independent living, or facing re-exposure to adverse conditions without the protective factor of childhood services. The RootWork SEL framework explicitly addresses these transitions, building the self-awareness, relationship skills, and decision-making capacity that support successful adult adjustment.

## CASEL Framework Alignment for Grades 9-12

### Self-Awareness

High school students are capable of sophisticated self-reflection, including understanding their own cognitive patterns, emotional tendencies, cultural positioning, and the ways trauma has shaped their development. Self-awareness at this level is not just knowing what you feel—it is understanding why you feel it, how it connects to your history, and how it influences your choices.

**Core Competencies:**
- Integrating emotional, cognitive, and somatic self-awareness into a coherent self-understanding
- Recognizing how personal history, culture, privilege, and adversity shape identity and perception
- Developing an accurate and compassionate self-concept that includes both strengths and growth areas
- Understanding personal values and aligning behavior with those values
- Recognizing cognitive biases and their influence on perception and decision-making

**Garden-Based Self-Awareness Activities:**

| Activity | Description | SEL Focus |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Identity Ecology | Create a detailed ecosystem map of your identity: what environments nurture you, what stresses you, what your "keystone" values are, where you are resilient and where you are vulnerable | Complex identity understanding, systems thinking about self |
| Adversity Integration Narrative | Write a narrative that explores how a difficult experience shaped who you are today—not as something that defines you, but as one element of your ecosystem | Trauma integration, narrative identity, post-traumatic growth |
| Values Audit | Track decisions for a week. Categorize each decision by which value it served. Identify gaps between stated values and lived behavior | Values clarification, integrity, honest self-assessment |
| Cognitive Bias Field Guide | Document your own cognitive biases as you observe them in garden enterprise decisions (confirmation bias, sunk cost fallacy, anchoring, etc.) | Metacognition, intellectual humility, decision-making |
| Somatic Mapping | Create a detailed body map showing where you hold different emotions, what your body's early warning signals are, and what physical practices help you regulate | Mind-body integration, interoception, regulation |

**TRACE Integration for Self-Awareness:**
- **Think:** "Before you respond, pause. What is your first impulse? What emotion is driving it? What assumption is behind it?"
- **Reason:** "Trace that impulse back. Where does it come from? Is it serving you right now, or is it a pattern from a different context?"
- **Articulate:** "Name what you are experiencing with precision. There is a difference between anger and frustration, between sadness and grief, between anxiety and anticipation."
- **Check:** "Test your self-assessment against evidence. Ask a trusted peer or mentor: 'Is this how you see it?'"
- **Extend:** "Given what you know about yourself, what is the wisest choice right now? Not the easiest—the wisest."

### Self-Management

Self-management in high school encompasses academic self-regulation, emotional regulation under pressure, professional conduct, long-term goal pursuit, and healthy coping with stress. The garden enterprise demands all of these simultaneously, providing authentic practice.

**Core Competencies:**
- Managing complex emotional experiences, including grief, rage, shame, and existential uncertainty
- Pursuing long-term goals through sustained effort despite obstacles and setbacks
- Organizing and managing complex projects with multiple deadlines and stakeholders
- Practicing healthy stress management and self-care
- Developing professional conduct skills (punctuality, follow-through, communication)
- Managing digital life, social media, and online identity intentionally

**Regulation Strategy Toolkit for 9-12:**

| Strategy | Mechanism | Evidence Base | Application |
|----------|-----------|---------------|-------------|
| Physiological Sigh | Double inhale through nose, long exhale through mouth | Huberman Lab research on real-time stress reduction | Immediate calming in high-stress moments (presentations, conflicts, test anxiety) |
| Non-Sleep Deep Rest (NSDR) | 10-20 minute guided relaxation protocol | Research on deliberate rest and learning consolidation | After intense cognitive work; before creative tasks; managing exhaustion |
| Cognitive Defusion (ACT) | Observe thoughts as mental events, not facts. "I notice I am having the thought that..." | Acceptance and Commitment Therapy research | Rumination, catastrophizing, self-critical thought spirals |
| Values-Based Action | When overwhelmed, identify one small action aligned with a core value | ACT research on values-driven behavior | When paralyzed by complexity or despair; re-establishing agency |
| Bilateral Stimulation | Walking, drumming, alternating tapping while processing difficult content | EMDR-informed practices; bilateral engagement research | Processing traumatic memories; managing flashbacks (in consultation with therapist) |
| Expressive Writing | 15-minute timed writing about emotional experience without editing | Pennebaker research on expressive writing and health | Processing complex emotions; before and after difficult conversations |
| Physical Exertion | Intense garden labor: digging, hauling, building | Exercise physiology; cortisol reduction through physical activity | High arousal states; pent-up energy; anger; feeling "stuck" |
| Social Co-Regulation | Seeking safe connection with a trusted person | Polyvagal theory; social engagement system | When self-regulation is insufficient; loneliness; after interpersonal injury |

**Personal Regulation Plan (PRP) for 9-12:**

The high school PRP is a sophisticated self-management document:

1. **My Nervous System Profile:** "I tend toward [hyperarousal / hypoarousal / fluctuation]. My window of tolerance is [description of optimal zone]."
2. **My Triggers and Patterns:** "I am most likely to become dysregulated when [specific situations]. The pattern usually looks like [description of escalation sequence]."
3. **My Early Warning System:** "The first signs that I am leaving my window of tolerance are [physical, emotional, cognitive, behavioral indicators]."
4. **My Regulation Toolkit:** "My most effective strategies for [hyperarousal / hypoarousal / dissociation] are [specific strategies with instructions]."
5. **My Support Network:** "People I trust to help me regulate: [names and how to access them]."
6. **My Crisis Plan:** "If I experience a crisis (panic attack, flashback, overwhelming urge to self-harm, suicidal ideation), I will: [specific steps including crisis hotline numbers]."
7. **My Recovery Protocol:** "After a dysregulated episode, I help myself get back on track by [specific steps]. I may need [time, space, activity] before I am ready to re-engage."
8. **My Growth Goals:** "I am working on improving my ability to [specific regulation skill]. My evidence of progress will be [measurable indicators]."

The PRP is confidential and co-developed with the student and a trusted adult (educator, counselor, or therapist). It is reviewed and updated quarterly. Students who have experienced significant trauma may develop their PRP in coordination with a mental health provider.

### Social Awareness

Social awareness in grades 9-12 extends beyond interpersonal empathy to include systemic awareness, critical consciousness, and the capacity to analyze how social structures, power dynamics, and historical legacies affect individuals and communities.

**Core Competencies:**
- Demonstrating empathy across significant differences in experience, identity, and worldview
- Analyzing how systems of power, privilege, and oppression affect access and opportunity
- Understanding intersectionality and how multiple identity factors interact
- Reading social and organizational dynamics accurately
- Engaging respectfully and productively across political, cultural, and ideological differences
- Understanding the historical context of current social conditions

**Garden-Based Social Awareness Activities:**

| Activity | Description | SEL Focus |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Food Justice Audit | Conduct a comprehensive analysis of food access in your community: map food sources, analyze pricing, interview community members, examine demographic patterns, research historical policies that shaped current conditions | Systemic awareness, research skills, community empathy |
| Land Acknowledgment and History Project | Research the complete agricultural history of your garden's land: Indigenous stewardship, colonial appropriation, enslaved labor, land use changes, environmental justice issues | Historical consciousness, systemic analysis, accountability |
| Enterprise Equity Assessment | Analyze who benefits from the garden enterprise and who might be excluded. Examine pricing, distribution, employment, leadership representation, and community voice in decision-making | Equity analysis, critical self-examination, inclusive design |
| Cross-Cultural Agricultural Exchange | Partner with a garden or farm in a different community (urban/rural, different state/country). Exchange growing techniques, recipes, and stories. Analyze the systemic factors that create different agricultural realities | Perspective-taking, global awareness, systemic analysis |
| Privilege and Access Reflection | Personal reflective writing: "What advantages and disadvantages have shaped my relationship with food, land, and education? How does my position affect what I see and don't see?" | Self-awareness, empathy, intellectual humility |

### Relationship Skills

Relationship skills in grades 9-12 focus on the skills needed for adult life: professional communication, healthy intimate relationships (at an age-appropriate level), cross-generational collaboration, conflict resolution in high-stakes situations, and leadership.

**Core Competencies:**
- Communicating effectively across power differentials (with administrators, employers, community leaders)
- Building and maintaining professional relationships
- Navigating romantic relationships with respect, consent, and communication (addressed in coordination with health curriculum)
- Mentoring younger students effectively and empathetically
- Leading collaboratively, sharing power, and supporting others' growth
- Advocating for self and others in institutional contexts

**Collaborative Structures:**

**Garden Enterprise Teams:**
- Teams function as professional units with defined roles (CEO, CFO, Operations Manager, Marketing Director, Research Lead)
- Roles involve real responsibility with real consequences (financial decisions, community commitments, event planning)
- Teams operate with significant autonomy, checking in with the educator-advisor weekly
- Performance reviews conducted quarterly with self-assessment, peer feedback, and advisor input

**Mentorship Program:**
- Each 11th-12th grader mentors a 6th-8th grade student in garden research and enterprise skills
- Mentors receive training in: active listening, TRACE facilitation, developmental awareness, boundary-setting, and when to refer to an adult
- Mentors document their mentorship experience in a reflective portfolio
- Monthly mentor supervision meetings address challenges, celebrate successes, and develop skills

**Restorative Justice Practices:**

High school conflicts are addressed through a full restorative justice model:

1. **Restorative Conference:** For significant harm. A trained facilitator guides a structured conversation between the person who caused harm, the person who was harmed, and affected community members. The conference follows the Zehr model:
   - What happened?
   - Who was affected and how?
   - What needs to happen to make things right?
   - How do we prevent this from happening again?

2. **Circle Process:** For community-level issues (trust erosion, exclusion patterns, morale). A talking piece circulates; each person speaks without interruption. The circle addresses:
   - What is our community experiencing right now?
   - What does each of us need?
   - What commitments will we make to each other?

3. **Peer Mediation:** Trained student mediators facilitate resolution of interpersonal conflicts using a structured protocol. Peer mediators receive 20 hours of training in active listening, neutrality, de-escalation, and when to escalate to an adult.

### Responsible Decision-Making

Decision-making in grades 9-12 encompasses ethical reasoning, professional judgment, civic responsibility, and planning for the future. The garden enterprise provides daily practice in consequential decision-making.

**Core Competencies:**
- Analyzing complex situations using multiple ethical frameworks (utilitarian, deontological, virtue ethics, care ethics)
- Making decisions that balance self-interest with community well-being
- Taking responsibility for decisions and their consequences, including unintended ones
- Planning for the future with realistic assessment of options and obstacles
- Engaging in civic participation and advocacy based on evidence and values
- Navigating institutional systems (financial aid, housing, healthcare, employment) effectively

**Ethical Decision-Making Framework:**

When facing a complex decision, students apply:

| Step | Question | Tool |
|------|----------|------|
| 1. Identify | What is the decision to be made? What are the relevant facts? | Situation analysis |
| 2. Stakeholders | Who is affected? What are their interests and perspectives? | Stakeholder mapping |
| 3. Options | What are all possible courses of action? (Generate at least four) | Brainstorming |
| 4. Ethical Analysis | Apply multiple ethical lenses: What maximizes well-being? What respects rights? What would a virtuous person do? What maintains relationships? | Ethical framework analysis |
| 5. Consequences | What are the likely consequences of each option for each stakeholder? | Consequence mapping |
| 6. Decision | Choose the option that best aligns with your values and ethical analysis | Decision matrix |
| 7. Action | Implement with integrity. Communicate transparently. | Action plan |
| 8. Reflect | What happened? What would you do differently? What did you learn? | Reflective practice |

**Real Enterprise Dilemmas:**

| Dilemma | Complexity | Skills Practiced |
|---------|-----------|-----------------|
| A team member is consistently absent but has a difficult home situation. Do you address performance or accommodate their circumstances? | Empathy vs. accountability; equity vs. equality; individual vs. team needs | Compassionate leadership, boundary-setting, seeking support |
| Your enterprise has excess revenue. Donate to community organizations, reinvest in the garden, distribute to team members, or save for future needs? | Multiple valid priorities; short-term vs. long-term; individual vs. community benefit | Democratic decision-making, financial reasoning, values clarification |
| A community partner asks you to endorse a political candidate who supports urban agriculture. Your enterprise is nonpartisan. | Mission alignment, political engagement, institutional integrity | Analyzing institutional boundaries, navigating pressure, principled refusal |
| A customer offers to pay double for all your organic produce, leaving none for the community distribution program. | Profit vs. mission; individual customer vs. community; financial sustainability vs. equity | Mission-driven decision-making, stakeholder analysis, creative solutions |

## Regulation Within the 5Rs Framework for 9-12

### Root Phase for 9-12 (3-5 minutes)

The Root phase for high school students is brief, student-directed, and normalized as professional practice rather than as a "school activity."

**Root as Professional Practice:**
- Framed as: "Every effective professional—athletes, surgeons, pilots, therapists—has a pre-performance routine. Your Root practice is your professional preparation."
- Options are fully student-selected from a menu developed in collaboration with the educator
- Students who struggle to settle may be dysregulated; the educator checks in privately rather than publicly redirecting

**Root Options:**
1. Silent mindfulness (self-guided or app-guided, 3-5 minutes)
2. Journaling (free-write or prompted)
3. Gentle movement (stretching, walking, tai chi in the garden)
4. Sensory grounding (garden-based: handling soil, smelling herbs, listening to birds)
5. Breathing practice (student-selected technique)
6. Social check-in (brief partner check-in using agreed-upon format)

### Regulate Phase for 9-12 (As Needed)

By high school, students are expected to manage their own regulation needs with minimal adult direction. The educator's role is consultant and resource, not director.

**Regulation Autonomy Expectations:**

| Skill | Expectation | Support Available |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| Self-monitoring | Independently recognize when leaving window of tolerance | PRP as reference; periodic check-in prompts |
| Strategy selection | Choose appropriate strategy based on state and context | Strategy menu posted; peer support; educator consultation |
| Self-implementation | Use strategy independently without disrupting others | Calm Space accessible; garden accessible; self-managed timing |
| Re-entry | Return to activity when ready; self-assess readiness | Re-entry protocol in PRP; brief check-in with educator if needed |
| Reflection | Process the experience to build self-knowledge | Growth journal; PRP update; optional conference with educator |

**Calm Space for 9-12:**
The high school Calm Space is designed as a professional wellness area:
- Private, dignified space with comfortable seating
- Noise-canceling headphones with meditation apps, calming music, or NSDR recordings
- Art supplies for expressive processing
- Weighted blanket
- Cold water access
- Timer (student-managed, no arbitrary limit)
- Crisis resources posted discreetly (crisis hotline, school counselor, community resources)
- Garden access: a designated quiet garden area functions as an outdoor Calm Space

### Restore Phase for 9-12 (As Needed)

Restore at the high school level addresses academic setbacks, professional failures, interpersonal harm, and existential challenges (identity crises, grief, major transitions) with sophistication and compassion.

**Shame Resilience for 9-12:**
- Students learn shame resilience as an explicit life skill, drawing on Brene Brown's research and adapted for adolescents:
  1. Recognize shame physically and emotionally
  2. Practice critical awareness: identify the messages and expectations driving the shame
  3. Reach out to someone who has earned your trust
  4. Speak the shame: naming it reduces its power
- Garden metaphor: "Shame is like root rot—it thrives in darkness and isolation. Bringing it into the light and air is how you heal."

**Academic and Professional Failure Recovery:**
- Failure is framed as essential data, not as identity: "This project failed. You did not fail."
- Post-mortem analysis (borrowed from engineering and business practice):
  1. What happened? (Facts, not blame)
  2. What was the root cause?
  3. What did we learn?
  4. What will we do differently?
  5. How do we take care of ourselves and each other after this?

**Transition-Specific Support:**
For students aging out of foster care, transitioning from juvenile justice, or entering independent living:
- Life skills integration through the garden enterprise (budgeting, cooking, nutrition, professional communication)
- Connection to community resources (housing, employment, healthcare, continuing education)
- Gradual responsibility transfer to build confidence and competence
- Sustained relationship with trusted adults beyond graduation when possible

### Trauma-Responsive Considerations for 9-12

Adolescents who have experienced trauma may present in complex ways at this age:
- Apparent maturity masking unprocessed trauma ("parentified" youth)
- Self-medication through substance use, risky behavior, or workaholism
- Difficulty with trust, especially with authority figures, manifesting as defiance or withdrawal
- Re-traumatization during developmental milestones that mirror earlier adverse experiences
- Ambivalence about success (fear that achievement will separate them from their community)
- Complicated grief related to losses compounded over time
- Hyperindependence as a survival strategy that interferes with help-seeking

**Trauma-Responsive Adaptations:**
- Never assume that a student's challenging behavior is about you. Ask: "What happened to you?" not "What is wrong with you?"
- Maintain consistent, boundaried relationships. Be predictable. Follow through on every commitment.
- Respect autonomy fiercely. Offer choice, explain rationale, and never use power coercively.
- Recognize that trust is built slowly and can be shattered quickly. Repair ruptures immediately and sincerely.
- Coordinate care with mental health providers, case workers, and family (with student consent).
- Address basic needs (food, safety, sleep, health) before expecting academic engagement.
- Create multiple pathways to success. One bad week should not derail a semester.
- Monitor your own secondary traumatic stress. Model self-care. Access supervision and support.
- Build in celebrations of growth, resilience, and contribution. Students need to hear what is right about them.
- The garden itself is therapeutic: physical activity, sensory engagement, contact with nature, nurturing living things, and seeing tangible results of your care over time.
