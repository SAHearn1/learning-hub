# 9-12 Grade Band Part 5: Assessment, Family Engagement & Garden Activities

## Assessment Framework for Grades 9-12

### Assessment Philosophy

Assessment in the 9-12 RootWork curriculum mirrors professional and post-secondary standards. Students are assessed through authentic products—research papers, enterprise financial reports, community presentations, portfolio defenses—that demonstrate both disciplinary knowledge and the capacity to apply that knowledge to real-world challenges. Traditional tests and quizzes are minimized in favor of performance-based assessments that honor the complexity of what students know and can do.

For students with trauma histories, assessment practices must avoid replicating the conditions that cause harm. High-stakes, timed, single-opportunity assessments trigger fight-or-flight responses that measure anxiety more than knowledge. The RootWork assessment framework provides multiple modalities, multiple attempts, extended timelines, and private feedback that supports growth without triggering shame.

### Diagnostic Assessment

| Assessment | Content Area | Format | Data Use |
|-----------|-------------|--------|----------|
| Mathematical Modeling Inventory | Functions, statistics, financial math | Garden-based performance tasks of increasing complexity | Differentiation; identify students needing foundational support |
| Scientific Inquiry Assessment | Experimental design, data analysis, scientific writing | Design and critique a research proposal | Identify gaps in scientific practices; inform lab skill instruction |
| Literacy Profile | Reading comprehension, analytical writing, academic vocabulary | Read and respond to a scientific article; timed writing sample | Reading level; writing targets; vocabulary needs |
| SEL and Transition Readiness | Regulation capacity, relationship skills, life skills, self-advocacy | Self-assessment + educator observation + interview | PRP development; transition planning; support service connections |
| Learning History Interview | Academic identity, past experiences, goals, barriers | Structured one-on-one conversation | Build rapport; understand context; identify needed supports |

### Formative Assessment

Formative assessment is continuous and embedded in all activities through the TRACE protocol.

**TRACE-Embedded Assessment for 9-12:**

| TRACE Step | Assessment Focus | Evidence |
|-----------|-----------------|----------|
| Think | Quality of observation, prior knowledge activation, question generation | Journal entries, discussion contributions, research question quality |
| Reason | Strategy sophistication, hypothesis quality, planning depth, reasoning move application | Written plans, experimental designs, mathematical models |
| Articulate | Communication clarity, precision, audience awareness, academic vocabulary use | Papers, presentations, discussions, mentoring sessions |
| Check | Metacognitive accuracy, verification methods, error detection, intellectual honesty | Self-assessments, peer reviews, error journals, revision quality |
| Extend | Transfer capacity, creative application, question generation, connection-making | Extension projects, problem-posing, cross-disciplinary connections |

**Additional Formative Tools:**

| Tool | Description | Frequency |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| Lab Notebooks | Detailed documentation of all research activities, data, and analysis | Ongoing during research |
| Growth Journals | Reflective writing on learning, regulation, identity, and goals | Daily or as needed |
| Enterprise Reports | Financial updates, project progress, team assessments | Weekly within teams |
| Peer Critique | Structured feedback using professional protocols (Ladder of Feedback, Critical Friends) | Per project draft |
| One-on-One Conferences | Brief, focused conversations about progress, obstacles, and support needs | Bi-weekly (rotating students) |
| Digital Portfolio Updates | Students curate and annotate portfolio artifacts in real time | Ongoing |

### Portfolio Assessment

The 9-12 growth portfolio is a comprehensive, professionally presented collection that serves as both an assessment tool and a transition document.

**Portfolio Structure:**

| Section | Contents | Purpose |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Professional Identity Statement | Who you are as a learner, professional, and community member (2-3 pages) | Self-awareness, voice, narrative identity |
| Academic Best Work | 2-3 exemplary pieces per discipline with reflective annotations | Demonstrate mastery and growth |
| Research Documentation | Complete research project from design to presentation | Demonstrate scientific practice |
| Enterprise Portfolio | Financial reports, business plans, marketing materials, team evaluations | Demonstrate professional skills |
| Growth Evidence | Before/after comparisons, error analyses, revised work | Demonstrate learning process |
| Community Impact | Documentation of community contributions (mentoring, food bank, events) | Demonstrate responsible citizenship |
| Transition Plan | Post-secondary plans, financial plan, support network, goals | Demonstrate future-readiness |
| Reflective Narrative | Essay connecting portfolio artifacts to personal growth story | Demonstrate integration and self-awareness |

**Portfolio Defense (12th Grade Capstone):**

The portfolio defense is a 30-minute formal presentation:

1. **Opening Statement (3 min):** Student presents their professional identity statement
2. **Portfolio Tour (12 min):** Student guides the panel through selected artifacts, explaining significance and growth
3. **Research Highlight (5 min):** Student presents the key finding from their garden research
4. **Transition Plan (5 min):** Student shares post-secondary plans and demonstrates readiness
5. **Panel Questions (5 min):** Panel asks questions; student responds using TRACE reasoning

**Panel Composition:** Educator, community partner, peer (a fellow student selected by the presenter), and one additional member (counselor, employer, family member, or college representative)

**Assessment Rubric:**

| Criterion | Developing (1-2) | Proficient (3) | Distinguished (4) |
|-----------|------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| Self-Awareness | Limited reflection; surface-level self-description | Accurate self-assessment with specific evidence; acknowledges strengths and growth areas | Deep, nuanced self-understanding; integrates multiple aspects of identity; demonstrates wisdom about own patterns |
| Academic Mastery | Partial understanding; relies on scaffolded work | Solid understanding demonstrated through independent work with consistent accuracy | Expert-level work that extends beyond assignment requirements; teaches others; contributes original insight |
| Research Quality | Incomplete design or analysis; limited interpretation | Sound methodology, appropriate analysis, clear interpretation with acknowledged limitations | Sophisticated design, rigorous analysis, nuanced interpretation; publication-quality work |
| Communication | Unclear or disorganized; limited audience awareness | Clear, organized, professional; appropriate for audience | Compelling, polished, and engaging; adapts fluidly to audience responses and questions |
| Community Impact | Limited evidence of contribution | Documented, meaningful contributions to community | Sustained, significant impact with evidence of leadership and initiative |
| Transition Readiness | Vague plans; limited resource awareness | Specific plans with identified steps, resources, and support network | Comprehensive, realistic plans with contingencies; demonstrated initiative (applications submitted, connections made) |

### Grading and Reporting

The RootWork curriculum recommends competency-based grading for high school:

| Level | Descriptor | Transcript Equivalent |
|-------|-----------|----------------------|
| 4 - Distinguished | Exceeds standards; demonstrates deep expertise and original contribution | A |
| 3 - Proficient | Meets all standards consistently through independent work | B |
| 2 - Developing | Approaching standards; demonstrates progress with some scaffolding | C |
| 1 - Beginning | Below standards; significant support needed; foundational gaps present | Incomplete (with support plan) |

Students can reassess to demonstrate improved understanding. The most recent evidence of learning replaces earlier scores. Behavior, attendance, and effort are reported separately and never affect academic grades.

## Family and Community Engagement for Grades 9-12

### Engagement Philosophy

Family engagement in high school respects the adolescent's developing autonomy while maintaining the family connections that support healthy development. For many students in the RootWork program, "family" may include biological relatives, foster families, kinship caregivers, group home staff, mentors, or chosen family. The program meets each student's family configuration with respect and inclusion.

### Communication Structures

| Channel | Frequency | Content |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| Enterprise Newsletter | Monthly | Written by students; reports on enterprise activities, financial updates, research highlights |
| RootGuide Family Portal | Ongoing | Student progress data (with student consent); activity summaries; transition planning resources |
| Student-Led Conferences | Twice yearly | Student presents portfolio and progress to family/supporters |
| Family Garden Events | Quarterly | Open events combining food, garden tours, and community building |
| Transition Planning Meetings | As needed (11th-12th grade) | Student, family, counselor, and educator collaborate on post-secondary planning |
| Emergency/Crisis Communication | As needed | Direct educator-family contact for safety concerns (following school protocols and student consent guidelines) |

### Engaging Families of Older Adolescents

| Strategy | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| Student-as-communicator | Students take primary responsibility for sharing their work and progress with family; educator supplements as needed |
| Family expertise invitation | Invite family members to share professional knowledge with the enterprise (accounting, marketing, agriculture, cooking, business management) |
| Celebration events | Quarterly harvest dinners where students cook garden produce for families; end-of-year enterprise showcase |
| Resource connection | Connect families to community resources (financial aid workshops, food assistance, health services, housing support) through the garden network |
| Cultural integration | Honor family cultural practices around food, agriculture, and community through garden activities and celebrations |
| Transition support | Provide families with information and support for navigating post-secondary transitions (FAFSA, college applications, housing, employment) |

### Supporting Diverse Family Structures

| Family Circumstance | Adaptation |
|--------------------|------------|
| Foster/kinship care with frequent placement changes | Consistent educator relationship regardless of placement; portable portfolio; flexible family event attendance |
| Incarcerated parent | Pen-pal garden journals; photo updates; sensitivity in family-themed activities; connection to visitation support |
| Student as parent | Child-friendly garden events; connection to parenting resources; flexible scheduling; garden produce shared with student's family |
| Student living independently | Educator serves as family-adjacent support; connection to adult mentors; practical life skills emphasis; crisis resources |
| Undocumented family members | Sensitivity about documentation requirements; safe space commitments; connection to legal resources; avoid activities requiring government ID |
| Military family | Acknowledgment of service-related stress; virtual participation options; deployment-aware scheduling |

### Garden Safety for 9-12

Students in grades 9-12 operate with pre-professional independence:

**Certifications:**
- Garden Safety Certification (annual): comprehensive assessment of tool use, chemical safety, first aid, and emergency procedures
- Laboratory Safety Certification: required before independent research involving chemicals, heat, or biological specimens
- Food Safety Certification (recommended): ServSafe or equivalent for students involved in enterprise food sales

**Research Safety:**
- Institutional Review Board (IRB)-informed process for any research involving human subjects (surveys, interviews)
- All experiments reviewed by educator-advisor before implementation
- Risk assessment completed for any novel procedures
- Data privacy protocols for student and community data

**Enterprise Safety:**
- Food handling protocols for all produce intended for sale or distribution
- Financial accountability structures to prevent mismanagement
- Liability awareness for public events
- Insurance and waiver protocols for volunteer activities

## RootGuide AI Tutor: 9-12 Adaptation

### Interaction Design

The RootGuide tutor for grades 9-12 functions as a sophisticated intellectual partner—a Socratic mentor that challenges, supports, and extends thinking without patronizing. It adapts to the student's demonstrated level and interests, offering increasing complexity and autonomy.

**Conversational Features:**
- Uses discipline-specific academic vocabulary with precision
- Challenges weak arguments constructively: "That is a claim. What is your evidence? Could someone argue the opposite?"
- Supports productive struggle without rescuing: "You have been working on this for a while. Before I offer a hint, tell me what you have tried and where you got stuck."
- Offers interdisciplinary connections: "This optimization problem connects to the ecological concept of carrying capacity you studied last month."
- Acknowledges emotional dimension of learning: "Mathematical modeling can feel overwhelming. That is normal. Let us break this into manageable pieces."
- Provides regulation support when cues indicate distress
- Adapts to student's stated goals (college prep, career readiness, personal interest)

**Sample RootGuide Interaction (Grade 11 Statistics):**

```
Student: I ran my t-test and got a p-value of 0.047. Does that mean
         my compost tea works?

RootGuide: Good—you have a p-value. Before interpreting it, let's make
           sure we understand what it means.

           THINK: In your own words, what does a p-value of 0.047 tell
           us? What does it NOT tell us? [TRACE: Think]

Student: It means there's a 4.7% chance the results happened by random
         chance.

RootGuide: Close, but that is a common misinterpretation. The p-value
           is the probability of observing results at least as extreme as
           yours, IF the null hypothesis were true. It is NOT the
           probability that the null hypothesis is true.

           REASON: Given that distinction, what can you actually
           conclude? And what significance level did you set before
           running the test? [TRACE: Reason]

Student: I set alpha at 0.05. So since 0.047 < 0.05, I reject the null.

RootGuide: Correct procedurally. Now the harder question:

           ARTICULATE: Write a results statement that a farmer could
           understand. Include the effect size, not just the p-value.
           A statistically significant difference of 0.2 lbs per plant
           might not be worth the cost of compost tea. [TRACE: Articulate]

Student: "Plants treated with compost tea produced an average of 2.6 lbs
          more tomatoes per plant than untreated plants (M = 12.4 vs. 9.8,
          t(18) = 2.14, p = .047). This difference is statistically
          significant and practically meaningful."

RootGuide: Strong. Two more questions to make this complete:

           CHECK: What are the limitations of your study? What would
           make your evidence stronger? [TRACE: Check]

           EXTEND: If you were advising the school district on whether
           to implement compost tea across all school gardens, what
           additional information would you need? [TRACE: Extend]
```

### Garden Enterprise as Resume Builder

The garden enterprise provides documented, verifiable experience in:
- **Leadership:** Team management, project oversight, strategic planning
- **Financial Management:** Budgeting, accounting, revenue optimization, grant management
- **Research:** Experimental design, data analysis, scientific writing, presentation
- **Marketing:** Branding, customer relations, social media, event planning
- **Community Engagement:** Volunteer coordination, partnership development, public speaking
- **Agriculture:** Organic farming practices, soil science, crop management, food safety

Students maintain a professional resume that is updated quarterly with enterprise accomplishments. Letters of recommendation reference specific enterprise contributions and growth.

For students transitioning to independent living, the garden enterprise teaches practical life skills:

| Time Management | Seasonal planning; meeting deadlines; balancing multiple responsibilities | Balancing work, school, and personal life |

| Self-Care | Regulation practices; stress management; physical activity through garden work | Maintaining physical and mental health; knowing when to seek help |
