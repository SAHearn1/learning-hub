# 9-12 Reflect Phase: Advanced Reasoning and Problem Posing

## Overview

> **Core Question**: "What am I thinking, and why?"

The Reflect phase for grades 9-12 represents the highest level of cognitive engagement in the FG2G curriculum. Students at this level do not merely follow the TRACE protocol -- they direct it. They pose their own problems, design their own investigations, deploy all seven reasoning moves with fluency, and engage with AP/honors-level content through garden-based research that integrates mathematics, science, and language arts. The educator functions as an intellectual partner and research advisor rather than an instructor.

This phase targets the upper reaches of Bloom's taxonomy -- Analyze, Evaluate, and Create -- while maintaining the trauma-informed stance that academic rigor must never compromise emotional safety.

## Student-Directed TRACE Protocol

At the 9-12 level, students internalize the TRACE protocol and apply it autonomously. The educator monitors and coaches rather than leads.

### TRACE at the 9-12 Level

| TRACE Step | K-2 Version | 9-12 Advanced Version | Student Self-Talk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Think** | "What do you see?" | Student independently observes, identifies variables, activates prior knowledge, and frames the problem | "What are the key elements here? What do I already know that connects?" |
| **Reason** | "What could we try?" | Student selects and justifies a strategy from multiple possibilities, considering efficiency and elegance | "Which approach is most efficient? What are the trade-offs between methods?" |
| **Articulate** | "Tell me what you did." | Student constructs a formal argument or explanation using discipline-specific language and evidence | "Can I explain this clearly enough that someone else could replicate my reasoning?" |
| **Check** | "Did it work?" | Student verifies through alternative methods, identifies limitations, and considers edge cases | "Does this hold in all cases? What assumptions am I making? How could I break this?" |
| **Extend** | "What else?" | Student generalizes to new contexts, poses follow-up questions, connects to other domains, and proposes original problems | "What if I changed this constraint? How does this apply to the garden project? What new question does this raise?" |

## All Seven Reasoning Moves in Advanced Application

Students in grades 9-12 deploy reasoning moves with discipline-specific sophistication.

### DECOMPOSE: Breaking Down Complex Systems

**Mathematics**: Factor a polynomial, decompose a complex function into transformations, partition a proof into lemmas
**Science**: Break an ecosystem into trophic levels, decompose a chemical reaction into half-reactions, parse a research paper into claims and evidence
**Language Arts**: Deconstruct an author's argument into premises and conclusions, analyze rhetorical strategies in a speech
**Garden Application**: Decompose the variables affecting crop yield (soil pH, sunlight hours, water volume, nutrient levels) into testable factors

### COMPARE: Identifying Patterns Across Domains

**Mathematics**: Compare linear and exponential growth models using garden data; compare statistical methods for analyzing harvest yields
**Science**: Compare aerobic and anaerobic decomposition in compost systems; compare photosynthesis and cellular respiration as mirror processes
**Language Arts**: Compare persuasive techniques across historical and contemporary texts about food justice
**Garden Application**: Compare growth rates of heritage vs. hybrid tomato varieties under controlled conditions

### QUESTION: Challenging Assumptions and Probing Deeper

**Mathematics**: "Does this formula work for negative values? What happens at the boundary?" Question the assumptions behind a statistical model
**Science**: "What variables are we not controlling? What would a skeptic say about our methodology?" Challenge experimental design
**Language Arts**: "Whose perspective is missing from this text? What are the author's unstated assumptions?"
**Garden Application**: "Our yield data shows a decline in October. Is this seasonal, or is something else happening? What would we need to measure to find out?"

### VERIFY: Rigorous Self-Checking

**Mathematics**: Prove a result by two independent methods; use dimensional analysis to verify units; test a formula with known values
**Science**: Replicate an experiment; cross-reference findings with published data; conduct a peer review of a lab partner's conclusions
**Language Arts**: Verify claims in a source by tracing citations; check logical consistency of an argument
**Garden Application**: Verify soil test results by sending samples to a university extension lab; cross-check garden data with regional agricultural databases

### GENERALIZE: From Specific to Universal

**Mathematics**: Derive a general formula from a pattern observed in garden measurements; extend a proof from specific cases to the general case
**Science**: Generalize findings from a garden experiment to broader ecological principles; formulate a hypothesis from repeated observations
**Language Arts**: Identify universal themes in garden narratives that connect to the broader human experience
**Garden Application**: "If our compost system works at this scale, what principles would transfer to a community-scale operation?"

### TRANSFORM: Reframing and Representing

**Mathematics**: Transform a word problem into an equation, a table, a graph, and a verbal description; convert between coordinate systems
**Science**: Transform raw data into visualizations; reframe a biology problem as a chemistry problem; represent a process as a flow diagram
**Language Arts**: Transform a research finding into a policy proposal, a personal narrative, a data visualization, and an oral presentation
**Garden Application**: Transform garden harvest data into a business plan; reframe a pest problem as an ecological balance problem

### CONNECT: Cross-Curricular and Real-World Integration

**Mathematics**: Connect statistical analysis to social justice (food desert mapping); connect geometry to garden design; connect financial mathematics to garden enterprise
**Science**: Connect cellular biology to plant health observations; connect climate science to seasonal planting decisions; connect nutrition science to harvest planning
**Language Arts**: Connect garden narratives to environmental justice literature; connect data analysis writing to scientific reporting standards
**Garden Application**: Connect every academic concept to a tangible garden context -- the garden is the living laboratory where all disciplines converge

## Problem Posing: Student-Created Problems

A hallmark of the 9-12 Reflect phase is **Problem Posing** -- students do not just solve problems; they create them.

### Problem Posing Levels

| Level | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Level 1: Variation** | Change numbers or context of an existing problem | "What if the garden bed were 12 ft instead of 8 ft? How would the soil volume change?" |
| **Level 2: Extension** | Add complexity or constraints to an existing problem | "If we also need to account for walkway space and irrigation access, how does the garden layout optimization change?" |
| **Level 3: Inversion** | Reverse the problem structure (given the answer, find the question) | "Our garden produced 47 kg of tomatoes. What combinations of planting density and plot size could have yielded this?" |
| **Level 4: Original** | Create an entirely new problem inspired by garden observations | "I noticed that our pollinator garden attracts different species at different times of day. Can I model this as a probability distribution and predict peak pollination windows?" |

### Problem Posing Protocol
1. **Observe**: Spend time in the garden noticing phenomena, patterns, or puzzles
2. **Wonder**: Generate "What if...?" and "I wonder why...?" questions
3. **Formalize**: Translate the wonder into a mathematically or scientifically precise question
4. **Scope**: Determine whether the problem is solvable with available data and methods
5. **Solve**: Apply TRACE protocol to the self-created problem
6. **Share**: Present the problem and solution to peers for critique and extension

## Garden-Based Research Projects

Students in grades 9-12 conduct semester-long or year-long research projects using the garden as a primary data source.

### Sample Research Project Structures

| Project Type | Description | Disciplines Integrated | Standards Alignment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Experimental Design** | Design and conduct a controlled experiment (e.g., effect of companion planting on pest reduction) | Biology, Statistics, Technical Writing | NGSS HS-LS2-6, AP Biology, CC RST.9-10.3 |
| **Data Analysis** | Collect and analyze longitudinal garden data (e.g., yield trends across seasons, correlation between weather and growth) | Statistics, Environmental Science, Mathematics | AP Statistics, NGSS HS-ESS3-4, CC S-ID |
| **Engineering Design** | Design and build a garden system (e.g., automated irrigation, vertical growing structure, composting system) | Engineering, Physics, Mathematics | NGSS HS-ETS1-2, HS-ETS1-3 |
| **Policy Research** | Investigate food access in the local community and propose garden-based solutions | Social Studies, Language Arts, Data Science | CC W.11-12.1, GSE SS Economics |
| **Business Plan** | Develop a garden enterprise business plan (see Reconnect phase) | Financial Literacy, Marketing, Mathematics | CC MP.4, GSE Economics/Business |

### Research Project TRACE Application

Each research project follows an extended TRACE cycle:

- **Think**: Literature review and observation phase (2-3 weeks)
- **Reason**: Hypothesis formation and experimental design (1-2 weeks)
- **Articulate**: Data collection and analysis with ongoing documentation (4-8 weeks)
- **Check**: Peer review, replication, and limitation analysis (2-3 weeks)
- **Extend**: Presentation, publication, and next-question generation (1-2 weeks)

## AP/Honors-Level Content Integration

The garden provides authentic contexts for advanced academic content.

### AP Biology Connections
- Cellular respiration and photosynthesis observed in real time through plant growth and gas exchange experiments
- Ecology and population dynamics through garden ecosystem monitoring
- Genetics through heritage seed preservation and hybridization observations
- Evolution through pest resistance and adaptive responses in garden organisms

### AP Environmental Science Connections
- Soil science and biogeochemical cycles through composting and soil testing
- Water resources through irrigation design and rainwater harvesting
- Biodiversity through pollinator gardens and companion planting
- Sustainability through garden-to-table food systems analysis

### AP Statistics Connections
- Experimental design using garden plots as treatment groups
- Data collection, visualization, and inference from real agricultural data
- Correlation and regression analysis of growth factors
- Sampling methods and confidence intervals using harvest data

### AP Language and Composition Connections
- Argumentative writing on food justice, environmental policy, and agricultural ethics
- Rhetorical analysis of food industry marketing vs. local food movement literature
- Research-based synthesis essays using garden project data and published sources

## Educator Language Models

### Facilitating Student-Directed TRACE
- "You've identified the problem. What's your TRACE plan? Walk me through it."
- "Before you solve, what reasoning moves do you anticipate needing?"
- "You've arrived at an answer. Now, before I respond -- how confident are you, and what would change your mind?"

### Coaching Reasoning Moves
- "That's a strong DECOMPOSE. Now, can you GENERALIZE? Does this pattern hold beyond this specific case?"
- "I notice you went straight to solving. What would happen if you QUESTIONED your assumptions first?"
- "You've VERIFIED your answer one way. Can you TRANSFORM the problem into a different representation to check again?"

### Responding to Correct and Incorrect Answers
- Correct: "Your answer is right, but I'm more interested in your reasoning. Can you ARTICULATE why this approach works?"
- Incorrect: "There's something interesting happening in your reasoning. Let's DECOMPOSE your approach step by step." (Transition to Restore if needed)
- Partially correct: "You're on a productive path. Where does this approach work well, and where does it start to break down?"

## Data Analysis and Statistics in the Garden

### Garden Data Literacy Sequence

| Skill | Garden Context | Statistical Concept |
|---|---|---|
| Data collection | Record daily temperature, rainfall, soil moisture, and plant height | Variables, measurement, data types |
| Data visualization | Create growth charts, yield comparisons, and seasonal trend graphs | Histograms, scatter plots, box plots, time series |
| Descriptive statistics | Calculate mean yield per plot, standard deviation of germination rates | Central tendency, spread, distribution shape |
| Inferential statistics | Determine if organic fertilizer significantly increases yield | Hypothesis testing, p-values, confidence intervals |
| Regression analysis | Model relationship between sunlight hours and tomato production | Linear regression, correlation, R-squared |
| Experimental design | Design a randomized block experiment comparing soil amendments | Randomization, blocking, replication, controls |

## Assessment Indicators

### Proficiency Indicators

- **Emerging**: Student follows TRACE with prompting; uses 2-3 reasoning moves; solves presented problems but does not pose original ones
- **Developing**: Student initiates TRACE independently; uses 4-5 reasoning moves; begins to pose variation and extension problems
- **Proficient**: Student directs TRACE fluently; deploys all seven reasoning moves appropriately; poses original problems; conducts garden research with guidance
- **Distinguished**: Student adapts TRACE to novel contexts; combines multiple reasoning moves in sophisticated chains; poses publishable-quality research questions; mentors peers in reasoning development

### Portfolio Evidence
- TRACE protocol self-documentation for 3+ major problems (quarterly)
- Reasoning move log with self-assessment of frequency and quality (ongoing)
- At least one student-posed problem with full solution and reflection (per semester)
- Research project documentation including proposal, data, analysis, and presentation (annual)
- Peer review contributions (ongoing)

## Monitoring During Reflect

Even during advanced cognitive engagement, the educator monitors for dysregulation:

- **Error cascades**: Sudden decline in reasoning quality after a period of strong performance
- **Perfectionism paralysis**: Student cannot begin because they fear making a mistake
- **Avoidance through performance**: Student gives quick, shallow answers to finish faster rather than engaging deeply
- **Intellectual shutdown**: "I don't care" or "This doesn't matter" as a defense against challenge
- **Comparison-driven distress**: Visible anxiety when peers solve faster or arrive at different answers

If dysregulation is detected, the session transitions to Regulate without academic penalty: "This is hard work and it takes real cognitive energy. Let's pause and regroup."

## Transition to Next Phase

- **To Restore**: When an error reveals a productive learning opportunity for deeper analysis
- **To Regulate**: When dysregulation signals are detected (self-rating drops below 40 or educator observes distress)
- **To Reconnect**: When the learning objective is met and the student is ready to apply, integrate, and reflect on growth
