# 9-12 Grade Band Part 3: Science & Literacy Integration

## Overview

Science and literacy instruction in the 9-12 RootWork curriculum functions at a pre-professional level. Students are not merely learning about science—they are doing science, engaging in authentic research that contributes genuine knowledge to the fields of sustainable agriculture, urban ecology, food systems, and environmental science. Literacy skills are developed through the rigorous reading, writing, and communication demands of scientific practice: reading peer-reviewed literature, writing research papers, presenting at symposiums, and advocating for evidence-based policy.

The garden enterprise provides an unparalleled science laboratory. Unlike contrived lab experiments with predetermined outcomes, garden-based research involves genuine uncertainty, uncontrolled variables, seasonal constraints, and the complex interactions of living systems. Students learn to tolerate ambiguity, design pragmatic experiments within real-world constraints, and draw defensible conclusions from imperfect data—skills that define professional scientific practice.

For students with trauma histories, science offers a particular therapeutic benefit: the natural world follows predictable patterns even when human relationships feel unpredictable. Learning to observe, predict, and understand natural systems can rebuild the sense of coherence and agency that trauma erodes.

## Unit 1: Environmental Science — Soil Ecology and Biogeochemistry (Weeks 1-10)

### Essential Questions
- How do soil biological communities support plant growth and ecosystem health?
- What chemical cycles operate within our garden soil, and how can we measure and optimize them?
- How do human agricultural practices affect soil health over time?
- What does sustainable soil management look like, and how does it differ from conventional approaches?

### Learning Objectives
- Analyze soil as a complex ecosystem containing biological, chemical, and physical components
- Design and conduct investigations into soil chemistry, biology, and structure
- Apply knowledge of nutrient cycles (nitrogen, carbon, phosphorus) to garden management decisions
- Evaluate the environmental impact of agricultural practices using empirical evidence
- Communicate scientific findings through technical writing and public-facing formats

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Weeks 1-3: Soil as an Ecosystem

**Lesson: Soil Biodiversity Census**

*Setup:* Students collect soil samples from multiple locations in the garden (composted beds, newly established beds, perennial borders, undisturbed soil) and conduct a comprehensive biodiversity survey.

*Activities:*
1. **Macrofauna identification:** Use Berlese funnels to extract soil organisms. Identify and count earthworms, insects, mites, springtails, and other invertebrates. Calculate diversity indices (Shannon-Wiener, Simpson's).
2. **Microbial activity assessment:** Measure CO2 respiration rates using sealed chambers with CO2 sensors or limewater traps. Higher respiration = more active microbial community.
3. **Fungal network observation:** Use microscopy to examine soil for mycorrhizal fungi. Research the role of fungal networks in nutrient transfer between plants.

*TRACE Application:*
- **Think:** "What would you predict about biodiversity differences between composted and non-composted soil? Why?"
- **Reason:** "Design a sampling protocol that allows valid comparison. How many samples? From what depth? How will you control for variables like moisture and temperature?"
- **Articulate:** "Write a methods section that another research team could replicate exactly."
- **Check:** "Calculate your diversity indices. Do they confirm or contradict your hypothesis? What are the limitations of your sampling method?"
- **Extend:** "How does soil biodiversity connect to above-ground biodiversity? Research the concept of the soil food web and create a visual model."

*Standards Alignment:*

| Standard | Application |
|----------|-------------|
| NGSS HS-LS2-1 | Use mathematical representations to support claims about ecosystem interactions |
| NGSS HS-LS2-2 | Use mathematical representations to support explanations of factors affecting biodiversity |
| NGSS HS-LS2-6 | Evaluate claims about the role of group behavior in organisms' survival |
| NGSS HS-ESS3-3 | Create a computational simulation to illustrate relationships among Earth's systems |
| GSE Biology | SB4: Ecology and ecosystem dynamics |

#### Weeks 4-6: Nutrient Cycles in the Garden

**Lesson: The Nitrogen Cycle — From Cover Crop to Crop**

*Context:* Students investigate the nitrogen cycle in the garden by tracking nitrogen through cover crop decomposition, soil biological processes, and plant uptake.

*Investigation Sequence:*

1. **Baseline soil testing:** Measure soil nitrate (NO3-) and ammonium (NH4+) levels using commercial test kits or colorimetric methods
2. **Cover crop experiment:** Compare nitrogen levels in plots where nitrogen-fixing cover crops (crimson clover, winter peas) were grown versus plots with non-fixing cover crops (rye, oats)
3. **Decomposition tracking:** Monitor nitrogen release from incorporated cover crops over 8 weeks
4. **Plant tissue testing:** Analyze leaf nitrogen content in crops growing in different plots using Kjeldahl or combustion methods (or simplified chlorophyll meter readings as proxy)
5. **Budget construction:** Create a nitrogen budget for the garden, accounting for inputs (fixation, fertilizer, atmospheric deposition) and outputs (crop harvest removal, leaching, volatilization)

*Mathematical Integration:*
- Calculate nitrogen fixation rates in kg/hectare and convert to garden-appropriate units
- Model nitrogen mineralization rates as a function of temperature and moisture using exponential decay: N_available(t) = N_total * (1 - e^(-kt))
- Create mass balance equations for the nitrogen budget
- Statistical comparison of soil nitrogen levels between treatments

**Lesson: Carbon Cycling and Climate Connection**

*Core Investigation:* "How much carbon does our garden sequester compared to a conventional lawn or a paved lot?"

*Research Activities:*
1. Estimate above-ground biomass carbon through harvest weighing and dry-weight conversion (multiply by 0.45 for approximate carbon content)
2. Estimate soil organic carbon using loss-on-ignition method: weigh soil, heat to 550C, reweigh; mass loss approximates organic matter content
3. Compare carbon storage in garden soil versus soil from lawn, forest, and disturbed areas
4. Calculate the carbon footprint of garden operations (inputs, transportation, tool use) versus carbon sequestration
5. Research the role of agriculture in climate change and the potential of regenerative agriculture as a climate solution

*Reasoning Moves:*
- **Decompose:** Break the carbon cycle into components: photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, soil storage, harvesting
- **Compare:** Compare carbon dynamics across different land uses and management systems
- **Generalize:** "If our garden sequesters X kg of carbon per year, what would happen if every school in our district had a similar garden?"
- **Connect:** Link garden-level carbon dynamics to global climate systems

#### Weeks 7-10: Applied Ecology — Integrated Pest Management and Pollination Biology

**Lesson: IPM Decision-Making**

*Setup:* Students develop and implement an Integrated Pest Management (IPM) plan for the garden, applying ecological principles to real pest challenges.

*IPM Hierarchy:*

| Level | Strategy | Garden Application | Science Concepts |
|-------|----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Prevention | Cultural practices that reduce pest pressure | Crop rotation, companion planting, resistant varieties, sanitation | Plant-herbivore coevolution; allelopathy; resistance mechanisms |
| Monitoring | Regular scouting and identification | Weekly pest surveys, trap crops, pheromone traps | Population dynamics; life cycles; identification keys |
| Threshold | Determine when intervention is justified | Action thresholds based on pest density and crop value | Economic injury levels; cost-benefit analysis; risk assessment |
| Biological Control | Use natural enemies of pests | Release ladybugs for aphid control; encourage parasitoid wasps; maintain habitat for predatory insects | Predator-prey dynamics; trophic cascades; biological control ecology |
| Mechanical/Physical | Physical removal or barriers | Hand-picking, row covers, traps, barriers | Physical ecology; microclimate modification |
| Chemical (last resort) | Least-toxic approved substances | Neem oil, insecticidal soap, Bt (organic-approved only) | Toxicology; environmental fate; mode of action |

*Student Research Projects (each team takes one):*
- "Does companion planting with basil reduce aphid populations on tomatoes?"
- "What is the economic threshold for flea beetle damage on brassicas?"
- "How effective are flowering border strips at attracting beneficial insects?"
- "Does trap cropping with nasturtiums reduce pest pressure on main crops?"

**Lesson: Pollination Ecology**

*Investigation:* Students conduct a semester-long pollination study documenting pollinator diversity, visitation rates, and the relationship between pollinator activity and fruit set.

*Data Collection Protocol:*
- Weekly 15-minute pollinator observation sessions at standardized stations
- Identify pollinators to functional group (honey bees, native bees, butterflies, flies, beetles)
- Count flower visits per minute per plant species
- Measure fruit set percentage for pollinator-dependent crops (squash, tomatoes, peppers)
- Correlate pollinator visitation rates with fruit set using regression analysis

*Literacy Integration:*
- Read and annotate primary research articles on pollinator decline
- Write a literature review connecting local observations to global pollinator research
- Create a policy brief advocating for pollinator-friendly practices in the school district

## Unit 2: Literacy — Reading, Writing, and Communication as Professional Practice (Integrated Throughout)

### Reading: Engaging with Primary Literature

Students in 9-12 read authentic scientific and policy texts, developing the skills to critically evaluate evidence and arguments.

**Required Reading Types:**

| Text Type | Examples | Skills Developed |
|-----------|----------|-----------------|
| Peer-reviewed journal articles | Studies on cover crop nitrogen fixation, pollinator decline, soil carbon sequestration | Scientific literacy, critical evaluation, understanding methodology |
| Policy documents | USDA organic standards, local food safety regulations, school wellness policies | Policy analysis, civic literacy, understanding regulatory frameworks |
| Investigative journalism | Food system exposés, environmental justice reporting | Media literacy, evaluating sources, understanding bias |
| Historical primary sources | Slave narratives about agriculture, Dust Bowl accounts, victory garden propaganda | Historical analysis, connecting past to present, understanding systemic inequity |
| Technical manuals | Soil testing procedures, greenhouse management guides, IPM handbooks | Technical reading, following procedures, applying information |

**Active Reading Protocol (TRACE-Aligned):**
1. **Think (Preview):** Scan title, abstract, headings, and figures. What is this about? What do you already know?
2. **Reason (Annotate):** Read actively. Highlight claims. Underline evidence. Question methods. Note connections to your own research.
3. **Articulate (Summarize):** Write a one-paragraph summary. What is the main finding? Why does it matter?
4. **Check (Evaluate):** Assess the quality of the evidence. Are the methods sound? Are conclusions supported? What are the limitations?
5. **Extend (Apply):** How does this connect to our garden? What implications does it have for our practice?

### Writing: Scientific and Professional Communication

Students produce writing across multiple genres, developing voice, precision, and audience awareness.

**Writing Portfolio Requirements (Annual):**

| Genre | Product | Assessment Criteria |
|-------|---------|---------------------|
| Research Paper | Full scientific paper from garden experiment (see Part 2 for details) | Scientific conventions, evidence quality, analytical depth |
| Literature Review | Synthesis of 8-10 sources on a food systems topic | Source quality, synthesis (not just summary), critical perspective |
| Policy Brief | 2-page brief recommending a policy change with evidence | Clarity, persuasiveness, appropriate audience, actionable recommendations |
| Grant Proposal | Proposal for garden enterprise funding | Problem statement, objectives, methods, budget, evaluation plan |
| Reflective Essay | Personal narrative connecting garden experience to identity, growth, or values | Voice, insight, vulnerability, craft |
| Technical Documentation | Procedure manual for garden operations (for future students) | Clarity, completeness, usability, accuracy |

### Speaking and Presentation

**Formal Presentation Opportunities:**

| Event | Audience | Format | Skills |

### Cross-Disciplinary Literacy: Social Studies Integration

The garden provides a lens for examining social studies content:

**Food Systems and Social Justice:**
- **History:** Agricultural labor history (slavery, sharecropping, migrant labor, food sovereignty movements)
- **Economics:** Food supply chains, farm economics, food pricing and access, agricultural subsidies
- **Government:** USDA regulations, food policy, school lunch programs, SNAP/WIC
- **Geography:** Agricultural regions, food miles, climate zones, food deserts/apartheid

**Research Projects:**
- "Food deserts in our city: Mapping access and proposing solutions"
- "The hidden cost of cheap food: Analyzing externalities in the industrial food system"
- "From field to fork: Tracing the supply chain of a common grocery item"
- "Land access and food sovereignty: Historical and contemporary perspectives"

*TRACE Application for Social Studies:*
- **Think:** "What assumptions do you bring to this topic? What personal experiences shape your perspective?"
- **Reason:** "What evidence supports different positions on this issue? Who benefits from the current system?"
- **Articulate:** "Present your analysis clearly, acknowledging the complexity and multiple perspectives."
- **Check:** "Have you considered counterarguments? Have you examined your own biases?"
- **Extend:** "What action can you take based on what you have learned? How does this connect to our garden enterprise?"

## Garden Safety for 9-12

Students in grades 9-12 operate with substantial independence. Safety protocols reflect pre-professional expectations:

**Laboratory Safety:**
- All chemistry-related activities (soil testing, water analysis, microbiology) follow standard lab safety protocols
- Students complete a safety certification before conducting independent research
- Material Safety Data Sheets (MSDS) reviewed before handling any chemicals or amendments
- Proper protective equipment (goggles, gloves, lab coats) required for all laboratory work

**Tool and Equipment Safety:**
- Students are certified on all hand tools, power tools (with supervision), and irrigation systems
- Heavy equipment (tillers, mowers) operated only by students 16+ with specific training and adult supervision
- Tool maintenance is a shared responsibility; damaged tools reported immediately

**Field Research Safety:**
- Buddy system required for off-campus research or data collection
- Weather protocols followed for all outdoor activities
- First aid kits accessible; at least one CPR/First Aid certified person present
- Communication devices available during off-site activities

**Biological Safety:**
- Proper hygiene when handling soil, compost, and biological specimens
- Awareness of zoonotic risks (roundworm from contaminated soil, Toxoplasma from cat feces in soil)
- Allergen awareness and emergency plans for insect stings
- Handwashing required before eating and after all garden/lab work
