# 3-5 Grade Band: Overview & Developmental Context

## Introduction

The 3-5 grade band represents a pivotal transition in the RootWork curriculum, serving children ages eight through eleven during a period of accelerating cognitive sophistication, deepening social awareness, and emerging independence. Students in grades three through five are moving from the concrete, sensory-driven learning of early childhood into a more analytical and self-directed phase. They can hold multiple ideas simultaneously, reason about cause and effect with increasing nuance, and begin to see themselves as members of communities that extend beyond their immediate classroom. For students who have experienced adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), this developmental window presents both heightened vulnerability -- as growing metacognition can bring painful self-awareness about their own histories -- and extraordinary opportunity, as the capacity for intentional self-regulation and meaning-making deepens significantly.

The RootWork 3-5 curriculum builds on the sensory-rich foundation established in K-2 by introducing more complex academic content, the full TRACE protocol, explicit self-regulation instruction, and authentic community-connected projects -- all grounded in the living laboratory of the school garden. At this level, students are no longer simply observing and counting in the garden; they are designing experiments, analyzing data, writing persuasive arguments about environmental issues, calculating area and fractions, and leading community projects. The garden shifts from being primarily a sensory regulation space to becoming an authentic context for rigorous academic inquiry while retaining its essential role as a place of safety, belonging, and healing.

## Developmental Profile: Ages 8-11

### Cognitive Development

Children in the 3-5 band are solidly within Piaget's concrete operational stage, with emerging formal operational capacities by late fifth grade. Key characteristics include:

- **Logical reasoning with concrete materials**: Students can classify, seriate, and conserve -- understanding, for example, that the amount of soil remains the same whether it is spread across a large bed or piled in a container. Garden activities leverage this capacity for systematic thinking about real-world phenomena.
- **Growing metacognition**: Students become increasingly aware of their own thinking processes. They can reflect on how they solved a problem, identify what confused them, and begin to select strategies intentionally. This capacity is the developmental foundation for the full TRACE protocol introduction.
- **Multi-step problem solving**: Working memory expands to hold 4-5 pieces of information simultaneously. Students can follow multi-step procedures, plan ahead, and coordinate multiple variables in garden experiments and math problems.
- **Emerging abstract thinking**: By fourth and fifth grade, students begin to reason about concepts that are not immediately visible -- understanding that plants convert sunlight into energy through photosynthesis, that unseen microorganisms decompose organic matter, and that mathematical relationships can be expressed with variables.
- **Reading to learn**: The shift from learning to read to reading to learn occurs during this band. Students can now access scientific texts, garden reference guides, and seed catalogs independently, transforming reading from a subject into a tool for garden-based inquiry.
- **Developing executive function**: Planning, organizing, self-monitoring, and time management skills are actively developing. Garden projects provide authentic, meaningful contexts for practicing these skills with real consequences (a poorly planned garden bed yields less than a well-designed one).
- **Interest in fairness and rules**: Students at this age are deeply invested in fairness, rules, and logical consistency. They notice when things are inequitable and can articulate why. This sensitivity is leveraged through garden economics, fair distribution of harvest, and collaborative decision-making.

### Social-Emotional Development

- **Peer orientation increasing**: Friendships become central to identity. Students compare themselves to peers and are acutely sensitive to social standing. The garden provides cooperative, non-competitive contexts where social bonds form through shared work rather than performance-based hierarchies.
- **Developing internal regulation**: Students are transitioning from co-regulation (needing an adult's calm presence) to self-regulation (managing their own states independently). The Regulate phase at this level explicitly teaches and practices self-regulation strategies while maintaining adult support as a safety net.
- **Emerging moral reasoning**: Students move beyond "rules exist because adults made them" toward understanding the purpose behind rules. Garden agreements become student-negotiated and student-enforced, building genuine ethical reasoning.
- **Vulnerability to shame and comparison**: The growing capacity for social comparison makes students in this band particularly vulnerable to academic shame. The Restore phase must explicitly address the relationship between mistakes, learning, and growth through garden metaphors that normalize struggle.
- **Identity formation**: Students are actively constructing narratives about who they are -- as learners, as community members, as capable (or incapable) people. The garden provides a concrete, visible domain where every child can develop competence and contribute meaningfully.
- **Increased emotional vocabulary**: Students can name and differentiate nuanced emotional states (frustrated vs. angry, disappointed vs. sad, nervous vs. scared) and are ready for more sophisticated emotion regulation instruction.

### Physical Development

- **Improved fine motor control**: Students can use standard garden tools, write detailed observations, create precise diagrams, and manipulate small seeds with accuracy.
- **Increased stamina and coordination**: Extended garden work sessions (30-45 minutes) are appropriate. Students can dig, build, carry heavy materials, and perform sustained physical tasks.
- **Growth spurts and body awareness**: Some students, particularly fifth graders, may be entering puberty. The garden provides a context for discussing growth, change, and the fact that all organisms develop at different rates -- normalizing the physical diversity in the classroom.
- **Need for movement remains high**: Despite increased capacity for sustained seated work, students in this band still require regular movement. Garden-based learning naturally integrates movement throughout the instructional block.

## 5Rs Framework Adaptation for 3-5

### Root Phase (5-7 minutes)

The Root phase in 3-5 shifts toward student-selected check-in strategies and increased student ownership of the opening ritual:

**Entry Rituals:**
- Student-selected check-in from a menu of options: journal prompt, garden observation walk, partner check-in, or individual grounding exercise
- "Root Question" posted daily on the board -- a thought-provoking garden-connected question that activates prior knowledge and primes thinking for the session ("If you could redesign our garden to attract the most pollinators, what would you change first?")
- Garden weather and growth report delivered by rotating student "Garden Reporters" who observe and report on garden conditions
- Emotion check-in using a regulation thermometer (0-10 scale) or color-coded wristband system (green = regulated, yellow = managing, red = need support)

**Regulation Assessment:**
- Students self-assess on a 1-5 regulation scale and record in their garden journals
- Teacher scans for discrepancies between reported state and observed behavior (a student who reports "5" but is bouncing, unfocused, or withdrawn)
- Quick individual check-ins for students on behavior plans, IEPs, or with known trauma triggers
- Preview of the session's learning objectives and connection to ongoing garden projects

**Garden Connection:**
- Brief observation of current garden state -- what has changed since the last session?
- Student-led discussion of garden needs: "What does our garden need from us today?"
- Connecting the day's academic content to a garden phenomenon: "Today we're exploring fractions, and our garden will help us think about equal parts"

### Regulate Phase (3-8 minutes, as needed)

For 3-5 students, the Regulate phase introduces explicit self-regulation instruction alongside responsive regulation support. Students are expected to develop a personal regulation toolkit:

**Tier 1 Strategies (whole class, proactive):**
- **Box breathing** (inhale 4 counts, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4) with visual timer on the board
- **Progressive muscle relaxation**: "Squeeze your hands like you're gripping a trowel... hold... release. Feel the difference between tension and relaxation"
- **Garden grounding exercise**: "Name 5 things you can see in the garden, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste" (5-4-3-2-1 sensory grounding)
- **Movement breaks**: Garden-themed stretching, "shake it out" breaks, brief walks around the garden perimeter
- **Mindful transition activities**: Sorting seeds, sketching a plant, or reading a garden observation quietly

**Tier 2 Strategies (small group or individual, responsive):**
- **Regulation partnerships**: Trained peer partners who can offer co-regulation ("I notice you seem frustrated. Want to take a walk to the garden together?")
- **Self-regulation menu cards**: Personal cards listing 3-5 strategies the student has identified as effective for them (created collaboratively with the teacher)
- **Garden-based regulation stations**: Designated areas where students can go independently -- the herb sensory station, the soil manipulation area, or the quiet observation bench
- **Journaling for regulation**: Students write or draw about what they are feeling and what they need; teacher checks and responds in writing

**Tier 3 Strategies (intensive individual support):**
- Private check-in with educator, school counselor, or designated support staff
- Modified participation plan: "Today you can work in the calm corner and join us when you're ready"
- Predetermined signal system between student and teacher (a card on the desk, a specific hand signal) to communicate need for support without public attention
- Crisis protocol activation if dysregulation is severe or sustained
- Documentation for IEP team, behavioral support team, or trauma-response team review

### Reflect Phase (20-30 minutes)

The Reflect phase in 3-5 introduces the full TRACE protocol as an explicit thinking framework that students learn to apply independently:

**Think** ("What do I notice? What do I already know?"):
- Students observe a garden phenomenon, data set, or problem and record initial noticings
- Activate prior knowledge through quick-writes, partner discussions, or KWL charts
- Make connections to previous learning: "How does this connect to what we learned about ecosystems last week?"
- TRACE journal entry: "I notice... I already know... I think this is about..."

**Reason** ("What is my plan? What strategies can I use?"):
- Students develop a plan or hypothesis before acting
- Select from a toolkit of strategies: draw a model, make a table, work backward, use manipulatives, estimate first
- Consider multiple approaches: "What are two different ways we could figure this out?"
- Apply the seven reasoning moves explicitly (see Reasoning Moves section below)
- TRACE journal entry: "My plan is... I chose this strategy because..."

**Articulate** ("How do I explain my thinking clearly?"):
- Students communicate their reasoning through writing, speaking, and visual models
- Partner and small-group discussions with structured protocols (think-pair-share, gallery walks, jigsaw)
- Mathematical or scientific explanations using discipline-specific vocabulary and evidence
- TRACE journal entry: "I can explain my thinking by... The evidence that supports my conclusion is..."

**Check** ("Does my answer make sense? How can I verify?"):
- Students verify their work using a different strategy or method
- Compare results with partners and reconcile differences
- Check for reasonableness: "Does this answer make sense in the context of our garden?"
- Identify and analyze errors: "Where did my reasoning break down?"
- TRACE journal entry: "I checked my work by... I found that... This makes sense because..."

**Extend** ("Where else does this apply? What new questions do I have?"):
- Students apply learning to new contexts or generate new questions
- Design follow-up investigations or experiments
- Connect to real-world applications beyond the garden
- TRACE journal entry: "This connects to... I now wonder... I could test this by..."

### Restore Phase (5-10 minutes, explicit instruction)

In the 3-5 band, the Restore phase becomes an explicit instructional component rather than an embedded response to errors:

**Growth Mindset Deepening:**
- "Your brain is like a garden -- it grows stronger when you work through challenges, not when everything is easy"
- Explicit instruction on neuroplasticity: "When you struggle with a problem, your brain is literally forming new connections. That struggle IS the growing"
- Error analysis as a celebrated practice: "Let's look at this mistake together. Understanding why it didn't work teaches us more than getting it right the first time"
- Class norms: "In our garden, we analyze errors. We don't avoid them -- we learn from them"

**Structured Error Analysis:**
- Students examine their own errors using a "Root Cause Analysis" protocol (borrowing the garden metaphor):
  1. **What happened?** (Describe the error without judgment)
  2. **Where did the roots of the error begin?** (Trace back to the point of misunderstanding)
  3. **What can I grow from this?** (Identify the learning that comes from understanding the error)
  4. **What will I do differently next time?** (Plan for future growth)
- Error analysis partners: Students pair up to examine each other's work respectfully and constructively

**Resilience Narratives:**
- Share stories of scientists, farmers, and innovators who failed repeatedly before succeeding
- Connect to garden experiences: "Remember when our first tomato planting failed because we didn't harden off the seedlings? What did we learn? What did we do differently?"
- Students maintain a "Growth Log" documenting challenges they overcame and what they learned from setbacks

### Reconnect Phase (5-10 minutes)

The Reconnect phase for 3-5 emphasizes community contribution, real-world application, and identity as a learner and contributor:

**Community Connection Projects:**
- Students identify a community need that garden learning can address (food insecurity at the school, a bare lot in the neighborhood, a local food bank needing donations)
- Plan and execute community-connected projects using academic skills: calculate quantities needed, write persuasive letters, design informational materials
- Reflect on the impact of their contributions: "How did our garden help our community this week?"

**Real-World Application:**
- "Where will you use this skill outside of school? Outside of the garden?"
- Connect garden economics to family grocery shopping, meal planning, and budgeting
- Identify garden science in the wider world: weather patterns, environmental issues, food systems

**Identity and Reflection:**
- Weekly reflection prompts: "This week I grew as a [mathematician/scientist/writer/community member] by..."
- Garden portfolio updates: students select work that demonstrates growth and annotate it with reflections
- Sharing learning with younger students (K-2 garden mentoring partnerships)

**Closing Ritual:**
- Garden maintenance task (watering, weeding, recording observations) that gives closure and responsibility
- "Seed of thought" -- one idea to carry into the rest of the day or week
- Preview of next session's learning and garden work
- Appreciation circle: students acknowledge a peer's contribution to the learning community

## Seven Reasoning Moves: 3-5 Implementation

The seven reasoning moves are introduced explicitly in grades 3-5 and practiced across all content areas:

| Reasoning Move | Definition | 3-5 Student Language | Garden Application Example |
|---------------|-----------|---------------------|---------------------------|
| **Decompose** | Breaking a complex problem into manageable parts | "I can break this into smaller pieces" | "To find the total area of our L-shaped garden bed, I'll break it into two rectangles and add the areas together" |
| **Compare** | Identifying similarities and differences between concepts | "How are these alike? How are they different?" | "Compost and fertilizer both feed plants, but compost is made from organic matter while fertilizer is manufactured" |
| **Question** | Generating productive questions that advance thinking | "I wonder... What if... Why does...?" | "Why do the tomato plants on the south side of the garden grow taller than the ones on the north side?" |
| **Verify** | Checking work through alternative methods or evidence | "Let me check this another way" | "I calculated we need 48 plants. Let me verify by drawing the grid and counting the spaces" |
| **Generalize** | Identifying patterns that apply beyond the specific case | "This always happens when... This works because..." | "Any time we increase one dimension of a rectangular bed, the area increases proportionally -- this is true for all rectangles, not just our garden bed" |
| **Transform** | Representing a problem or concept in a different form | "I can show this a different way" | "Instead of describing the food web in words, I'll create a diagram with arrows showing energy flow" |
| **Connect** | Linking current learning to prior knowledge or other domains | "This reminds me of... This is related to..." | "The way water moves through soil layers is similar to how we learned water moves through a filter in our water quality experiment" |

### Reasoning Moves Instruction Sequence

**Weeks 1-4:** Introduce Decompose and Compare through garden measurement activities
**Weeks 5-8:** Introduce Question and Verify through garden science investigations
**Weeks 9-12:** Introduce Generalize and Transform through data analysis projects
**Weeks 13-16:** Introduce Connect through cross-curricular garden projects
**Weeks 17+:** Students select and apply reasoning moves independently, identifying which move they are using and why

## Classroom Environment Design

### Physical Space

The 3-5 learning environment should include:

- **Outdoor garden access**: Raised beds at standard height (24-36 inches), in-ground beds, a greenhouse or cold frame if possible, and dedicated experimental plot space for student-designed investigations
- **Indoor garden lab**: Space with grow lights, microscopes, measuring tools, and a designated area for seed starting, soil testing, and specimen examination
- **Flexible seating areas**: Collaboration tables for group work, individual study spaces, and a garden reading nook with reference materials
- **Regulation station**: A private, non-stigmatized space with regulation tools, self-monitoring charts, and journaling supplies (upgraded from the K-2 "calm corner" to reflect age-appropriate language)
- **Data wall**: A class data display showing ongoing garden metrics -- growth measurements, weather data, harvest records, and experiment results -- updated by students
- **TRACE protocol display**: Visual anchor chart showing the five TRACE steps with student-friendly language and the seven reasoning moves with icons
- **Word wall**: Organized by discipline (math vocabulary, science vocabulary, literary terms) with garden-connected definitions and student-created examples
- **Community connection board**: Display of ongoing community projects, letters received, partner organization information, and impact documentation

## Trauma-Informed Considerations for Middle Childhood

### Understanding Trauma in Ages 8-11

Students in the 3-5 band may manifest trauma differently than younger children due to their increasing cognitive sophistication and social awareness:

- **Academic avoidance**: Students may refuse to attempt challenging work, not from defiance but from fear of failure and exposure. Growing metacognition means they are increasingly aware of what they do not know, which can trigger shame.
- **Perfectionism and control**: Some trauma-affected students develop rigid perfectionism as a coping mechanism -- if they can control their performance perfectly, they feel safer. These students may become distressed by errors, avoid risk-taking, and resist the Restore phase.
- **Social withdrawal or aggression**: Peer relationships become more complex, and students with insecure attachment histories may struggle with trust, rejection sensitivity, and conflict resolution. They may oscillate between clinging and pushing away.
- **Somatic complaints**: Stomachaches, headaches, and fatigue remain common. Some students may experience panic symptoms (racing heart, difficulty breathing) when triggered.
- **Emotional flooding**: Growing emotional vocabulary does not automatically mean growing emotional regulation. Students may be able to name their anger or sadness but still become overwhelmed by its intensity.
- **Dissociation**: Some students with complex trauma histories may "zone out" or appear disconnected during instruction, particularly during stressful moments. This is a protective response, not willful inattention.
- **Hypervigilance**: Students may remain watchful, monitoring the teacher's mood, classroom dynamics, and environmental sounds. They may be the first to notice a change in routine or a visitor at the door.

### Curriculum Response

The 3-5 RootWork curriculum addresses these presentations through:

1. **Predictability with growing flexibility**: The 5Rs structure remains consistent, but within that structure, students are given increasing choices about how they engage. This combination of safety and agency is essential for trauma recovery.
2. **Explicit regulation instruction**: Rather than relying solely on environmental regulation (calm corner, sensory tools), students are taught the neuroscience of regulation in age-appropriate language: "When your brain senses danger, it activates the survival part -- that's fight, flight, or freeze. Garden breathing helps activate the thinking part of your brain so you can choose your response."
3. **Shame reduction through error normalization**: The Restore phase explicitly teaches that errors are learning data, not personal failures. Garden metaphors provide safe distance: "A gardener who has never had a plant die has never taken a risk."
4. **Relational repair**: When relationships between students (or between a student and teacher) are strained, the curriculum provides structured repair protocols based on garden metaphors: "Sometimes plants get damaged by storms. But with care, they can heal and grow back even stronger."
5. **Narrative coherence**: Students develop their personal learning narratives through portfolios, growth logs, and reflection journals, building the coherent self-story that trauma often fragments.
6. **Community belonging**: The shift toward community projects in grades 3-5 gives students with trauma histories the experience of contributing to something larger than themselves -- a powerful antidote to the helplessness and isolation that trauma can produce.

## Sample Weekly Schedule: 3-5

### Standard Week (60-75 minute integrated block)

| Day | Root (5-7 min) | Regulate (as needed) | Reflect (25-30 min) | Restore (5-8 min) | Reconnect (5-10 min) |
|-----|----------------|---------------------|--------------------|--------------------|----------------------|
| **Monday** | Root question + regulation self-assessment + garden report | Box breathing or grounding as needed | Math: Garden measurement, area/perimeter calculation, or data analysis using TRACE protocol | Error analysis of practice problems; growth mindset discussion | Connect to weekly community project; preview the week |
| **Tuesday** | Journal quick-write + partner check-in | Progressive muscle relaxation | Science: Garden investigation or experiment with hypothesis, data collection, and analysis | Discuss experimental challenges and what they revealed | Real-world connection discussion; update data wall |
| **Wednesday** | Garden observation walk + emotion check-in | Movement break or garden grounding | Literacy: Reading scientific/garden text, research writing, or persuasive writing with TRACE protocol | Peer revision using constructive feedback protocols | Share writing with a partner; cross-curricular connections |
| **Thursday** | Student-selected check-in from menu | Regulation partnership check-in | Math/Science Integration: Multi-step problems, data representation, or engineering design challenge | Root Cause Analysis of challenges encountered | Plan community contribution for Friday |
| **Friday** | Garden planning meeting (student-led) | Garden-based regulation (outdoor) | Integrated Garden Work: Planting, maintaining, harvesting, experimenting + embedded academic discourse | Celebrate productive struggles from the week | Community connection activity; garden journal reflection; closing circle |

*Note: The Regulate phase is responsive rather than scheduled -- it occurs when needed based on student regulation assessments. The Restore phase is both scheduled (explicit instruction) and responsive (addressing errors as they arise).*

## Transition from K-2 to 3-5

### Key Shifts

Students entering the 3-5 band from the K-2 curriculum will experience these intentional transitions:

| K-2 Practice | 3-5 Evolution | Rationale |
|-------------|---------------|-----------|
| Teacher-led emotion check-in | Student-selected check-in from a menu | Builds agency and self-awareness |
| Simplified TRACE elements | Full TRACE protocol with explicit instruction | Leverages growing metacognition |
| Co-regulation with adult | Self-regulation with adult safety net | Develops independence while maintaining support |
| Restore embedded in instruction | Restore as explicit instructional phase | Builds metacognitive capacity for error analysis |

### Transition Support for Students with Trauma

Students with trauma histories may find the transition from the highly structured, adult-led K-2 framework to the more student-directed 3-5 approach challenging. Support strategies include:

- Gradual release: Introduce new expectations one at a time over the first six weeks
- Maintain K-2 supports alongside new expectations until the student demonstrates readiness
- Explicit instruction in new self-regulation strategies before withdrawing adult-led regulation support
- Regular check-ins about the transition: "What feels different this year? What feels the same? What do you need from me?"
- Collaborate with the student's previous teacher to ensure continuity of support strategies
- Review and update IEP, 504, or behavior support plans to reflect new developmental expectations
