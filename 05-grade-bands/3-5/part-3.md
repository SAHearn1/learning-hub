# 3-5 Grade Band: Science & Cross-Curricular Integration

## Science Curriculum Through the Garden

### Overview

Science in the 3-5 RootWork curriculum moves from the observation-centered approach of K-2 into the realm of systematic investigation, experimental design, and evidence-based explanation. Students at this level are cognitively ready to formulate testable hypotheses, identify and control variables, collect quantitative data, and construct explanations grounded in evidence rather than intuition. The garden provides an unparalleled context for this work because it presents genuine scientific questions -- Why do the plants on the south side grow taller? What happens to decomposing matter over time? How does soil composition affect plant health? -- that students can investigate with real consequences and real discoveries.

The 3-5 science curriculum aligns with the Georgia Standards of Excellence and the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS), emphasizing three dimensions of science learning: disciplinary core ideas (the content), science and engineering practices (the methods), and crosscutting concepts (the big ideas that span disciplines). The garden naturally integrates all three dimensions in every investigation.

For students with trauma histories, science investigation can be both liberating and anxiety-provoking. The open-ended nature of scientific inquiry -- where you genuinely do not know the answer before you investigate -- can trigger anxiety in students who need predictability and control. The RootWork approach addresses this by providing clear investigative structures (the TRACE protocol maps directly onto the scientific method) while celebrating the discovery of unexpected results as the most exciting part of science. "The experiment didn't fail -- it taught us something we didn't expect."

### Science and Engineering Practices Integration

| NGSS Practice | Garden Implementation | TRACE Connection |
|--------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| Asking questions and defining problems | Students generate questions from garden observations; define engineering problems (How do we keep deer out? How do we water during breaks?) | Think: "What do I notice? What questions arise?" |
| Developing and using models | Draw diagrams of plant systems, food webs, water cycles; build scale models of garden designs | Transform: "Show it a different way" |
| Planning and carrying out investigations | Design controlled experiments in the garden with independent, dependent, and controlled variables | Reason: "What is my plan?" |
| Analyzing and interpreting data | Collect, graph, and analyze growth data, weather data, soil test results | Check: "Does my data support my hypothesis?" |
| Using mathematics and computational thinking | Calculate growth rates, measure precisely, graph trends, compute averages | Connect: "How does math help me understand this?" |
| Constructing explanations and designing solutions | Write evidence-based explanations for garden phenomena; design solutions to garden engineering problems | Articulate: "How do I explain my thinking?" |
| Engaging in argument from evidence | Debate garden management decisions using data; defend conclusions in peer review | Articulate + Check |
| Obtaining, evaluating, and communicating information | Research garden topics using multiple sources; present findings to audiences | Extend: "Where else does this apply?" |

## Unit 1: Plant Biology -- Form, Function, and Life Processes (Weeks 1-10)

### Essential Questions
- How do plants obtain the energy and materials they need to grow?
- How do the structures of plants help them survive in different environments?
- Why do offspring look similar to but not identical to their parents?

### Key Vocabulary
Photosynthesis, chlorophyll, glucose, carbon dioxide, oxygen, transpiration, xylem, phloem, stomata, adaptation, trait, inherited trait, acquired trait, variation, germination, pollination, fertilization, seed dispersal, annual, perennial, biennial

### Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Photosynthesis Investigation (4th-5th Grade)

**Root Phase:** "Every living thing needs energy. Where do YOU get your energy? Where do plants get theirs? Today we're going to investigate the most important chemical reaction on Earth -- one that happens right here in our garden every single day."

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **The Photosynthesis Puzzle:**
   - Begin with the driving question: "Plants get bigger over time. Where does the new mass come from?"
   - Students brainstorm hypotheses: "From the soil? From water? From air? From sunlight?"
   - Introduce the historical experiment of Jan Baptist van Helmont (1648): He grew a willow tree in a pot, carefully measuring the soil. After 5 years, the tree gained 164 pounds, but the soil lost only 2 ounces.
   - TRACE Protocol:
     - **Think**: "What do you notice about van Helmont's data? The tree gained a lot of weight, but the soil barely changed."
     - **Reason**: "If the mass didn't come from the soil, where did it come from? What else went into the pot?" (water, air, sunlight)
     - **Articulate**: "Explain your hypothesis to your investigation partner."
     - **Check**: "Let's design an experiment to test our ideas."
     - **Extend**: "How can we investigate whether light, water, or air is most responsible for plant growth?"

2. **Light Deprivation Experiment:**
   - Set up: Place identical plants in four conditions:
     - Full sunlight + water (control)
     - No sunlight + water (covered with box)
     - Full sunlight + no water
     - Partial sunlight + water (shaded area of garden)
   - Students predict outcomes, record in investigation journals
   - Measure and photograph weekly for 4 weeks
   - Collect data: height, leaf color, leaf count, overall health rating (1-5)
   - Analysis: "What can we conclude about what plants need? Which factor seemed most critical?"

3. **Leaf as a Factory:**
   - Examine leaves with hand lenses and simple microscopes
   - Observe stomata (visible on leaf impressions made with clear nail polish)
   - "These tiny holes let carbon dioxide in and oxygen out. The leaf is like a factory that takes in CO2 and water, uses sunlight energy, and produces glucose (sugar) and oxygen."
   - Create a labeled diagram of photosynthesis: 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy --> C6H12O6 + 6O2
   - Garden connection: "Every time you see a green leaf in our garden, it's making food and producing the oxygen we breathe."
   - Reasoning Move -- **Transform**: "How can you represent this process in a different way? Draw it, act it out, or create a flowchart."

4. **Transpiration Demonstration:**
   - Place a clear plastic bag over a leafy branch in the garden. Seal with a twist tie.
   - Check after several hours: water droplets collect inside the bag
   - "Where did this water come from? The plant absorbed it through its roots, moved it up through the stem, and released it through the stomata in the leaves."
   - Connect to the water cycle: "Plants are part of the water cycle! They move water from the soil back into the air."
   - Reasoning Move -- **Connect**: "How does transpiration connect to what we learned about the water cycle in our weather unit?"

#### Lesson: Plant Adaptations (3rd-5th Grade)

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Adaptation Scavenger Hunt:**
   - Students examine plants in the garden and identify structural adaptations:
     - Thorns on roses (defense against herbivores)
     - Waxy leaves on succulents (water conservation)
     - Deep taproots on dandelions (accessing deep water)
     - Bright flowers on zinnias (attracting pollinators)
     - Tendrils on pea plants (climbing toward light)
   - Record findings in observation journals with detailed drawings and explanations of function
   - TRACE: "Think -- What do you notice about this structure? Reason -- Why might the plant have this? Articulate -- Explain how this adaptation helps the plant survive."

2. **Adaptation Design Challenge (Engineering):**
   - "Design a plant that could survive in [extreme environment: desert, arctic, swamp, cliff face]."
   - Students must include at least three adaptations and explain the function of each
   - Present designs in a gallery walk; peers provide feedback using sentence frames:
     - "I think your adaptation for ___ would work because ___"
     - "I wonder whether ___ would be effective because ___"
     - "Have you considered ___?"

3. **Comparative Plant Anatomy:**
   - Dissect and compare plants from different environments:
     - Cactus vs. fern (water conservation vs. water dependence)
     - Grass vs. oak tree (different growth strategies)
     - Garden lettuce vs. wild ancestor (selective breeding discussion)
   - Create a comparison chart documenting structural differences and their environmental explanations
   - Reasoning Move -- **Compare**: "What is similar about how these plants solve the problem of getting water? What is different?"

## Unit 2: Ecosystems and Food Webs in the Garden (Weeks 11-18)

### Essential Questions
- What is an ecosystem and how do organisms within it depend on each other?
- How does energy flow through a garden ecosystem?
- What happens when part of an ecosystem is disrupted?

### Key Vocabulary
Ecosystem, community, population, organism, producer, consumer, decomposer, herbivore, carnivore, omnivore, food chain, food web, energy transfer, predator, prey, symbiosis, mutualism, competition, niche, habitat

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Mapping the Garden Food Web

**Root Phase:** "Our garden isn't just plants. It's a whole community of living things that depend on each other. Today we become ecosystem scientists." Students do a brief garden walk to center themselves and observe garden life.

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Garden Census (Data Collection):**
   - Students conduct a systematic biodiversity survey of the school garden
   - Survey protocol: 30-minute observation in assigned quadrants
   - Record all organisms observed: plants, insects, arachnids, birds, earthworms, fungi, other
   - Use field guides and identification apps to identify species
   - Compile class data into a master species list

   | Quadrant | Producers | Primary Consumers | Secondary Consumers | Decomposers |
   |----------|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|-------------|
   | NW (herb bed) | Basil, rosemary, mint, grass | Aphids, caterpillars, grasshoppers | Ladybugs, spiders, praying mantis | Earthworms, fungi, pill bugs |
   | NE (tomato bed) | Tomatoes, marigolds, weeds | Tomato hornworms, whiteflies | Paper wasps, garden spiders | Millipedes, soil bacteria |
   | SE (root vegetables) | Carrots, beets, radishes | Root maggots, flea beetles | Ground beetles, centipedes | Earthworms, nematodes |
   | SW (flower garden) | Sunflowers, zinnias, coneflowers | Bees, butterflies, ants | Birds (wrens, cardinals), dragonflies | Decomposing mulch organisms |

2. **Building the Food Web:**
   - Using yarn and species cards, students physically construct a food web
   - Each student holds a card representing one organism from the garden census
   - Connect yarn between organisms that have feeding relationships
   - "Who eats whom? How does energy flow from the sun through our garden?"
   - Remove one organism and observe the cascading effects: "What happens to the food web if we remove all the ladybugs?" (Aphid population explodes, plants suffer, other consumers lose prey)
   - Reasoning Move -- **Question**: "What questions does this food web raise? What would happen if we introduced a new organism? Removed a predator?"

3. **Energy Flow Diagram:**
   - Students create energy pyramid diagrams for the garden ecosystem
   - Discuss the 10% rule: only about 10% of energy transfers from one trophic level to the next
   - "Why are there more plants than herbivores, and more herbivores than carnivores, in our garden?"
   - Calculate: "If the plants in our garden capture 10,000 units of energy from the sun, how much energy is available to the herbivores? To the carnivores?" (1,000 and 100, respectively)
   - Reasoning Move -- **Generalize**: "Is this pattern (more producers than consumers) true in all ecosystems, not just our garden?"

4. **Ecosystem Disruption Simulation:**
   - Simulate three disruption scenarios and analyze the effects:
     - **Scenario A**: A new pest is introduced that eats all the tomato plants
     - **Scenario B**: The school applies pesticide that kills all insects
     - **Scenario C**: A drought reduces water availability by 75%
   - Students work in groups to trace the effects through the food web using their diagrams
   - Write an analysis paragraph using TRACE: "Based on my evidence, the most damaging disruption would be ___ because ___"

## Unit 3: Soil Science and Decomposition (Weeks 19-24)

### Essential Questions
- What is soil made of and why does it matter for plant growth?
- How does decomposition recycle matter in ecosystems?
- How can we improve our garden soil through scientific methods?

### Key Vocabulary
Soil horizon, topsoil, subsoil, bedrock, humus, clay, silt, sand, loam, pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, organic matter, microorganism, decomposition, composting, aerobic, anaerobic, nutrient cycle

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Soil Composition Investigation

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Soil Texture Analysis:**
   - Collect soil samples from 4-6 different locations (garden bed, playground, woods, roadside, potting mix, sandy area)
   - Perform the "jar test": mix each sample with water in a clear jar, shake vigorously, and let settle for 24-48 hours
   - Observe layers: sand (bottom, largest particles), silt (middle), clay (top, finest particles), organic matter (floating)
   - Calculate percentages of each component by measuring layer heights
   - Use a soil texture triangle to classify each sample (sandy loam, clay loam, silty clay, etc.)
   - TRACE: "Think -- what do you notice about the different layers? Reason -- why do the particles settle in this order? Articulate -- explain the relationship between particle size and settling rate."

2. **Soil Chemistry Testing:**
   - Use commercial soil test kits to measure pH, nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) in garden soil
   - Record results and compare to optimal ranges for common garden plants:
     - Tomatoes: pH 6.0-6.8, moderate N, high P, high K
     - Lettuce: pH 6.0-7.0, high N, moderate P, moderate K
     - Blueberries: pH 4.5-5.5, low N, moderate P, low K
   - "Based on our soil test results, which crops will grow best in our soil? What amendments might we need?"
   - Reasoning Move -- **Compare**: "Compare our garden soil to the optimal ranges. Where are we strong? Where do we need improvement?"

3. **The Living Soil Investigation:**
   - Extract organisms from a soil sample using a Berlese funnel (light and heat drive organisms downward through a screen into a collection jar)
   - Identify and count organisms under magnifying glasses: mites, springtails, nematodes, earthworms, beetle larvae, fungal threads
   - "One tablespoon of healthy soil contains more organisms than there are people on Earth. Our garden soil is alive!"
   - Discuss the role of soil organisms: decomposition, nutrient cycling, soil aeration, water infiltration
   - Reasoning Move -- **Connect**: "How do these soil organisms connect to the food web we studied? They're the decomposer layer that recycles matter back to the producers."

#### Lesson: Building and Monitoring a Compost System

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Composting Science:**
   - Build a multi-bin compost system with the class
   - Teach the science of aerobic decomposition: microorganisms break down organic matter in the presence of oxygen, producing heat, CO2, water, and nutrient-rich compost
   - "What conditions do decomposing organisms need?" (oxygen, moisture, carbon-rich materials, nitrogen-rich materials)
   - Set up a monitoring protocol:
     - Temperature (using a compost thermometer): target 130-160 degrees F for active decomposition
     - Moisture (squeeze test: should feel like a wrung-out sponge)
     - Carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (aim for approximately 30:1 by mixing brown and green materials)
   - Students record data weekly and graph temperature over time

2. **Decomposition Rate Experiment:**
   - Bury different materials in labeled mesh bags at the same depth in the compost:
     - Apple slice, bread, newspaper, cardboard, plastic bag, aluminum foil, leaf, cotton fabric
   - Predict which will decompose fastest and slowest; record predictions
   - Unearth bags at 2-week intervals and observe changes
   - Create a decomposition rate chart ranking materials from fastest to slowest
   - "What do the materials that decompose quickly have in common? What about those that don't decompose?"
   - Reasoning Move -- **Generalize**: "Can you create a rule about what kinds of materials decompose and which don't?"

3. **Nutrient Cycling Model:**
   - Students create a physical model of the nutrient cycle in the garden:
     - Soil nutrients are absorbed by plant roots
     - Plants use nutrients to grow
     - Animals eat plants
     - Animals and plants die and fall to the ground
     - Decomposers break down dead matter
     - Nutrients return to the soil
     - The cycle repeats
   - "Matter is never created or destroyed -- it cycles through the ecosystem. Our compost bin is speeding up this natural process."
   - Reasoning Move -- **Transform**: "How can you represent this cycle visually? As a diagram? A physical model? A dramatic performance?"

## Unit 4: Weather, Climate, and Garden Planning (Weeks 25-28)

### Essential Questions
- How do weather patterns affect our garden?
- What is the difference between weather and climate?
- How can we use weather data to make better gardening decisions?

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Long-Term Weather Data Analysis

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Weather Station Data Review:**
   - Analyze the class weather station data collected throughout the year
   - Create multi-variable graphs showing temperature, rainfall, and plant growth on the same timeline
   - Identify correlations: "During which weeks did plants grow most? What were the weather conditions during those weeks?"
   - Calculate averages: mean temperature per month, total monthly rainfall
   - TRACE: "Think -- what patterns do you see in the data? Reason -- what might explain the relationship between rainfall and growth rate? Articulate -- write a data-supported explanation."

2. **Climate Zones and Garden Planning:**
   - Introduce USDA Plant Hardiness Zones using the interactive zone map
   - "Our school is in Zone 7b/8a. What does that tell us about what we can grow?"
   - Research frost dates: average last spring frost and first fall frost
   - Calculate the growing season length for our area
   - Plan a planting calendar based on climate data: "When should we start tomato seeds indoors? When is it safe to transplant outdoors?"
   - Reasoning Move -- **Connect**: "How does our local climate zone connect to the weather data we've collected all year?"

3. **Climate Change and Gardens:**
   - Compare historical climate data for our region (temperature and rainfall averages from 50 years ago vs. today)
   - Discuss observable changes: "Have you noticed any changes in weather patterns? Have gardeners in our community noticed changes?"
   - "How might shifting climate patterns affect what we can grow in our garden?"
   - Write a research-based response using evidence from multiple sources
   - Engineering connection: "What can gardeners do to adapt to changing conditions?" (season extenders, drought-resistant varieties, water conservation systems)

## Unit 5: Engineering Design Challenges (Weeks 29-34)

### Essential Questions
- How can we use engineering to solve real garden problems?
- What does the design process look like in practice?
- How do we evaluate and improve our designs?

### Engineering Design Process

1. **Define the Problem**: What garden challenge are we solving? What are the constraints?
2. **Research**: What solutions already exist? What do we know about the science involved?
3. **Brainstorm**: Generate multiple possible solutions without judgment
4. **Plan**: Select the most promising design and create detailed plans with measurements
5. **Build**: Construct a prototype using available materials
6. **Test**: Evaluate the prototype against the original problem criteria
7. **Improve**: Revise the design based on testing results
8. **Communicate**: Present the solution, process, and results

### Design Challenge 1: Irrigation System Design

**Problem:** "Our garden needs water, but we can't be here every day during summer break. Design a system that will water our garden automatically or with minimal human intervention."

**Constraints:**
- Budget: $50 maximum for materials
- Must water at least 4 garden beds
- Must function for at least 3 days without human intervention
- Cannot waste water (runoff should be minimal)

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Research Phase:**
   - Study existing irrigation methods: drip irrigation, soaker hoses, olla pots, wicking beds, rain barrel gravity systems
   - Calculate water needs: "Each bed needs approximately 1 inch of water per week. Each bed is 4 ft x 8 ft = 32 sq ft. One inch of water on 32 sq ft = 32 x 144 cubic inches / 231 = about 20 gallons per bed per week."
   - Research materials and costs; create a budget spreadsheet

2. **Design and Build Phase:**
   - Teams of 3-4 students design their irrigation system
   - Create scaled drawings with measurements
   - Build prototypes (start with small-scale models using recycled materials)
   - Test with actual water flow and measure distribution uniformity

3. **Testing and Evaluation:**
   - Each team tests their design over one week
   - Measure: water distribution evenness (place collection cups at multiple points), total water used, ease of setup, cost
   - Data table comparing all team designs

4. **TRACE Protocol for Engineering:**
   - **Think**: "What do I notice about how water flows through my system?"
   - **Reason**: "Why is the water pressure lower at the end of the line? What could I change?"
   - **Articulate**: "Explain your design decisions and what the test results showed."
   - **Check**: "Did your system meet all the constraints? Where did it fall short?"
   - **Extend**: "How would you modify your design for a larger garden? A garden on a hillside?"

### Design Challenge 2: Raised Bed Construction

**Problem:** "Design and build a raised garden bed that maximizes growing space, is accessible to students in wheelchairs, and will last at least 5 years."

**Constraints:**
- Must be accessible (wheelchair height: 24-30 inches)
- Must contain at least 16 cubic feet of soil
- Must use materials that won't leach chemicals into the soil
- Must include drainage

**Activities:**
- Research safe materials (untreated cedar, recycled plastic lumber, galvanized steel)
- Calculate dimensions using volume formula (V = l x w x h)
- Create scale drawings with precise measurements
- Build a model at 1:4 scale before constructing full-size
- Present design rationale to school administration for approval

### Design Challenge 3: Season Extension Structures

**Problem:** "Design a structure that will allow us to grow cool-weather crops at least 3 weeks past the first fall frost."

**Constraints:**
- Must cover at least one 4 x 8 garden bed
- Must allow light penetration
- Must withstand winds up to 30 mph
- Must be easy to open for ventilation on warm days
- Budget: $75 maximum

**Activities:**
- Research cold frames, hoop houses, and row covers
- Test insulating properties of different materials (plastic sheeting, greenhouse film, old windows, fabric)
- Measure temperature inside and outside the structure over several days
- Calculate temperature differential and relate to frost protection
- Reasoning Move -- **Verify**: "Our structure kept the inside 8 degrees warmer. Is that enough to protect against a 25-degree frost if it's 30 degrees outside?"

### Writing Through the Garden

#### Research Reports (3rd-5th Grade)

**Research Report Sequence (2-3 weeks):**

1. **Topic Selection:** Students choose a garden-connected research question:
   - "What companion plants grow best with tomatoes and why?"
   - "How do earthworms improve soil health?"
   - "What is the history of the Three Sisters planting method?"
   - "How does composting reduce landfill waste?"

2. **Source Evaluation:**
   - Students gather 3-5 sources (books, websites, interviews)
   - Evaluate each source using the CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose)
   - Create a source evaluation chart
   - 5th grade: Introduce primary vs. secondary sources

3. **Note-Taking and Organization:**
   - Use graphic organizers to categorize information by subtopic
   - Practice paraphrasing (not copying) from sources
   - Create an outline with introduction, body paragraphs organized by subtopic, and conclusion

4. **Drafting and Revision:**
   - Draft following the outline
   - Peer revision using "Stars and Wishes" protocol: two specific strengths and one specific suggestion
   - Teacher conference for targeted feedback
   - Revise for content, organization, and conventions

5. **Publication and Presentation:**
   - Final draft with proper formatting (title page, headings, bibliography)
   - Present findings to the class or at a garden open house
   - Add to the class garden reference library

#### Persuasive Writing (4th-5th Grade)

**Prompt Examples:**
- "Write a letter to the principal arguing that our school should expand the garden program."
- "Should our garden use organic methods only, or is it acceptable to use synthetic pesticides in some situations?"
- "Write an op-ed for the school newspaper about food waste in the cafeteria and how composting could help."

**Persuasive Writing Structure:**
1. Claim: State your position clearly
2. Reasons: Provide at least three reasons supported by evidence
3. Evidence: Include data from garden experiments, research from credible sources, and examples from personal garden experience
4. Counterargument: Acknowledge the opposing view and respond to it
5. Call to action: What do you want the reader to do?

**TRACE Protocol for Persuasive Writing:**
- **Think**: "What do I already know about this issue? What is my initial position?"
- **Reason**: "What evidence supports my position? What evidence challenges it?"
- **Articulate**: "How can I present my argument clearly and convincingly?"
- **Check**: "Have I addressed the counterargument? Is my evidence strong enough?"
- **Extend**: "Who else needs to hear this argument? How can I take action beyond writing?"

## Social Studies Connections

### Agriculture History and Food Systems

#### Lesson: The History of Agriculture

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Timeline of Agriculture:**
   - Students research and create a collaborative timeline:
     - 10,000 BCE: Beginning of agriculture in the Fertile Crescent
     - 7,000 BCE: Rice cultivation in China
     - 5,000 BCE: Maize domestication in Mesoamerica
     - 3,000 BCE: Three Sisters planting (corn, beans, squash) by Indigenous peoples of North America
     - 1700s: Agricultural revolution in Europe (crop rotation, selective breeding)
     - 1800s: Mechanization (cotton gin, reaper, tractor)
     - 1900s: Green Revolution (synthetic fertilizers, hybrid seeds, pesticides)
     - 2000s: Organic movement, urban farming, sustainable agriculture
   - "How has farming changed over time? What stayed the same?"

2. **Cultural Gardening Traditions:**
   - Research and present on gardening traditions from diverse cultures:
     - **Indigenous Three Sisters**: Corn provides structure for beans to climb; beans fix nitrogen in soil; squash leaves shade the ground to retain moisture. "Each plant helps the others -- like a healthy community."
     - **Japanese Kitchen Gardens (satoyama)**: Integration of forest, field, and garden; harmony between human needs and ecosystem health
     - **West African Compound Gardens**: Multi-story cropping with trees, shrubs, and ground crops; year-round food production
     - **Medieval European Monastery Gardens**: Medicinal herbs, food production, contemplation spaces
     - **Victory Gardens (WWII)**: Citizens growing food as a patriotic duty; community self-reliance
   - Connect to SEL: "What values does each gardening tradition reflect? How do they show respect for the land and community?"
   - Reasoning Move -- **Compare**: "What similarities do you notice across cultures in how people approach growing food?"

3. **Food Systems Analysis:**
   - Trace the journey of a tomato from seed to plate:
     - Commercial path: seed company --> farm --> harvesting (often picked green) --> transportation --> distribution center --> grocery store --> home
     - Garden path: save seeds --> plant --> grow --> harvest (ripe) --> eat or share
   - Calculate food miles: "How far does a winter tomato travel to reach our grocery store? How far does a garden tomato travel?"
   - Discuss implications: environmental impact, nutrition, freshness, cost, labor practices
   - "Why does this matter? What choices can we make?"

### Economics Through the Garden

**Activities:**
- Calculate the cost of growing vegetables vs. buying them at the grocery store
- Research supply and demand: "Why are strawberries expensive in December and cheap in June?"
- Explore careers in agriculture: farmer, agricultural scientist, extension agent, food safety inspector, landscape designer, environmental engineer
- Practice budgeting with the garden business plan (cross-reference with Math Unit 5)

### Geography and Environmental Science

**Activities:**
- Map global food production: "Where are most of the world's bananas grown? Coffee? Rice? Why?"
- Study climate zones and their relationship to agriculture
- Investigate food deserts in urban areas: "What happens when communities don't have access to fresh produce? How can gardens help?"
- Calculate the school garden's ecological footprint vs. commercially produced food

### Art Integration: Botanical Illustration

**Detailed Lesson:**

1. **Observation Drawing:**
   - Students select a plant specimen from the garden
   - Practice contour drawing (drawing the outline without looking at the paper)
   - Add interior details: veins, textures, color gradations
   - Label structures with scientific vocabulary

2. **Scientific Illustration Standards:**
   - Accurate proportions (use measuring techniques)
   - Multiple views (whole plant, close-up of leaf, cross-section of stem)
   - Color accuracy (observe carefully, mix precisely)
   - Proper labeling with lines (not arrows) connecting labels to structures
   - Scale indicator ("actual size" or "2x magnification")

3. **Gallery Exhibition:**
   - Frame and display botanical illustrations in the school hallway or library
   - Include scientific descriptions alongside each illustration
   - Invite families and community members to view
   - Connect to the history of botanical illustration and its role in scientific documentation before photography
