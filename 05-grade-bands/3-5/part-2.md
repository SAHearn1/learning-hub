# 3-5 Grade Band: Mathematics Curriculum

## Overview

Mathematics instruction in the 3-5 RootWork curriculum transforms the garden into a living mathematics laboratory where abstract concepts become tangible, purposeful, and deeply understood. At this developmental level, students are ready for multiplicative thinking, fractional reasoning, systematic measurement, data analysis, and geometric applications -- and every one of these domains finds authentic expression in garden-based contexts. When a student calculates the area of a garden bed to determine how many plants will fit, they are not performing a worksheet exercise; they are solving a real problem with visible consequences. When they scale a recipe to serve their entire class using fraction multiplication, the result is an actual meal. This authenticity is the engine of mathematical engagement and understanding.

For students with trauma histories, mathematics can be a particularly fraught domain. The right-or-wrong nature of arithmetic, the public nature of math class participation, and the cultural narrative that some people are "math people" and others are not -- all of these can trigger shame, avoidance, and learned helplessness. The RootWork 3-5 math curriculum addresses these barriers directly through the 5Rs Framework: the Root phase establishes safety before mathematical thinking begins, the Regulate phase ensures students can access their thinking brain, the Reflect phase uses the TRACE protocol to make mathematical reasoning visible and valued (not just answers), the Restore phase normalizes errors as essential learning data, and the Reconnect phase connects mathematical skills to real-world agency and community contribution.

## Unit 1: Multiplication & Division Through Garden Planning (Weeks 1-8)

### Essential Questions
- How do arrays help us understand multiplication?
- How are multiplication and division related?
- How can we use multiplication and division to plan our garden?

### Learning Objectives
- Interpret products of whole numbers as the total number of objects in equal groups or arrays (3.OA.1)
- Use multiplication and division within 100 to solve garden word problems (3.OA.3)
- Determine the unknown whole number in a multiplication or division equation (3.OA.4)
- Apply properties of operations as strategies to multiply and divide (3.OA.5)
- Fluently multiply and divide within 100 (3.OA.7)
- Solve multi-step garden problems using the four operations (4.OA.3)
- Find factor pairs and multiples in garden design contexts (4.OA.4)

### Week-by-Week Lesson Sequence

#### Week 1-2: Garden Rows as Arrays

**Root Phase:** Students visit the garden and observe how plants are arranged. "What patterns do you notice in how our garden is organized? Why do you think farmers and gardeners plant in rows?" Students record their regulation level and one observation about the garden layout.

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Living Arrays:**
   - In the garden, students physically arrange themselves in equal rows to model arrays
   - Teacher photographs the arrays from above (or from a second-floor window) to show the rectangular structure
   - Students count total people in different arrangements: 3 rows of 4, 4 rows of 3, 2 rows of 6, 6 rows of 2
   - Discovery: "3 rows of 4 gives us the same total as 4 rows of 3. Why?" (commutative property)

2. **Seed Tray Arrays:**
   - Each student receives a planting tray and seeds
   - "Plant 5 rows of 4 seeds each. How many seeds did you plant? Write the multiplication equation."
   - "Now plant 4 rows of 5 seeds. How does this arrangement compare? What stayed the same? What changed?"
   - TRACE Application:
     - **Think**: "What do you notice about the two arrays?"
     - **Reason**: "Why do both give us 20 seeds?"
     - **Articulate**: "Explain to your partner why changing the order of the factors doesn't change the product."
     - **Check**: "Count all the seeds in both trays to verify."
     - **Extend**: "If we needed exactly 24 seeds planted in equal rows, what are all the possible arrangements?"

3. **Garden Bed Design Challenge:**
   - "We have 36 new seedlings to transplant into a rectangular bed. What are all the possible row-and-column arrangements?"
   - Students find all factor pairs of 36: 1x36, 2x18, 3x12, 4x9, 6x6
   - Discussion: "Which arrangement would be best for our garden? Why?" (Consider spacing, sunlight access, ease of weeding)
   - Reasoning Move -- **Decompose**: "How can we break this into a smaller problem? What are the factor pairs of 36?"

**Restore Phase:** Students examine a fictional student's work: "Alex wrote 3 x 8 = 21. Where do you think Alex's thinking went wrong? What strategy might help Alex check their work?" Students practice Root Cause Analysis on the error without shame or judgment.

**Reconnect Phase:** "If a community garden has 8 plots and each plot has 6 rows of 5 tomato plants, how many tomato plants are there in the whole community garden? How could knowing multiplication help you plan a garden at home?"

#### Week 3-4: Division as Fair Sharing and Grouping

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Harvest Division:**
   - Actual garden harvest (or simulated with manipulatives): "We harvested 42 cherry tomatoes and need to share them equally among 7 students. How many does each person get?"
   - Students physically divide the harvest, dealing tomatoes one at a time ("fair sharing" model)
   - Record: 42 / 7 = 6
   - "What if we had 45 tomatoes for 7 students? How many would each get? Would there be any left over?"
   - Introduction to remainders in context: "We each get 6, and there are 3 left over. What should we do with the extras?"

2. **Planting in Groups:**
   - "We have 56 seeds and want to plant 8 in each pot. How many pots do we need?" (Grouping model)
   - Students fill pots with 8 seeds each, counting how many groups they can make
   - Record: 56 / 8 = 7 pots
   - Compare to fair sharing: "Both problems used 56 and 8, but the question was different. How?"

3. **Multiplication and Division as Inverse Operations:**
   - "If 7 rows of 8 plants equals 56 plants total, what does 56 / 8 tell us? What does 56 / 7 tell us?"
   - Students create fact families from garden scenarios: 7 x 8 = 56, 8 x 7 = 56, 56 / 7 = 8, 56 / 8 = 7
   - Reasoning Move -- **Connect**: "How is division related to multiplication? This is like how subtraction is related to addition."

**TRACE Protocol Full Application:**

*Problem: "Our school garden produced 84 ears of corn. We want to give equal amounts to 6 classrooms. How many ears does each classroom receive? If each classroom then divides their share equally among 7 students, how many ears does each student get?"*

- **Think**: What information do I have? What am I trying to find? (84 ears, 6 classrooms, 7 students per classroom)
- **Reason**: This is a two-step division problem. First divide 84 by 6, then divide the result by 7. Or I could find the total number of students (6 x 7 = 42) and divide 84 by 42.
- **Articulate**: "Each classroom gets 14 ears because 84 divided by 6 is 14. Then each student gets 2 ears because 14 divided by 7 is 2."
- **Check**: Verify: 6 classrooms x 14 ears = 84 total. 42 students x 2 ears = 84 total. Both methods confirm the answer.
- **Extend**: "What if we only harvested 80 ears? How would the distribution change? Would it still work out evenly?"

#### Week 5-6: Multi-Step Garden Word Problems

**Problem Bank (increasing complexity):**

| Problem | Operations | Grade Level | Reasoning Move |
|---------|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| "We planted 8 rows of lettuce with 6 plants per row. A frost killed 9 plants. How many survived?" | Multiplication + Subtraction | 3rd | Decompose |
| "We need 4 garden beds. Each bed requires 12 bricks for the border and 3 bags of soil. How many total bricks? How many total bags of soil?" | Multiplication (two separate) | 3rd | Decompose |
| "Tomato seedlings cost $2.75 each. We bought 8 for each of our 3 beds. What was the total cost?" | Multiplication (three factors) | 4th | Decompose |
| "We harvested 156 pounds of vegetables. We donated 1/3 to the food bank and sold the rest at $2.50 per pound. What was our revenue?" | Division + Fractions + Multiplication | 5th | Transform |
| "If we increase the length of each 6-foot bed by 3 feet, and we have 4 beds, how much total additional fencing do we need for the perimeters?" | Multi-step with area/perimeter | 5th | Generalize |

**Error Analysis Activity:**

Present three student solutions to the same problem, one correct and two with common errors:

*Problem: "We planted 7 rows of 9 sunflowers. A windstorm knocked over 15. How many are still standing?"*

- **Student A**: 7 + 9 = 16, 16 - 15 = 1. "One sunflower is left."
- **Student B**: 7 x 9 = 63, 63 - 15 = 58. "Fifty-eight sunflowers are standing."
- **Student C**: 7 x 9 = 63, 63 - 15 = 48. "Forty-eight sunflowers are standing."

Students discuss: Which solution is correct? For each incorrect solution, identify the specific error and explain how the student could fix their thinking. This activity normalizes error analysis and builds the Restore phase into mathematical practice.

#### Week 7-8: Properties and Patterns

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Distributive Property in the Garden:**
   - "We need to calculate 7 x 8 for our planting plan, but that's a hard fact. Can we break it into easier parts?"
   - Model with garden bed: split the 7 x 8 bed into a 7 x 5 section and a 7 x 3 section
   - 7 x 8 = 7 x 5 + 7 x 3 = 35 + 21 = 56
   - Students demonstrate with grid paper, drawing the split and calculating both parts
   - Reasoning Move -- **Transform**: "I can transform a hard problem into two easier problems"

2. **Patterns in Multiplication Tables:**
   - Create a multiplication chart using garden harvest data
   - Identify patterns: "What pattern do you notice in the 9s column? The 5s? What about the diagonal?"
   - Reasoning Move -- **Generalize**: "The product of any number times 9 has digits that add to 9. Does this always work?"

### Differentiation Strategies for Unit 1

| Readiness Level | Modification | Example |
|----------------|-------------|---------|
| **Approaching** | Use smaller factors (within 5 x 5); provide concrete manipulatives (actual seeds or counters); offer array templates; reduce number of steps in word problems | "Plant 3 rows of 4 seeds. How many total?" with counters available |
| **On Level** | Standard tasks as described; both concrete and representational models; two-step word problems | "We planted 6 rows of 8 seedlings and 15 didn't germinate. How many plants survived?" |
| **Advanced** | Larger factors; open-ended problems with multiple solutions; create problems for peers; three-step problems; connect to algebraic thinking | "Design a garden layout for exactly 120 plants using at least 3 different bed sizes. Write expressions showing how you calculated each bed." |

## Unit 2: Fractions Through Harvest Division & Recipe Scaling (Weeks 9-18)

### Essential Questions
- What does it mean to divide something into equal parts?
- How can fractions help us share fairly?
- How do we adjust recipes when we need more or less?

### Learning Objectives
- Understand a fraction as part of a whole and as a number on the number line (3.NF.1-3)
- Explain equivalence of fractions and compare fractions (3.NF.3, 4.NF.1-2)
- Add and subtract fractions with like and unlike denominators (4.NF.3, 5.NF.1)
- Multiply fractions and mixed numbers (5.NF.4-6)
- Divide unit fractions by whole numbers and whole numbers by unit fractions (5.NF.7)

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Dividing the Garden Bed (3rd Grade Focus)

**Root Phase:** "Imagine you and three friends find one piece of garden bed. How would you share it so everyone gets a fair piece?" Students discuss fairness and equal sharing as a regulation and community-building exercise.

**Reflect Phase:**

1. **Physical Partitioning:**
   - Using a rope or garden stakes, students divide an actual garden bed into equal sections
   - Divide a 12-foot bed into 2 equal parts (each part is 6 feet = 1/2 the bed)
   - Divide into 3 equal parts (each part is 4 feet = 1/3 the bed)
   - Divide into 4 equal parts (each part is 3 feet = 1/4 the bed)
   - Divide into 6 equal parts (each part is 2 feet = 1/6 the bed)
   - "Which parts are larger? Why is 1/3 bigger than 1/4?" Students physically stand in the sections to feel the difference.

2. **Fraction Naming in the Garden:**
   - "If we plant herbs in 2 of the 4 sections, what fraction of the bed has herbs?" (2/4)
   - "If we plant tomatoes in 1 of the 4 sections, what fraction has tomatoes?" (1/4)
   - "What fraction is NOT planted yet?" (1/4)
   - "Is 2/4 the same as any other fraction we know?" (1/2 -- equivalent fractions through garden observation)

3. **Fraction Number Line -- The Garden Path:**
   - Lay a 12-foot rope along the garden as a number line from 0 to 1
   - Mark 1/2 (6 feet), 1/4 and 3/4 (3 feet and 9 feet), 1/3 and 2/3 (4 feet and 8 feet)
   - Students walk the number line, stopping at fractional points
   - "If you walk 1/3 of the garden path, have you walked more or less than 1/2?"

**TRACE Application:**
- **Think**: "I notice that when we divided the bed into more parts, each part got smaller."
- **Reason**: "This makes sense because we're splitting the same whole into more pieces, so each piece must be smaller."
- **Articulate**: "The more pieces you cut from the same garden bed, the smaller each piece is. So 1/6 is smaller than 1/4 which is smaller than 1/3."
- **Check**: "I can verify by looking at the actual bed sections -- the 1/6 pieces really are smaller."
- **Extend**: "What if we divided the bed into 12 parts? Each would be only 1 foot! What about 24 parts?"

#### Lesson: Recipe Scaling (4th-5th Grade Focus)

**Setup:** Students prepare a garden herb pesto recipe, adjusting quantities for different serving sizes.

**Base Recipe (serves 4):**
- 2 cups fresh basil leaves (from garden)
- 1/4 cup pine nuts
- 1/2 cup olive oil
- 3/4 cup Parmesan cheese
- 2 cloves garlic (from garden)

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Doubling the Recipe (4th Grade -- multiplying fractions by whole numbers):**
   - "We need to serve 8 people instead of 4. What must we do to each ingredient?"
   - 2 cups x 2 = 4 cups basil
   - 1/4 cup x 2 = 2/4 = 1/2 cup pine nuts
   - 1/2 cup x 2 = 2/2 = 1 cup olive oil
   - 3/4 cup x 2 = 6/4 = 1 1/2 cups Parmesan
   - Reasoning Move -- **Verify**: "Does 6/4 really equal 1 1/2? Let me check with measuring cups."

2. **Halving the Recipe (5th Grade -- dividing fractions):**
   - "What if we only need to serve 2 people?"
   - 2 cups / 2 = 1 cup basil
   - 1/4 cup / 2 = 1/8 cup pine nuts
   - 1/2 cup / 2 = 1/4 cup olive oil
   - 3/4 cup / 2 = 3/8 cup Parmesan
   - "How do you divide a fraction by 2? What does 3/4 divided by 2 look like with measuring cups?"

3. **Scaling to Serve the Class (5th Grade -- fraction multiplication):**
   - "Our class has 24 students. The recipe serves 4. What multiplier do we need?" (24/4 = 6)
   - Students multiply each ingredient by 6
   - "We need 6 x 3/4 cup of Parmesan. How do we calculate that?"
   - 6 x 3/4 = 18/4 = 4 2/4 = 4 1/2 cups
   - Students actually make the recipe and serve the class

**Error Analysis -- Common Fraction Misconceptions:**

| Misconception | Example Error | Garden-Based Correction |
|--------------|--------------|------------------------|
| Larger denominator means larger fraction | Student claims 1/8 > 1/4 | "Cut a garden bed into 4 parts and 8 parts. Which pieces are bigger? The fewer cuts, the bigger each piece." |
| Adding numerators AND denominators | 1/4 + 1/4 = 2/8 | "If you eat 1/4 of a pie and then another 1/4, you've eaten 2/4 or 1/2 -- not 2/8!" Use harvest division with actual vegetables. |
| Treating fraction as two separate whole numbers | "3/4 is three and four" | "3/4 means 3 out of 4 equal parts. If our garden bed has 4 sections and 3 have plants, 3/4 of the bed is planted." |
| Improper fractions are "wrong" | Student resists writing 5/4 | "If we harvested enough tomatoes to fill 5 quarter-baskets, that's 5/4 baskets -- more than one full basket. That's not wrong; it's a lot of tomatoes!" |

## Unit 3: Measurement & Data with Garden Growth Tracking (Weeks 19-26)

### Essential Questions
- How do we accurately measure and record garden data?
- What stories do our data tell about our garden?
- How can measurement help us make better garden decisions?

### Learning Objectives
- Measure and estimate liquid volumes and masses of objects (3.MD.2)
- Draw scaled picture graphs and bar graphs from garden data (3.MD.3)
- Understand area as covering and calculate area of rectangles (3.MD.5-7)
- Know and use formulas for perimeter and area of rectangles (4.MD.3)
- Convert measurement units within a system (4.MD.1, 5.MD.1)
- Represent and interpret data in line plots with fractional measurements (4.MD.4, 5.MD.2)
- Understand volume as filling and calculate volume of rectangular prisms (5.MD.3-5)

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Garden Growth Data Collection (Ongoing Project, All Grades)

**Setup:** Each student or pair is assigned a plant to track over 8+ weeks. Students maintain a data notebook with systematic records.

**Data Collection Protocol:**

| Measurement | Tool | Unit | Frequency | Recording Method |
|-------------|------|------|-----------|-----------------|
| Plant height | Ruler/tape measure | Inches (convert to feet) | Weekly | Data table + line graph |
| Leaf count | Observation | Count | Weekly | Data table + bar graph |
| Leaf length | Ruler | Inches (to nearest 1/4 inch) | Biweekly | Line plot |
| Stem diameter | Calipers or string + ruler | Centimeters | Biweekly | Data table |
| Fruit/flower count | Observation | Count | Weekly (when applicable) | Data table + pictograph |
| Soil moisture | Moisture meter | Scale 1-10 | Daily (rotating student job) | Class chart |
| Rainfall | Rain gauge | Inches | Daily | Class chart + cumulative graph |

**TRACE Protocol for Data Analysis (Week 6):**

Students analyze six weeks of growth data:

- **Think**: "Look at your growth data table and graph. What patterns do you notice? What surprises you?"
- **Reason**: "Why do you think the growth rate slowed down in Week 4? Look at the weather data for that same period. What was happening with rainfall and temperature?"
- **Articulate**: "Write a data analysis paragraph explaining what your data shows about your plant's growth. Use at least three specific data points as evidence."
- **Check**: "Review your measurements. Are there any that seem wrong compared to the pattern? Remeasure if needed. Exchange data tables with a partner and check each other's graphs for accuracy."
- **Extend**: "Based on your data, predict your plant's height in two weeks. What additional data would you want to collect to make a better prediction?"

#### Lesson: Area and Perimeter of Garden Beds (3rd-4th Grade)

**Root Phase:** "Today we're going to figure out exactly how much space our garden takes up and how much material we'd need to put a border around it. Where have you seen people measure the edges or surfaces of things?"

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Perimeter Discovery:**
   - Students measure each side of a rectangular garden bed with tape measures
   - Calculate perimeter by adding all four sides: "Our bed is 8 feet long and 4 feet wide. P = 8 + 4 + 8 + 4 = 24 feet."
   - "If fencing costs $3.50 per foot, how much would it cost to fence this bed?"
   - Reasoning Move -- **Generalize**: "Can you find a shortcut formula for the perimeter of any rectangle?" (P = 2l + 2w or P = 2(l + w))

2. **Area Through Covering:**
   - Lay 1-foot square tiles (or grid paper) inside the garden bed
   - Count the tiles: 8 rows of 4 = 32 square feet
   - "If each square foot can hold one tomato plant, how many plants fit?"
   - "What if we want 6 inches between plants? How does that change our calculation?"
   - Connect to multiplication: A = l x w = 8 x 4 = 32 square feet

3. **Garden Design Challenge:**
   - "We have 40 feet of fencing and need to create a rectangular garden bed. What dimensions should we choose to maximize the growing area?"
   - Students test different dimensions with the same perimeter:
     - 1 x 19 = 19 sq ft (perimeter = 40)
     - 5 x 15 = 75 sq ft (perimeter = 40)
     - 8 x 12 = 96 sq ft (perimeter = 40)
     - 10 x 10 = 100 sq ft (perimeter = 40)
   - Discovery: "The square has the greatest area for a given perimeter!"
   - Reasoning Move -- **Generalize**: "Is this always true? Let's test with different perimeters."

#### Lesson: Volume of Raised Beds (5th Grade)

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **How Much Soil?**
   - Measure the dimensions of a raised bed: length, width, and depth
   - "Our bed is 8 ft long, 4 ft wide, and 1 ft deep. How many cubic feet of soil do we need?"
   - V = l x w x h = 8 x 4 x 1 = 32 cubic feet
   - "Soil is sold in bags of 2 cubic feet. How many bags do we need?" (32 / 2 = 16 bags)
   - "If each bag costs $4.99, what's our total cost?" ($4.99 x 16 = $79.84)

2. **Comparing Bed Designs:**
   - "Which holds more soil: a bed that is 10 ft x 2 ft x 1.5 ft or one that is 6 ft x 4 ft x 1.5 ft?"
   - Bed A: 10 x 2 x 1.5 = 30 cubic feet
   - Bed B: 6 x 4 x 1.5 = 36 cubic feet
   - "The bed with less perimeter holds MORE soil. Why?"
   - Reasoning Move -- **Compare**: "What is similar and different about these beds? Same height, different base dimensions."

3. **Water Volume Estimation:**
   - "Our rain barrel is a cylinder (approximately 2 ft diameter, 3 ft tall). About how much water can it hold?"
   - Introduce cylinder volume conceptually: "It's like stacking circular layers"
   - Approximate: "The circular cross-section is about 3 square feet (pi x 1^2 is about 3.14). Times 3 feet tall gives us about 9.4 cubic feet."
   - Convert to gallons: 1 cubic foot is about 7.5 gallons, so approximately 70 gallons
   - "How many garden watering sessions can we get from one full rain barrel?"

## Unit 4: Geometry Through Garden Design & Construction (Weeks 27-32)

### Essential Questions
- What geometric shapes and properties help us design better gardens?
- How does understanding angles help us build garden structures?
- How can coordinate systems help us plan and map our garden?

### Learning Objectives
- Classify shapes by properties of their lines and angles (3.G.1, 4.G.1-3)
- Recognize and draw lines of symmetry (4.G.3)
- Use a coordinate system to specify locations and graph data (5.G.1-2)
- Understand angle measurement and use a protractor (4.MD.5-7)
- Classify two-dimensional figures based on properties (5.G.3-4)

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Garden Blueprint Design (4th-5th Grade)

**Reflect Phase Activities:**

1. **Coordinate Grid Garden Map:**
   - Students create a scaled map of the garden on a coordinate grid
   - Establish the origin (0,0) at the southwest corner of the garden
   - Plot key features: garden beds, paths, water sources, tool shed, compost bin
   - "The tomato bed starts at (2, 3) and ends at (6, 7). What are the dimensions?" (4 units x 4 units)
   - "Plot the location where we should install the new rain gauge so it's equidistant from the two garden beds."

2. **Symmetry in Garden Design:**
   - Examine formal garden designs (printouts of botanical gardens, traditional Islamic gardens, French parterre gardens)
   - Identify lines of symmetry in each design
   - "Design a symmetrical garden layout using the coordinate grid. Your design must have at least one line of symmetry."
   - Students fold their designs along the proposed line of symmetry to verify

3. **Trellis Angle Investigation:**
   - Examine the angles in trellis and support structures
   - Measure angles with protractors: "What angle does the trellis make with the ground?"
   - "Trellises work best at 60-75 degree angles. Is ours within that range?"
   - Classify angles found in garden structures: right angles in bed corners, acute angles in A-frame trellises, obtuse angles in leaning supports
   - Reasoning Move -- **Question**: "Why does the angle of the trellis matter for plant growth? What would happen if it were too steep? Too shallow?"

## Unit 5: Multi-Step Problems with Garden Economics (Weeks 33-36)

### Essential Questions
- How can mathematics help us run a successful garden?
- What does it mean for a garden to be economically sustainable?
- How do decimals and operations work together in real financial planning?

### Learning Objectives
- Solve multi-step word problems with whole numbers, fractions, and decimals (4.OA.3, 5.NBT.7)
- Apply operations with decimals in financial contexts (5.NBT.5-7)
- Write and interpret numerical expressions (5.OA.1-2)
- Use mathematical reasoning to make decisions with real-world consequences

### Core Lesson: The Garden Business Plan

**Overview:** This culminating project integrates all mathematical units into a comprehensive garden business plan. Students work in teams to plan, budget, and project revenue for a school garden produce stand.

**Phase 1: Start-Up Costs (Weeks 33-34)**

Students calculate total start-up costs:

| Item | Unit Price | Quantity Needed | Calculation | Total |
|------|-----------|----------------|-------------|-------|
| Seed packets (tomato) | $3.49 | 4 | 4 x $3.49 | $13.96 |
| Seed packets (lettuce) | $2.99 | 6 | 6 x $2.99 | $17.94 |
| Seed packets (herb mix) | $4.25 | 3 | 3 x $4.25 | $12.75 |
| Soil (2 cu ft bags) | $5.99 | 16 | 16 x $5.99 | $95.84 |
| Compost (1 cu ft bags) | $3.75 | 8 | 8 x $3.75 | $30.00 |
| Garden gloves (pairs) | $4.50 | 12 | 12 x $4.50 | $54.00 |
| Watering can | $8.99 | 3 | 3 x $8.99 | $26.97 |
| Plant labels | $0.15 | 50 | 50 x $0.15 | $7.50 |
| **Total Start-Up Cost** | | | | **$258.96** |

- Students use multi-digit decimal multiplication and addition
- Reasoning Move -- **Decompose**: "Break the budget into categories (seeds, soil, tools) and total each category before finding the grand total"

**Phase 2: Revenue Projection (Week 35)**

Students project harvest yields and potential revenue:

- "If each tomato plant produces approximately 15 pounds of tomatoes, and we have 16 plants, how many total pounds can we expect?" (240 pounds)
- "If we sell tomatoes at $2.50 per pound, what is our projected revenue?" (240 x $2.50 = $600.00)
- "But realistically, about 1/4 of our harvest may be damaged by pests or weather. Adjust your projection." (3/4 x 240 = 180 pounds; 180 x $2.50 = $450.00)
- Students calculate profit: Revenue - Costs = $450.00 - $258.96 = $191.04
- "Is our garden business profitable? What could we do to increase profit?"

**Phase 3: Decision-Making with Data (Week 36)**

Students analyze which crops are most profitable:

| Crop | Yield per Plant | Plants | Total Yield | Price per Pound | Revenue | Seed Cost | Profit per Plant |
|------|----------------|--------|-------------|----------------|---------|-----------|-----------------|
| Tomatoes | 15 lbs | 16 | 240 lbs | $2.50 | $600 | $0.87 | $36.63 |
| Lettuce | 0.75 lbs | 24 | 18 lbs | $4.00 | $72 | $0.50 | $2.50 |
| Basil | 0.5 lbs | 12 | 6 lbs | $12.00 | $72 | $1.42 | $4.58 |
| Peppers | 8 lbs | 8 | 64 lbs | $3.00 | $192 | $1.06 | $22.94 |

- "Which crop has the highest profit per plant? The highest total revenue?"
- "If you could only plant two crops, which would you choose and why?"
- "What factors besides profit should we consider?" (nutrition, community needs, growing difficulty, water requirements)
- Reasoning Move -- **Compare**: "Compare the crops on multiple dimensions, not just profit."

### Assessment of Mathematical Understanding

**Performance Task Rubric for Garden Business Plan:**

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Distinguished (4) |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Computation** | Multiple errors in calculations | Some computational errors that don't affect overall reasoning | Accurate calculations throughout | Accurate calculations with verification using alternative methods |
| **Problem Solving** | Attempts but doesn't complete multi-step problems | Completes most steps but may miss one component | Completes all steps logically and accurately | Identifies additional considerations and extends the problem |
| **Mathematical Reasoning** | Limited explanation of thinking | Partial explanations; some reasoning visible | Clear explanations with mathematical vocabulary and evidence | Sophisticated reasoning that compares strategies, generalizes, and connects to broader principles |
| **TRACE Protocol** | Minimal engagement with TRACE steps | Engages with some TRACE steps | Completes all TRACE steps meaningfully | Uses TRACE autonomously with depth and insight; generates new questions |
| **Representation** | One mode of representation | Two modes of representation | Multiple representations (table, graph, equation, words) with connections between them | Creates innovative representations that clarify thinking and communicate to an audience |
| **Real-World Application** | Limited connection to real context | Some connection to garden context | Strong connection between math and real garden decisions | Uses mathematics to propose actionable solutions with evidence-based justification |

### Year-Round Mathematical Connections Calendar

| Season | Mathematical Focus | Garden Connection |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Fall** | Multiplication arrays, measurement introduction, data collection setup | Planting fall crops in arrays; measuring bed dimensions; establishing data collection routines |
| **Winter** | Fractions, recipe scaling, area/perimeter planning | Scaling recipes with indoor herbs; designing spring garden layouts on graph paper; fraction of seed packets used |
| **Spring** | Data analysis, volume, geometry, garden economics | Analyzing growth data; filling raised beds (volume); mapping garden with coordinates; budgeting for plant sale |
| **Year-End** | Multi-step problems, garden business plan, data presentation | Culminating project integrating all domains; presenting mathematical findings to community |
