# 3-5 Grade Band: Social-Emotional Learning & Self-Regulation

## Overview

Social-emotional learning in the 3-5 RootWork curriculum undergoes a fundamental shift from the adult-directed, co-regulation model of K-2 to a model that builds genuine self-regulation capacity, deepens interpersonal skills, and cultivates emerging identity as a competent, contributing community member. Students ages eight through eleven are in a critical period for social-emotional development: they are becoming increasingly self-aware, capable of understanding others' perspectives, and invested in peer relationships. They are also developing the metacognitive capacity to observe their own emotional responses and, with explicit instruction and practice, to choose regulatory strategies intentionally.

The garden continues to serve as the central metaphor and physical environment for SEL development, but at this level, the metaphors become more sophisticated. Where K-2 students understood that "plants need water and sunlight to grow, and so do we," 3-5 students can explore deeper parallels: that growth requires struggle (plants that face wind develop stronger stems), that ecosystems depend on diversity (monocultures are vulnerable), that pruning -- cutting away what is not healthy -- makes room for new growth, and that seasons of dormancy are not failure but preparation for the next cycle of growth.

For students with trauma histories, the 3-5 developmental period can surface new challenges. Growing metacognition means students become more aware of their own histories and how they differ from peers. Social comparison intensifies, and students may begin to construct narratives about themselves as "damaged" or "different." The RootWork SEL curriculum intentionally counters this through garden-based experiences that build competence, belonging, and narrative coherence -- helping every student see themselves as a capable grower, thinker, and contributor.

## CASEL Framework Alignment for Ages 8-11

### 1. Self-Awareness

**Definition:** Recognizing one's own emotions, thoughts, values, and their influence on behavior across contexts.

**3-5 Developmental Markers:**
- Can identify and differentiate between nuanced emotions (frustrated vs. angry, anxious vs. scared, disappointed vs. sad)
- Beginning to recognize physical signals of emotional states (tight chest = anxiety, clenched fists = anger, heavy limbs = sadness)
- Growing awareness of personal strengths and challenges
- Can reflect on how emotions influence behavior and academic performance
- Developing a sense of personal identity and values

**Garden-Integrated Activities:**

| Skill | Activity | 5Rs Connection |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Emotional granularity | "Regulation Thermometer" -- students rate and name their emotional state on a 0-10 scale with nuanced vocabulary (0 = frozen/numb, 3 = low energy/sad, 5 = regulated/calm, 7 = alert/excited, 10 = overwhelmed/explosive) | Root Phase |
| Body-emotion connection | "Body Weather Report" -- students scan their body and note where they feel tension, energy, or calm, then connect these physical sensations to emotional states. "My shoulders are up by my ears -- I think I'm stressed." | Root/Regulate |
| Strengths identification | "Root System Map" -- students draw themselves as a tree, with roots representing their strengths and values, trunk representing their core identity, branches representing their goals, and leaves representing what they're currently growing | Reconnect Phase |
| Self-reflection | Weekly TRACE journal prompt: "This week, I noticed that when I feel ___, I tend to ___. A strategy that helped me was ___." | Reflect Phase |
| Values clarification | "What matters to you in the garden?" discussion -- students identify what they value (fairness, beauty, productivity, community) and how those values guide their decisions | Reconnect Phase |

**Detailed Activity: The Regulation Thermometer**

Materials: Large poster-sized thermometer display for the classroom; individual pocket-sized thermometer cards for each student

Procedure:
1. Teach the regulation scale explicitly during the first two weeks of school:
   - **0-2 (Blue zone):** Very low energy. Frozen, numb, disconnected, shut down. "Like a garden in deep winter -- everything is dormant."
   - **3-4 (Cool green zone):** Low energy. Sad, tired, bored, withdrawn. "Like a garden on a cloudy day -- growing slowly."
   - **5-6 (Green zone):** Regulated. Calm, focused, alert, ready to learn. "Like a garden in perfect growing conditions -- warm sun, enough water, rich soil."
   - **7-8 (Yellow zone):** High energy. Excited, anxious, silly, frustrated. "Like a garden on a very hot day -- things are moving fast, and we need to be careful."
   - **9-10 (Red zone):** Overwhelmed. Angry, panicked, out of control. "Like a garden in a storm -- everything is blowing around, and it's hard to see clearly."

2. During Root phase each day, students privately note their regulation level on their pocket thermometer card
3. Teacher scans the room and notes any students consistently in blue or red zones for individual follow-up
4. Students are taught that ALL zones are normal and expected. The goal is not to always be in the green zone, but to notice where you are and know what to do about it.
5. Students develop personal "If-Then" regulation plans: "If I notice I'm at a 7 or 8, then I will ___ (take three deep breaths, ask to visit the regulation station, journal for two minutes, squeeze my stress ball)."

**Developmental Progression:**
- 3rd grade: Learn the scale; identify zones; begin connecting physical sensations to emotional states
- 4th grade: Track patterns over time ("I notice I'm usually in the yellow zone on Monday mornings"); develop personal regulation plans
- 5th grade: Analyze triggers and patterns across contexts; coach peers; articulate regulation strategies in academic settings ("I was feeling overwhelmed during the math test, so I used box breathing and was able to refocus")

### 2. Self-Management

**Definition:** Managing one's emotions, thoughts, and behaviors effectively in different situations to achieve goals and aspirations.

**3-5 Garden Integration:**

| Skill | Activity | 5Rs Connection |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Impulse control | Long-term garden projects requiring patience (growing from seed to harvest: 60-120 days); resisting the urge to pull up seedlings to check roots | Reflect/Regulate |
| Stress management | Personal regulation toolkit development; explicit teaching of box breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, and cognitive reframing | Regulate Phase |
| Self-discipline | Independent garden maintenance responsibilities; completing multi-step academic tasks without constant reminders | Root/Reflect |
| Goal setting | SMART garden goals: "By the end of the month, I will have completed three garden journal entries with data tables and analysis." | Root/Reconnect |
| Organizational skills | Managing investigation notebooks, maintaining data collection schedules, organizing garden tools and supplies | Reflect Phase |
| Self-motivation | Connecting garden work to personal values and interests; choosing independent research topics | Reconnect Phase |

**Detailed Activity: Personal Regulation Toolkit**

Over the first month of school, students develop a personalized set of regulation strategies:

**Week 1: Explore and Experiment**
- Students rotate through 8 regulation strategy stations, spending 5 minutes at each:
  1. **Box Breathing Station:** Inhale 4 counts, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4. Visual guide on the wall.
  2. **Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR) Station:** Audio recording guides students through tensing and releasing muscle groups. "Squeeze your fists tight like you're gripping a garden hose... hold... now release and feel the difference."
  3. **5-4-3-2-1 Grounding Station:** Identify 5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you can touch, 2 you smell, 1 you taste. Use garden items for sensory anchors.
  4. **Journaling Station:** Write or draw about what you're feeling. Prompts available but not required.
  5. **Movement Station:** Garden stretching sequence, walking the perimeter, gentle yoga poses.
  6. **Mindful Observation Station:** Spend 5 minutes observing a single plant or garden creature in complete silence. Draw what you see.
  7. **Sensory Station:** Touch soil, smell herbs (lavender, mint, rosemary), sort seeds by size.
  8. **Peer Regulation Station:** Practice asking a partner, "How are you doing? Is there anything I can do to help?"

**Week 2: Reflect and Select**
- Students journal about which strategies felt most effective for them
- Complete a "My Regulation Toolkit" card listing their top 3 strategies
- Discuss: "Not every strategy works for every person. That's normal. A gardener has many tools, and they choose the right one for the job."

**Week 3: Practice and Apply**
- Students practice using their selected strategies during academic tasks
- Teacher creates opportunities for low-stakes practice: "Before we start this challenging math problem, take a moment to check your thermometer and use a strategy if you need one."

**Week 4: Refine and Commit**
- Students finalize their toolkit cards (laminated, kept in desk or journal)
- Partner share: "My top regulation strategy is ___ because ___"
- Class agreement: "In our learning garden, everyone has a toolkit and everyone has permission to use it."

### 3. Social Awareness

**Definition:** Understanding the perspectives of others, including those from diverse backgrounds, cultures, and contexts; empathizing with others; recognizing societal norms.

**3-5 Garden Integration:**

| Skill | Activity | 5Rs Connection |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Perspective-taking | Socratic seminars on garden dilemmas where students argue from assigned perspectives (farmer, environmentalist, consumer, pollinator) | Reflect Phase |
| Empathy | "Compassion Projects" -- identifying community needs and designing garden-based responses (growing food for food banks, creating sensory gardens for special needs students) | Reconnect Phase |
| Appreciating diversity | Growing plants from diverse cultural traditions; celebrating different foodways; exploring how different communities garden | Reconnect Phase |
| Recognizing resources and supports | Mapping community garden resources; identifying people who can help when garden challenges arise | Reconnect Phase |
| Understanding social norms across contexts | Developing and revising class garden agreements; discussing why different contexts have different expectations | Root Phase |

**Detailed Activity: Garden Dilemma Socratic Seminar**

Students practice perspective-taking through structured discussions of authentic garden dilemmas:

**Dilemma 1: The Pesticide Question**
"Aphids are destroying our lettuce crop. We could use an organic pesticide (neem oil) that would kill the aphids but might also harm beneficial insects like ladybugs and bees. Or we could try companion planting and hand-removal, which is slower and might not save this harvest."

Assigned perspectives:
- The gardener who planted the lettuce and wants to save the harvest
- The student who researched pollinators and cares about bees
- The food bank coordinator who is counting on this lettuce donation
- The garden ecosystem scientist who studies insect populations

Discussion protocol:
1. Each student prepares their perspective with evidence (5 minutes)
2. Opening statements from each perspective (2 minutes each)
3. Open discussion with sentence frames (10 minutes):
   - "From my perspective as ___, I believe ___ because ___"
   - "I hear your concern about ___. However, from my perspective ___"
   - "What if we considered ___?"
4. Seek common ground: "Is there a solution that addresses everyone's concerns?" (5 minutes)
5. Reflection: "How did it feel to argue from a perspective that wasn't your own?" (5 minutes)

**Dilemma 2: The Fair Share Question**
"Our garden produced 50 pounds of tomatoes. The 5th graders did most of the physical work, the 4th graders managed the data and planning, and the 3rd graders watered and weeded. How should we distribute the harvest?"

**Dilemma 3: The Expansion Question**
"We have the opportunity to expand our garden, but it would mean removing the only shade tree on the playground. The tree provides shade for students during recess and is home to birds."

### 4. Relationship Skills

**Definition:** Establishing and maintaining healthy relationships; communicating clearly; collaborating effectively; resolving conflicts constructively; resisting negative social pressure.

**3-5 Garden Integration:**

| Skill | Activity | 5Rs Connection |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Clear communication | Using "I" statements during garden conflicts; giving specific, constructive feedback on peer work | Reflect/Restore |
| Active listening | Structured partner sharing with "listener repeats back what they heard" protocol | Reflect Phase |
| Collaboration | Team garden projects with defined roles (manager, researcher, recorder, builder); rotating roles to build flexibility | Reflect Phase |
| Conflict resolution | "Garden Repair" protocol for resolving interpersonal conflicts using garden metaphors | Restore Phase |
| Resisting negative social pressure | Discussing scenarios where peers want to damage the garden, take more than their share, or exclude someone | Reconnect Phase |
| Leadership | Student-led garden planning meetings; mentoring younger students in K-2 garden sessions | Reconnect Phase |

**Detailed Activity: Regulation Partnerships**

In 3-5, students develop "Regulation Partnerships" -- trained peer support relationships:

**Training Sequence (4 sessions, 20 minutes each):**

*Session 1: Understanding Co-Regulation*
- "When your brain is in the storm zone, sometimes another person's calm can help you find your calm. This is called co-regulation."
- Garden metaphor: "When a plant is being battered by wind, a stake can help hold it up until the storm passes. A regulation partner is like a garden stake -- not doing the growing for you, but providing support when things are rough."
- Practice: Partner A closes their eyes and breathes fast and shallow (simulating stress). Partner B says calmly: "I'm right here. Let's breathe together." Both partners slow their breathing together.

*Session 2: What to Say and What NOT to Say*

| Helpful (Say This) | Not Helpful (Avoid This) |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| "I notice you seem ___ right now. Is that right?" | "What's wrong with you?" |
| "Would you like to take a walk to the garden together?" | "Just calm down." |
| "Is there anything I can do?" | "You're overreacting." |
| "I'm going to sit here with you if that's okay." | "Why are you so upset? It's not a big deal." |
| "Want to try some box breathing together?" | "Stop crying." |
| "This is hard. It's okay to take a break." | "You should..." (unsolicited advice) |

*Session 3: Boundaries and When to Get an Adult*
- "You are not a counselor or a therapist. Your job is to offer calm presence and suggest strategies."
- "If your partner is hurting themselves or someone else, is talking about wanting to die, or is so upset that nothing you do helps after 5 minutes -- get an adult immediately."
- Practice scenarios with role-play

*Session 4: Practice and Partnership Assignment*
- Students are paired (teacher-selected, considering personality fit and emotional maturity)
- Partnerships last 6-8 weeks, then rotate
- Daily check-in during Root phase: "How's your partner doing today?"
- Weekly reflection: "How did I support my regulation partner this week? How did they support me?"

**Detailed Activity: Garden Repair Protocol**

When interpersonal conflict occurs (which it will), the Garden Repair protocol provides a structured path to resolution:

**Step 1: Cool Down (Regulate Phase)**
- Both parties take 5 minutes apart to use their personal regulation strategies
- "You can't repair a garden during a storm. Wait until the weather settles."

**Step 2: Root the Problem (Root Phase)**
- Each person states the problem from their perspective using "I" statements:
  - "I felt ___ when ___ because ___"
  - "What I needed was ___"
- The other person listens without interrupting and then paraphrases: "I hear you saying that you felt ___ because ___. Is that right?"

**Step 3: Reflect on Impact (Reflect Phase)**
- Each person describes the impact of the conflict on themselves, on the other person, and on the garden community
- "How did this affect our work together? How did it affect others around us?"

**Step 4: Restore the Relationship (Restore Phase)**
- Each person takes responsibility for their part: "What I could have done differently is ___"
- Develop a plan together: "Next time this happens, we agree to ___"
- "What do we need from each other going forward?"

**Step 5: Reconnect (Reconnect Phase)**
- Return to collaborative work together
- Follow up in one week: "How is our agreement working? Do we need to adjust anything?"
- Teacher checks in privately with both students

### 5. Responsible Decision-Making

**Definition:** Making caring and constructive choices about personal behavior and social interactions considering ethics, safety, and well-being.

**3-5 Garden Integration:**

| Skill | Activity | 5Rs Connection |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Identifying problems | Noticing garden problems (pests, disease, soil issues) and framing them as challenges to solve rather than catastrophes | Reflect Phase |
| Analyzing situations | Using TRACE protocol to examine garden decisions from multiple angles before acting | Reflect Phase |
| Problem solving | Engineering design challenges; garden management decisions using data | Reflect/Restore |
| Evaluating consequences | "If-Then" analysis of garden decisions: "If we use all our water today, then what happens tomorrow?" | Reconnect Phase |
| Ethical reasoning | Debates about organic vs. conventional methods, pesticide use, water conservation, food distribution fairness | Reconnect Phase |
| Reflecting on outcomes | Post-decision analysis: "We decided to ___. Here's what happened. Was it the right choice? What would we do differently?" | Restore/Reconnect |

**Detailed Activity: Garden Ethics Case Studies**

Students analyze real-world scenarios that connect garden science to ethical decision-making:

**Case Study 1: Water Rights**
"During a drought, the city restricts water use. Our school garden uses 200 gallons per week. Should we continue watering at the same rate, reduce watering (and risk losing crops donated to the food bank), or stop watering entirely?"

Students must:
- Research the situation (drought severity, water sources, community impact)
- Consider multiple stakeholders (school, community, food bank recipients, water system, environment)
- Propose a solution with evidence-based justification
- Present and defend their reasoning to the class

**Case Study 2: The Invasive Species Decision**
"A beautiful flowering plant in our garden turns out to be an invasive species that is spreading into the neighboring natural area and displacing native plants. Should we remove it? It's the only food source for a particular butterfly in our garden."

**Case Study 3: Genetic Modification**
"A company offers our school free seeds that are genetically modified to resist pests, which would reduce our need for any pesticides. However, some families in our school have concerns about GMOs. Should we plant them?"

## Trauma-Responsive Practices for Middle Childhood

### Understanding Fight, Flight, Freeze in Academic Settings

Students in grades 3-5 benefit from explicit, age-appropriate instruction about their nervous system responses:

**Lesson: Your Brain in the Garden**

**Objective:** Students understand the basics of the stress response and how it affects learning.

**Reflect Phase:**

1. **The Brain Garden Model:**
   - Draw the brain as a garden with three sections:
     - **The Survival Garden (brain stem and amygdala):** "This is the oldest part of your brain. It's like the roots of a plant -- always working underground to keep you safe. When it senses danger, it triggers fight (attack the threat), flight (run from the threat), or freeze (become invisible to the threat)."
     - **The Emotion Garden (limbic system):** "This is like the stems and branches -- it connects the roots to the leaves. It processes feelings and memories. It decides whether something is safe or dangerous based on past experience."
     - **The Thinking Garden (prefrontal cortex):** "This is like the leaves and flowers -- the newest growth, the part that does the most complex work. It's where you plan, reason, create, and learn. But here's the important thing: when the Survival Garden activates, it can temporarily shut down the Thinking Garden."

2. **Recognizing Your Response Pattern:**
   - "Which response do you tend to have when you feel stressed or unsafe?"
   - **Fight:** Feeling angry, argumentative, wanting to resist or push back. "In the garden, this might look like throwing tools, arguing with a partner, or refusing to participate."
   - **Flight:** Feeling the urge to run, escape, avoid. "In the garden, this might look like leaving the group, hiding, or finding excuses not to do the challenging task."
   - **Freeze:** Feeling stuck, numb, unable to think or move. "In the garden, this might look like staring blankly, not responding when called on, or feeling like your brain went offline."
   - Normalize all responses: "None of these are bad. They are your brain trying to protect you. But when you recognize them, you can choose to send a safety signal to your survival brain so your thinking brain can come back online."

3. **The Safety Signal Toolkit:**
   - Practice strategies that specifically activate the parasympathetic nervous system (the body's "rest and digest" system that counteracts the stress response):
     - **Extended exhale breathing:** Inhale for 4 counts, exhale for 8 counts. "The longer exhale tells your survival brain that you are safe."
     - **Bilateral stimulation:** Alternating tapping on knees, walking, or butterfly hug (cross arms and tap shoulders alternately). "This helps both sides of your brain reconnect."
     - **Orienting to safety:** Look around the room and name 5 safe things you see. "This tells your survival brain that there is no actual threat right now."
     - **Temperature change:** Hold a cold water bottle, splash cool water on wrists, step outside into cool air. "The temperature shift activates a calming reflex."
     - **Social engagement:** Make eye contact with a trusted person, hear a calm voice, feel a safe touch (if the student consents). "Human connection is the most powerful safety signal."

### Supporting Students with Complex Trauma

**Guiding Principles:**

1. **Safety first, always.** Before any academic demand, ask: "Does this student feel safe right now?" If not, address safety before content. The 5Rs framework ensures this by beginning every session with Root and Regulate.

2. **Behavior is communication.** When a student acts out, shuts down, or disengages, ask "What is this behavior telling me about what this child needs?" rather than "How do I stop this behavior?"

3. **Relationships before content.** A student who does not trust the adult in the room cannot learn from them. Invest in relationship-building: learn their interests, greet them by name, notice their efforts, remember what they share.

4. **Predictability reduces hypervigilance.** The 5Rs structure provides a predictable rhythm that gradually allows the survival brain to stand down. Changes to routine should be previewed whenever possible.

5. **Choice restores agency.** Trauma involves a loss of control. Offering choices -- even small ones (which garden bed to work in, which tool to use, which partner to work with) -- restores a sense of agency.

6. **Strength-based framing.** Students with trauma histories have developed remarkable survival skills. Reframe these as strengths when appropriate: "You are really observant -- you noticed every change in the garden today. That's a skill that makes you an excellent scientist."

**Classroom Strategies by Trauma Response:**

| Response | Classroom Presentation | Teacher Strategy |
|----------|----------------------|-----------------|
| **Fight** | Arguing, defiance, aggression, destroying work, verbal outbursts | Reduce demands; offer structured choices; avoid power struggles; de-escalate privately; provide heavy work in the garden (digging, carrying); after regulation, address the behavior without shame |
| **Flight** | Leaving the area, avoiding tasks, shutting down, excessive bathroom requests, hiding under furniture | Do not chase or demand return; offer a safe place to go (regulation station, garden bench); check in calmly: "I noticed you needed some space. I'm here when you're ready."; reduce the perceived threat (simplify the task, offer help) |
| **Freeze** | Blank staring, non-responsiveness, extreme compliance, inability to make decisions, mutism | Reduce stimulation; speak softly and slowly; offer sensory grounding (cold water, textured object, strong scent); do NOT demand verbal response; wait; offer simple, closed-ended choices: "Would you like to stay here or walk to the garden?" |
| **Fawn** (people-pleasing) | Excessive agreement, inability to express needs, doing whatever others want, abandoning own preferences | Explicitly validate their opinions; offer low-stakes choices to practice autonomy; normalize disagreement; model that saying "no" is healthy; in the garden: "What do YOU think we should plant? I want YOUR opinion." |

### Building Resilience Through Garden Experiences

**Resilience-Building Practices:**

1. **Productive Struggle with Support:**
   - Garden challenges (seedlings that die, pest invasions, weather damage) provide natural opportunities for resilience-building
   - Teacher response model: "This is disappointing. Let's feel that disappointment for a moment. Now, what can we learn from this? What will we try next?"
   - Document "comeback stories" in the garden: plants that were damaged but recovered, harvests that initially failed but succeeded with adjustments

2. **Mastery Experiences:**
   - Ensure every student experiences genuine success in the garden: growing a plant from seed, completing a challenging investigation, presenting findings to an audience
   - "Self-efficacy (believing you can do hard things) is built through doing hard things and succeeding. The garden gives every student access to mastery experiences."

3. **Contributing to Something Larger:**
   - Community projects (food bank donations, beautification efforts, teaching younger students) give students the experience of mattering -- of being needed and valued
   - "Trauma can make you feel small and powerless. Contributing to your community helps you experience your own power and significance."

4. **Narrative Coherence:**
   - Help students construct a coherent story about their growth over time
   - Portfolio reviews: "Look at your work from September. Now look at your work today. What do you notice?"
   - Garden narrative: "This garden was bare dirt in August. Look at it now. We did that. And we grew too."

### Data Collection and Use

**Teacher Data Collection Schedule:**

| What | When | How | Purpose |
|------|------|-----|---------|
| Regulation thermometer data | Daily | Student self-report; teacher verification | Track regulation patterns; identify students needing support |
| SEL observation notes | Weekly (focus on 4-5 students per week) | Anecdotal notes during garden activities | Document competency development; inform instruction |
| Peer interaction quality | Biweekly | Structured observation during collaborative work | Assess relationship skills; adjust groupings |
| Conflict frequency and resolution | Ongoing | Incident documentation | Identify patterns; evaluate effectiveness of repair protocol |
| Student self-assessments | Monthly | Student-completed reflection forms | Build metacognition; compare to teacher observations |
| Comprehensive SEL assessment | Quarterly | Full competency rubric | Report to families; inform IEP goals; plan instruction |

**Using SEL Data:**
- Review weekly to identify students who may need additional support
- Share relevant data (with appropriate privacy protections) with school counselors, support teams, and families
- Use patterns to adjust instruction: if many students are in the blue zone on Monday mornings, build in more energizing activities; if conflicts spike during collaborative work, revisit communication skills
- Connect to IEP and 504 goals when applicable
- Celebrate growth: share trend data with students to make progress visible
