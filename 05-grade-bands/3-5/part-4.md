# 3-5 Restore Phase: Error Analysis Foundations

## Overview

The Restore phase transforms errors from sources of shame into engines of cognitive growth. For grades 3-5, this phase builds the foundations of structured error analysis -- a skill that will deepen through middle and high school. Students at this developmental level are particularly vulnerable to shame responses around academic mistakes because of growing peer awareness and self-evaluation capacity. The Restore phase deliberately counteracts this vulnerability by teaching students that errors are neurological events: moments when the brain forms new synaptic connections.

At the 3-5 level, error analysis is always conducted with educator support. Students are not expected to independently analyze their errors; rather, they are guided through a structured process that builds both cognitive understanding and emotional resilience.

**Core Question**: "What can I learn from this mistake?"
**Duration**: 5-10 minutes per error analysis episode
**Garden Metaphor**: Pruning and composting -- cutting back what is not working and turning it into nourishment for future growth.

## Core Principles for 3-5 Error Recovery

### Separating the Person from the Error

This principle is paramount at the 3-5 level, where students are developing a sense of academic identity. Language must consistently place the error in the work, not in the student.

| Instead of This | Use This |
|----------------|----------|
| "You made a mistake." | "There's something interesting happening in this step." |
| "That's wrong." | "Let's take a closer look at this part." |
| "You weren't paying attention." | "Something tricky happened here -- let's figure it out together." |
| "Try harder next time." | "Your brain just grew a new connection. Let's use it." |
| "That's not how you do it." | "I see a different path here. Walk me through your thinking." |

### The Brain Growth Reframe

Explicitly teach students the neuroscience behind learning from mistakes. At the 3-5 level, this can be introduced through the garden compost metaphor.

**Lesson Sequence: "Compost for Your Brain" (20 minutes, taught early in the year)**

1. **Garden demonstration (5 min):** Show students the school garden compost bin. Explain how food scraps and dead plants break down into rich soil that feeds new plants. (CONNECT: Link physical composting to cognitive composting.)
2. **Brain science explanation (5 min):** "When you make a mistake and figure out what went wrong, your brain builds a new pathway -- like a new root growing in soil. Scientists have actually studied this. Your brain grows more from mistakes than from getting things right the first time."
3. **Reframe practice (5 min):** Present 5 common error scenarios. Students practice saying "My brain just grew!" instead of "I messed up." (TRANSFORM: Reframe the emotional narrative around errors.)
4. **Journal entry (5 min):** Students write or draw what brain composting means to them. This page becomes a reference anchor for future Restore phase episodes.

## Structured Error Analysis Process for 3-5

When a student makes an error during the Reflect phase, the educator transitions to Restore using this five-step process.

### Step 1: Acknowledge and Reframe (30 seconds)

The educator names the moment positively and without alarm.

**Educator Language:**
- "Your brain just built a new connection. Let's explore what happened."
- "This is exactly the kind of moment where real learning happens."
- "I'm glad this happened -- we're about to learn something important."

### Step 2: Locate the Divergence Point (1-2 minutes)

Guide the student to find where their reasoning departed from a valid path. This step builds the VERIFY reasoning move.

**Educator Language:**
- "Walk me through your steps from the beginning. I'll follow along."
- "Everything looks good up to here. What happened at this step?"
- "Let's go back to the last point where you felt sure. Start from there."

### Step 3: Identify the Error Type (1-2 minutes)

Help the student categorize their error using the 3-5 error type framework. At this level, students learn three primary categories (the full five-type system is introduced in grades 6-8).

| Error Type | 3-5 Student Language | Description | Garden Example |
|------------|---------------------|-------------|----------------|
| **Computational** | "I knew what to do but my numbers got mixed up." | Correct strategy, arithmetic mistake | Measuring a bed as 6 feet when it is actually 8 feet |
| **Procedural** | "I skipped a step or did steps out of order." | Steps performed incorrectly or incompletely | Planting seeds before preparing the soil |
| **Conceptual** | "I used the wrong idea for this problem." | Fundamental misunderstanding of the concept | Thinking that bigger seeds always grow into bigger plants |

**Educator Language for Error Typing:**
- "Let's figure out what kind of mistake this was. Did you know the right steps but the numbers tripped you up? That's a computational error -- easy to fix."
- "It looks like you had a good plan but missed a step in the middle. That's a procedural error -- let's find the step that got skipped."
- "This one is interesting. The way you were thinking about this concept might need an update. That's a conceptual error -- the most powerful kind because it means your brain is about to grow the most."

### Step 4: Student Self-Correction (2-3 minutes)

Guide the student to correct the error themselves whenever possible. Self-correction builds agency and deepens understanding beyond what educator-provided corrections can achieve.

**Scaffolding Self-Correction:**
- "Now that you see where it went off track, can you try that step again?"
- "What would happen if you used a different operation here?"
- "Can you VERIFY your new answer by checking it a different way?"
- If the student struggles: "What if we DECOMPOSE this into a simpler version first? Try it with smaller numbers."

### Step 5: Consolidation and Journaling (2-3 minutes)

After correction, the student extracts a lesson from the error and records it in their growth journal under "Brain Growth Moments."

**Journal Prompt:** Students complete a simple three-part entry:

| What Happened | What I Learned | What I'll Do Next Time |
|---------------|---------------|----------------------|
| I multiplied 7 x 8 and got 54 | I sometimes mix up 7x8 and 6x9. They're close but different. | I'll use the VERIFY move -- check my multiplication a second way. |

**Educator Language for Consolidation:**
- "What would you tell a classmate to watch out for on this kind of problem?"
- "If you saw this problem again, what would you do differently?"
- "Let's add this to your Brain Growth Moments -- your brain did real work just now."

## Garden-Based Error Examples

Using garden contexts for error analysis reduces the emotional charge of mistakes by grounding them in concrete, familiar experiences.

### Activity: "Garden Measurement Mix-Up" (Grade 3-4)

Present students with a fictional garden scenario containing a deliberate error:

> "Marcus measured his garden bed to figure out how much soil he needs. The bed is 4 feet wide and 6 feet long. Marcus wrote: 4 + 6 = 10, so I need 10 square feet of soil."

1. **Think:** "What do you notice about Marcus's work?" (Students identify the given information.)
2. **Reason:** "What did Marcus do? What should he have done?" (COMPARE Marcus's approach to the correct one.)
3. **Articulate:** "Explain what error Marcus made and why." (Students identify this as a conceptual error -- addition instead of multiplication for area.)
4. **Check:** "What is the correct answer? How can you VERIFY it?" (Students calculate 4 x 6 = 24 and verify by drawing a grid.)
5. **Extend:** "Design a garden problem where someone might make the same kind of error." (GENERALIZE the error pattern to a new context.)

### Activity: "The Seed Counting Slip" (Grade 3)

> "Priya counted her seeds for planting. She counted 3 groups of 8 seeds and wrote: 3 x 8 = 28."

Students identify this as a computational error (3 x 8 = 24, not 28). They correct it, VERIFY by skip counting (8, 16, 24), and discuss strategies for avoiding multiplication errors.

### Activity: "The Growth Chart Puzzle" (Grade 4-5)

> "Jaylen recorded his plant's growth every week: Week 1: 2 cm, Week 2: 5 cm, Week 3: 8 cm, Week 4: 10 cm. He wrote: 'My plant grows exactly 3 cm every week.'"

Students identify this as a procedural error in data analysis (the pattern breaks at Week 4: the growth was only 2 cm, not 3). They practice reading data carefully and QUESTIONING assumptions. (GENERALIZE: "Just because a pattern starts doesn't mean it always continues.")

## Brain Growth Moments Journaling

Students maintain a dedicated section in their growth journal for documenting errors and lessons learned. This practice normalizes error, builds metacognitive habits, and creates a personal reference for common pitfalls.

### Journal Format

Each "Brain Growth Moment" entry follows this structure:

**Date:** ___________
**The error:** (What happened in my work)
**Error type:** Computational / Procedural / Conceptual (circle one)
**What my brain learned:** (What I understand now that I did not before)
**My strategy for next time:** (What I will do differently)
**A similar problem I can now solve:** (Student creates and solves a related problem)

### Building a Growth Mindset Through Journaling

Over time, students accumulate a record of their own growth. Periodically (monthly or quarterly), students review their Brain Growth Moments and reflect:

- "How many brain growth moments did I have this month?" (GENERALIZE: More moments means more learning, not more failure.)
- "What types of errors do I make most often?" (DECOMPOSE: Break down error patterns.)
- "What strategies have helped me the most?" (COMPARE strategies across entries.)
- "How has my thinking changed since the beginning of the year?" (CONNECT past learning to present understanding.)

## Peer Error Analysis Introduction (Grade 4-5)

Beginning in grade 4, students practice analyzing errors in other people's work. This builds critical analysis skills in a low-stakes context because the student is examining someone else's reasoning rather than defending their own.

### "Error Detective" Protocol

1. Present work attributed to a fictional student (never use real student work at this level).
2. Students work in pairs to find the error. (DECOMPOSE: Examine each step separately.)
3. Partners discuss: "What kind of error is this? What was the student probably thinking?" (QUESTION the reasoning behind the error.)
4. Partners write a "helpful hint" -- advice they would give the fictional student. (CONNECT error analysis to empathetic communication.)

### Ground Rules for Peer Error Analysis

| Do | Do Not |
|----|--------|
| Use respectful language about the fictional student's work | Laugh at the error or call it "easy" |
| Focus on the reasoning, not just the answer | Simply say "It's wrong" without explanation |
| Suggest what the student did well before noting the error | Skip acknowledging what was correct |
| Offer strategies for improvement | Just provide the right answer |

**Educator Language:**
- "Remember, this student was thinking hard when they did this work. Let's be respectful detectives."
- "What did this student do right before the error happened? Let's give them credit for that."
- "Your job is not just to find the mistake but to explain why it happened and how to fix it."

## Assessment Indicators

| Indicator | Developing (1) | Approaching (2) | Proficient (3) |
|-----------|----------------|------------------|-----------------|
| **Emotional response to errors** | Shuts down, cries, or refuses to engage | Shows frustration but remains in the process | Treats errors as expected; uses "brain growth" language |
| **Error identification** | Cannot locate where the error occurred | Locates error with educator guidance | Independently locates the divergence point |
| **Error typing** | Cannot categorize the error | Categorizes with prompting | Independently names error type |
| **Self-correction** | Requires educator to provide correction | Self-corrects with scaffolding questions | Self-corrects after locating the error |
| **Journaling** | Does not complete Brain Growth Moments entries | Completes entries with sentence starters | Writes detailed, reflective entries independently |
| **Peer error analysis** | Does not engage or is unkind | Participates but focuses only on finding the error | Finds, explains, and offers constructive strategies |

## Transition Out of Restore

After the error has been analyzed, corrected, and documented, the student returns to the Reflect phase to continue learning. The transition is always forward-looking and celebratory.

**Educator Language:**
- "Great work figuring that out. Your brain just got stronger. Ready to keep going?"
- "That brain growth is going to help with the next problem. Let's see."
- "You just turned a mistake into understanding. That's what real learners do."

If the error analysis process reveals a deeper conceptual gap, the educator adjusts the difficulty level or instructional approach before continuing. If the Restore process triggers dysregulation (frustration, tears, shutdown), the session transitions to the Regulate phase: "It seems like this was a lot for right now. Let's take a garden break and come back when your brain is rested."
