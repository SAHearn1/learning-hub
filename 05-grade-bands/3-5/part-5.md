# 3-5 Grade Band: Assessment, Projects & Community Connection

## Assessment Framework for 3-5

### Assessment Philosophy

Assessment in the 3-5 RootWork curriculum serves three essential purposes: it informs instructional decisions, documents student growth over time, and builds students' own capacity for self-assessment and metacognition. At this developmental level, students are ready to move beyond teacher-only evaluation toward a shared assessment model where they actively participate in evaluating their own learning, setting goals, and reflecting on progress. The garden provides an ideal context for authentic assessment because it produces visible, tangible evidence of learning -- a well-designed garden bed demonstrates understanding of area and geometry, a thriving plant demonstrates scientific knowledge, and a data-rich growth journal demonstrates mathematical and literacy skills simultaneously.

For students with trauma histories, traditional assessment practices -- timed tests, high-stakes evaluations, public grading, and competitive ranking -- can trigger shame, anxiety, and avoidance. The RootWork assessment framework mitigates these risks through portfolio-based documentation, performance tasks that allow multiple modes of expression, emphasis on growth over absolute performance, and explicit separation of assessment from personal worth. "We are assessing your work, not your value. Your worth as a person is never in question."

### Assessment Types and Purposes

| Assessment Type | Purpose | Frequency | Who Evaluates | 5Rs Connection |
|----------------|---------|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Diagnostic** | Identify starting points and gaps; plan instruction | Beginning of year; beginning of each unit | Teacher | Root (establishing where students are) |
| **Formative** | Monitor understanding; adjust instruction in real time | Daily/weekly | Teacher + student self-assessment | Reflect + Restore (identifying and addressing errors) |
| **Performance Tasks** | Demonstrate deep understanding through authentic application | Monthly | Teacher + peer + self | Reflect + Restore + Reconnect |
| **Portfolio** | Document growth over time; celebrate learning | Ongoing collection; quarterly review | Teacher + student + family | Reconnect (identity and narrative) |
| **Student Self-Assessment** | Build metacognitive capacity; develop autonomy | Weekly reflection; monthly comprehensive | Student (with teacher guidance) | Restore + Reconnect (growth mindset) |
| **Summative** | Evaluate mastery at the end of a unit or period | End of unit; trimester | Teacher | Reflect (demonstrating learning) |

### Diagnostic Garden Assessments

#### Beginning-of-Year Garden Assessment (3-5)

Administered during the first two weeks through garden-based activities, this diagnostic assessment evaluates students' incoming academic and social-emotional skills without the anxiety of a traditional test:

**Mathematical Thinking Tasks:**

| Task | What It Measures | Materials | Grade Adaptation |
|------|-----------------|-----------|-----------------|
| "Estimate how many seeds are in this jar, then count to verify" | Estimation, counting, place value | Jar of seeds (150-300) | 3rd: count by groups of 10; 4th: use multiplication; 5th: use estimation strategies and evaluate accuracy |
| "Design a rectangular garden bed that can hold exactly 24 plants with equal spacing" | Area, multiplication, factor pairs | Grid paper, ruler | 3rd: find arrays; 4th: calculate area; 5th: optimize dimensions for maximum area with a fixed perimeter |
| "This recipe serves 4. Adjust it to serve 12" | Fractions, multiplication, proportional reasoning | Recipe card, measuring cups | 3rd: double and add; 4th: find the multiplier; 5th: work with fraction multiplication |
| "How much soil do we need for this bed?" (provide dimensions) | Measurement, volume, multi-step computation | Tape measure, calculator | 3rd: measure and multiply length x width; 4th: add depth for volume; 5th: convert units and calculate cost |
| "Analyze this plant growth data and tell me what you notice" | Data interpretation, graphing, mathematical communication | Sample data table and graph | 3rd: read the graph; 4th: identify trends; 5th: draw conclusions and make predictions |

**Scientific Thinking Tasks:**

| Task | What It Measures | Materials |
|------|-----------------|-----------|
| "Design an experiment to find out whether plants grow better in sunlight or shade" | Understanding of controlled experiments, variables, hypothesis formation | Notebook, pencil |
| "Identify these organisms and explain their role in the garden ecosystem" | Classification, food web understanding, scientific vocabulary | Photos or specimens of 6-8 garden organisms |
| "What do you think would happen to our garden if it didn't rain for a month? Why?" | Cause and effect reasoning, systems thinking, evidence-based prediction | Verbal prompt |
| "Read this seed packet and create a planting plan" | Informational text comprehension, procedural understanding | Seed packet with growing instructions |

**TRACE Protocol Assessment:**
- Present a novel garden problem and observe whether students naturally engage in TRACE steps
- Record which steps students engage with independently and which require prompting
- Use as a baseline for TRACE protocol instruction

Results are recorded on a developmental continuum and used to plan differentiated instruction, form flexible groups, and identify students who may need additional support or enrichment.

### Formative Assessment Strategies

#### Daily Formative Checks

**1. Exit Tickets (Garden-Themed):**
At the end of each Reflect phase, students complete a brief exit ticket:
- **Mathematical:** "Draw a model showing 3/4 of a garden bed planted with tomatoes. How many square feet of tomatoes are there if the bed is 12 square feet?"
- **Scientific:** "Based on today's experiment, what is one claim you can make? What evidence supports it?"
- **Literacy:** "What is the main idea of the article we read about pollinators? What evidence from the text supports your answer?"
- **TRACE reflection:** "Which TRACE step was most challenging for you today? Why?"

**2. Observation-Based Checks:**
- Teacher circulates during garden activities with a clipboard and class roster
- Focus on 5-6 students per session for detailed observation (rotating through the class over two weeks)
- Look for: reasoning moves being used, TRACE steps being applied, common misconceptions, evidence of transfer

**Observation Recording Template:**

```
Student: _______________  Date: ___________  Activity: _______________

Reasoning Moves Observed:
[ ] Decompose  [ ] Compare  [ ] Question  [ ] Verify
[ ] Generalize  [ ] Transform  [ ] Connect

TRACE Steps Demonstrated:
[ ] Think (activated prior knowledge, made observations)
[ ] Reason (developed a plan, selected a strategy)
[ ] Articulate (explained thinking clearly)
[ ] Check (verified work, caught errors)
[ ] Extend (applied to new context, generated questions)

Specific Evidence:
_____________________________________________

Misconceptions or Errors Noted:
_____________________________________________

Next Steps for This Student:
_____________________________________________
```

**3. Journal Analysis:**
- Teachers review investigation journals weekly
- Look for growth in:
  - Scientific observation detail and accuracy
  - Mathematical reasoning and representation
  - Writing quality (organization, evidence use, vocabulary)
  - Self-reflection depth
  - TRACE protocol application
- Provide written feedback that is specific, growth-oriented, and connected to garden metaphors: "Your data analysis is getting more detailed -- like a garden getting more lush each season. Next, try including a prediction based on your data trends."

**4. Conference-Based Assessment:**
- Brief individual conferences (3-5 minutes) during garden work time
- "Tell me about what you're working on. What was challenging? What strategy did you use?"
- Record key observations and plan targeted instruction

#### Performance Tasks (Monthly)

Performance tasks are the cornerstone of 3-5 assessment. They require students to apply multiple skills to authentic, garden-based problems and communicate their thinking clearly.

**Example Performance Task: The Garden Redesign (4th Grade, mid-year)**

*Context:* "Our school has been given a 20-foot by 30-foot plot for a new garden. The garden committee (your class) must design the layout, calculate all measurements, and present the proposal to the school board."

*Task Requirements:*
1. Design a garden layout on a coordinate grid (scale: 1 unit = 2 feet)
2. Include at least 4 rectangular garden beds with calculated area and perimeter
3. Include pathways wide enough for a wheelchair (at least 4 feet)
4. Calculate the total planting area and the total pathway area
5. Create a budget using provided price list for soil, seeds, and materials
6. Write a persuasive letter to the school board explaining your design choices
7. Complete a TRACE journal documenting your reasoning process

*Performance Task Rubric:*

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Distinguished (4) |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Mathematical Accuracy** | Multiple errors in area, perimeter, or budget calculations | Some errors; self-corrects when prompted | Accurate calculations throughout with appropriate units | Accurate with verification; identifies most efficient use of space |
| **Design Quality** | Layout is impractical or incomplete | Layout is functional but doesn't optimize space | Layout is practical, accessible, and well-organized | Layout demonstrates creative problem-solving and consideration of multiple factors (sun, water access, companion planting) |
| **Communication** | Minimal explanation of choices | Some reasoning provided but incomplete | Clear, organized explanation with evidence from calculations | Compelling, well-structured argument that anticipates questions and addresses multiple stakeholder perspectives |
| **TRACE Protocol** | Minimal TRACE documentation | Partial TRACE entries; some steps missing | Complete TRACE entries showing thinking process | TRACE entries demonstrate depth, self-correction, and genuine metacognition |
| **Mathematical Reasoning** | Uses one strategy with limited explanation | Uses a strategy with partial explanation | Uses multiple strategies and explains reasoning clearly | Demonstrates sophisticated reasoning; compares approaches; generalizes findings |
| **Collaboration** (if group task) | Limited contribution to group | Contributes but doesn't engage with others' ideas | Active contributor who builds on others' ideas | Facilitates productive collaboration; ensures all voices are heard |

### Portfolio Development

#### Portfolio Structure

Each student maintains a year-long garden learning portfolio organized into five sections aligned with the 5Rs:

**Section 1: Root (Who I Am as a Learner)**
- Regulation thermometer data summary (trends over time)
- "Root System Map" (strengths, values, identity -- updated quarterly)
- Learning style reflections
- Personal regulation toolkit card

**Section 2: Regulate (How I Manage My Learning)**
- Regulation strategy effectiveness reflections
- Examples of productive struggle documentation
- Self-management goal tracking sheets
- Evidence of regulation partnership contributions

**Section 3: Reflect (What I Know and Can Do)**
- Selected work samples from each academic domain (minimum 2 per subject per trimester)
- Investigation journals with teacher feedback
- Performance task responses with rubrics
- TRACE journal entries demonstrating growth in thinking
- Best examples of each reasoning move in use

**Section 4: Restore (How I Learn from Challenges)**
- Error analysis examples showing Root Cause Analysis
- Growth log entries documenting challenges overcome
- Revised work showing improvement from first draft to final version
- Reflections on setbacks in the garden and what was learned

**Section 5: Reconnect (How I Contribute to My Community)**
- Community project documentation (photos, reflections, impact data)
- Evidence of peer mentoring or teaching (K-2 mentoring reflections)
- Family engagement artifacts (take-home activities completed together)
- "Seed of Thought" collection (ideas carried beyond the classroom)

## Culminating Projects

### Project 1: Garden Business Plan (Mathematics Focus)

**Overview:** Students work in teams to develop a comprehensive business plan for a school garden produce stand, integrating multiplication, fractions, decimals, measurement, data analysis, and persuasive communication. (See Part 2 for detailed mathematics content.)

**Project Components:**
1. Market research: survey the school community about preferred vegetables and willingness to pay
2. Garden layout design with area calculations and crop selection rationale
3. Start-up budget with itemized costs and total investment
4. Revenue projection with realistic yield estimates and loss adjustments
5. Profit analysis and break-even calculation
6. Persuasive presentation to the school administration requesting funding
7. TRACE journal documenting the entire process

**Assessment:** Performance rubric (see Part 2) plus portfolio documentation and team self-evaluation.

### Project 2: Ecosystem Research Report (Science + Literacy Focus)

**Overview:** Students conduct an in-depth investigation of the school garden ecosystem and produce a research report that demonstrates scientific understanding, informational writing skills, and data analysis competence.

**Project Sequence (3-4 weeks):**

**Week 1: Investigation Design**
- Students select a focus question:
  - "What is the relationship between soil health and plant growth in our garden?"
  - "How does biodiversity in our garden compare to a nearby natural area?"
  - "What is the effect of companion planting on pest populations?"
  - "How does mulching affect soil moisture and temperature?"
- Design investigation protocol: hypothesis, variables, data collection plan, timeline
- TRACE: Think (what do I know?), Reason (what's my plan?)

**Week 2: Data Collection**
- Execute investigation in the garden
- Collect quantitative and qualitative data
- Organize data in tables and create appropriate graphs
- Photograph and document observations
- TRACE: Check (is my data reliable? Do I need more?)

**Week 3: Analysis and Writing**
- Analyze data: identify patterns, draw conclusions, evaluate hypothesis
- Draft the research report following the structure:
  - Introduction: Background information and research question
  - Methods: Detailed description of investigation procedure
  - Results: Data tables, graphs, and descriptions of findings
  - Discussion: Interpretation of results, connection to prior knowledge, limitations
  - Conclusion: Summary of findings and implications for garden management
  - Bibliography: Proper citation of sources
- TRACE: Articulate (how do I explain my findings clearly?)

**Week 4: Revision and Presentation**
- Peer review using "Stars and Wishes" protocol
- Revise based on feedback
- Prepare a 5-minute presentation for the class science symposium
- Families invited to attend the symposium
- TRACE: Extend (what new questions does my research raise?)

**Assessment Rubric for Ecosystem Report:**

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Distinguished (4) |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Research Question** | Vague or unresearchable | Clear but not fully testable | Clear, specific, testable | Original, insightful, and connected to broader ecological concepts |
| **Investigation Design** | Missing key elements (control, variables) | Includes most elements; some gaps | Well-designed with clear variables, controls, and procedure | Sophisticated design that anticipates and addresses potential confounding variables |
| **Data Collection** | Incomplete or inaccurate data | Adequate data; some gaps or inconsistencies | Thorough, accurate data with appropriate precision | Exemplary data collection with multiple measures and quality checks |
| **Data Analysis** | Minimal analysis; no graphs | Some analysis; basic graphs | Thorough analysis with appropriate graphs and clear interpretation | Sophisticated analysis that identifies complex patterns, uses statistics, and acknowledges limitations |
| **Written Report** | Disorganized; missing sections | Most sections present; some organizational issues | Well-organized report with all sections; clear writing | Exceptionally clear, well-structured report with precise vocabulary and compelling narrative |
| **Evidence-Based Reasoning** | Claims without evidence | Claims with some evidence | Claims fully supported by evidence; reasoning is clear | Claims supported by multiple types of evidence; alternative explanations considered and addressed |
| **TRACE Documentation** | Minimal documentation | Some TRACE entries | Complete TRACE journal showing thinking process | TRACE journal demonstrates genuine metacognition, self-correction, and intellectual growth |

### Project 3: Garden Recipe Book with Math (Cross-Curricular Focus)

**Overview:** Students create a class recipe book using garden produce, with each recipe including mathematical calculations, nutritional analysis, cultural context, and personal reflection.

**Each Student/Pair Contributes:**
1. **Recipe page:** Clear procedural writing with measurements
2. **Scaling page:** The recipe scaled for 2, 4, 8, and 24 servings (demonstrating fraction and multiplication skills)
3. **Nutritional analysis page:** Basic nutritional information for key ingredients, calculated per serving
4. **Cultural connection page:** Research on the cultural origins of the dish or its key ingredients
5. **Garden connection page:** Which ingredients came from the garden; growing information; seasonal availability
6. **Personal reflection:** Why this recipe matters to them; what they learned through making it

**Math Integration:** Each recipe includes at least three different fraction operations, unit conversions, and cost calculations.

**Assessment:** Rubric evaluating mathematical accuracy, writing quality, research depth, and presentation quality.

## Community Connections

### Farmers Market Math

**Project Description:** Students operate a garden produce stand at the school entrance or local farmers market (with adult supervision and parent volunteer support).

**Mathematical Applications:**
- Calculate pricing based on production costs and desired profit margin
- Make change accurately using mental math strategies
- Track inventory: starting quantity, quantity sold, remaining quantity
- Record daily revenue and calculate weekly/monthly totals
- Create graphs showing sales trends over time
- Calculate profit: revenue - costs = profit

**SEL Applications:**
- Customer service skills: greeting, eye contact, answering questions
- Handling disappointment (slow sales days)
- Making change accurately under the pressure of real transactions
- Collaborative decision-making about pricing and product selection
- Pride in selling something they grew themselves

**Literacy Applications:**
- Creating marketing materials (signs, flyers, social media posts for school accounts)
- Writing product descriptions
- Designing attractive displays with informational labels
- Recording transactions in a ledger

### Community Garden Partnerships

**Building Relationships with Local Gardens:**

| Partnership Activity | Student Role | Community Partner Role | Academic Connection |
|---------------------|-------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Joint planting days | Students work alongside community members | Community gardeners share expertise and stories | Oral history interviews (literacy); measurement of shared beds (math) |
| Knowledge exchange visits | Students teach garden science to community gardeners | Community members share cultural growing practices | Persuasive speaking (literacy); cultural research (social studies) |
| Shared harvest distribution | Students help organize and distribute produce to food-insecure community members | Food bank provides logistics and recipient data | Data analysis of distribution (math); understanding food systems (social studies) |
| Garden design consulting | Students apply geometry and measurement skills to help design community garden beds | Community provides real-world design challenges | Area, perimeter, and volume (math); engineering design (science) |
| Seasonal celebrations | Students plan and lead garden celebration events | Community members attend and participate | Event planning (math: budgeting; literacy: invitations and programs) |

### Food Bank Donations

**Growing for Others Program:**

Students designate a portion of the garden specifically for food bank donations:

1. **Planning Phase:** Research which crops are most needed by the local food bank (typically fresh vegetables are in highest demand). Calculate how much can be grown in the designated area. Contact the food bank to confirm needs and delivery logistics.

2. **Growing Phase:** Students maintain the donation beds with the same care as all garden beds. Track growth data alongside other garden data. Calculate projected yield.

3. **Harvest and Delivery Phase:** Weigh and record all donated produce. Create labels with the school name and growing information. Deliver to the food bank (field trip or partnership with a parent volunteer).

4. **Impact Analysis:**
   - "We donated ___ pounds of produce this season"
   - "At grocery store prices, our donation was worth approximately $___"
   - "Our donation provided approximately ___ servings of vegetables"
   - Graph donations by week and by crop
   - Write a summary report to share with the school community

5. **Reflection:**
   - "Why does food insecurity exist in our community?"
   - "How does our garden address one small part of this problem?"
   - "What more could we do?"
   - Connect to SEL: empathy, responsible decision-making, community contribution

### Family Participation Opportunities

**Tier 1: Low Barrier (anytime, from home):**
- Complete the "Ask Your Child" conversation and respond (written, audio, or photo)
- Share a family recipe using garden vegetables
- Send in recyclable materials for garden projects (containers, newspapers for composting)
- Read a garden-themed book together and return a response card
- Practice math facts using garden contexts from the weekly update

**Tier 2: Moderate Involvement:**
- Attend quarterly Family Garden Days (Saturday morning, 2 hours)
- Volunteer for garden maintenance during school breaks
- Share cultural gardening knowledge with the class (in person or video)
- Attend student-led portfolio conferences (twice yearly, with flexible scheduling)
- Participate in the produce stand or harvest festival

**Tier 3: High Involvement:**
- Join the Garden Advisory Committee (meets quarterly, 1 hour)
- Lead a cooking demonstration or cultural food activity
- Mentor student garden teams during weekly sessions
- Help coordinate community partnerships and donations
- Assist with grant writing or fundraising for garden expansion

### Trauma-Informed Family Engagement

- Never require family participation for student assessment or grades
- Provide multiple ways to participate (in-person, at home, digitally, in native language)
- Acknowledge that some families have had negative school experiences; build trust gradually
- Start every family interaction with a strength: "Your child showed incredible persistence this week when..."
- Offer food, childcare, and flexible scheduling at all in-person events
- Ensure all communications are available in the most common home languages
- Follow up personally (phone call, not just a note) with families who do not respond to written communication
- Respect diverse family structures without assumption; use "family" rather than "parents"
- Create welcoming physical spaces: clean, well-lit, with comfortable seating and student work displayed

## RootGuide AI Tutor: 3-5 Adaptation

### Interaction Design for Ages 8-11

The RootGuide AI tutor adapts its behavior for 3-5 students in the following ways compared to K-2:

**Language Level:**
- Medium-complexity sentences (10-20 words average)
- Academic vocabulary used with garden-context definitions
- Multi-part questions introduced (maximum 2 parts)
- Explanations include reasoning, not just procedures
- Garden metaphors used to illustrate abstract concepts

**Pacing:**
- Moderate wait times (8-12 seconds for responses)
- Multi-step problems presented one step at a time with check-ins
- Sessions: 15-20 minutes for 3rd grade, 20-30 minutes for 4th-5th
- Student-controlled pacing: "Ready for the next challenge, or want to review?"

**Scaffolding:**
- Problems begin with garden context but build toward abstraction
- Students are prompted to select their own strategies: "What approach will you use?"
- Drawing and modeling encouraged as first steps before symbolic representation
- Error responses include guided self-correction: "Let's trace back to where the thinking shifted. Look at step 2 again -- does that result make sense?"
- Reasoning moves are explicitly named: "You just used the Compare reasoning move! Nice work."

**TRACE Protocol Integration:**
- RootGuide guides students through TRACE steps for each problem
- Students are gradually given more autonomy in TRACE application
- By 5th grade, students initiate TRACE steps independently with RootGuide confirming

**Regulation Monitoring:**
- Monitors response patterns for signs of frustration (rapid incorrect answers, delayed responses, session abandonment)
- Proactive check-ins: "How is your regulation thermometer right now? Green zone? Yellow? Red?"
- Offers regulation breaks: "It sounds like that was a tough one. Want to take a garden breathing break before we continue?"
- Adjusts difficulty based on emotional state (not just accuracy)

### Sample RootGuide Interaction (4th Grade Math -- Fractions)

```
RootGuide: Welcome back, garden mathematician! Before we start,
           check your regulation thermometer. What zone are you in?

Student:   Yellow -- kind of hyper today

RootGuide: Thanks for noticing that! Being in the yellow zone is
           normal -- it just means you might want to take a couple
           of deep breaths to move toward green. Want to try box
           breathing together? Or would you rather jump into a
           problem and see how it goes?

Student:   Let's just start

RootGuide: OK! Here's our garden challenge:

           THINK: Our garden bed is divided into 8 equal sections.
           3 sections have tomatoes and 2 sections have herbs.
           What fraction of the bed has plants?
           What fraction is empty?

           What do you notice about this problem?

Student:   I need to add the tomato and herb sections

RootGuide: Great observation! You're using the DECOMPOSE move --
           breaking the problem into parts. What's your plan?

           REASON: How will you find the total planted fraction?

Student:   3/8 + 2/8 = 5/8

RootGuide: Excellent! You added the numerators because the
           denominators are the same. Now...

           ARTICULATE: Can you explain what the 5/8 means in
           the context of our garden bed?

Student:   5 out of 8 sections have plants in them

RootGuide: Perfect explanation. Now for the second part:
           What fraction is empty?

Student:   3/8

RootGuide: CHECK: Let's verify. If 5/8 is planted, and the whole
           bed is 8/8, then 8/8 - 5/8 = 3/8 empty. Your answer
           checks out!

           EXTEND: Here's a challenge -- if we want to plant
           herbs in HALF of the empty sections, how many more
           sections get herbs? And what fraction of the total
           bed would then have herbs?

Student:   Half of 3 is... 1.5? But you can't plant half a section

RootGuide: Interesting! You've discovered something important.
           3 doesn't divide evenly by 2. In the garden, we
           can't split a section in half easily.

           What if I asked it differently: 1/2 of 3/8 is what?
           Can you multiply 1/2 x 3/8?

Student:   1 x 3 = 3 and 2 x 8 = 16. So 3/16?

RootGuide: That's the mathematical answer -- 3/16 of the total bed.
           But you raised a great practical point: in a real garden,
           we might not be able to split sections into 16ths!

           This is why math AND practical thinking both matter in
           gardening. Your brain is growing like our tomato plants!

           Ready for another challenge, or want to review what
           we just did?
```

### Educator Dashboard: 3-5 Metrics

The educator dashboard for 3-5 students provides comprehensive data visualization:

| Metric | Display | Purpose |
|--------|---------|---------|
| **Regulation trend** | Line graph showing daily thermometer data over time | Track emotional regulation patterns; identify cyclical stressors |
| **TRACE engagement** | Heat map showing depth of engagement with each TRACE step | Identify which protocol steps need more instruction |
| **Reasoning moves profile** | Radar chart showing frequency of each reasoning move | Identify student strengths and gaps in reasoning repertoire |
| **Skill progression** | Standards-aligned checklist with evidence trail | Track academic growth toward grade-level standards |
| **Error pattern analysis** | Categorized list with frequency and misconception identification | Inform targeted instruction; identify common class-wide misconceptions |
| **Growth trajectory** | Line graph comparing current performance to beginning-of-year diagnostic | Celebrate and communicate growth; identify students not making expected progress |
| **Session engagement** | Duration, completion rate, self-selected difficulty | Monitor motivation and stamina; detect disengagement |
| **Self-assessment alignment** | Comparison of student self-assessment to teacher assessment | Identify students who over- or under-estimate their abilities; inform metacognitive instruction |
| **IEP accommodation tracking** | Log of accommodations used and their effectiveness | Document for IEP team meetings; adjust accommodation plans |
| **Family communication** | Outreach log, response tracking, conference notes | Monitor family engagement; identify families needing additional connection |

### Data-Driven Instructional Decisions

**Weekly Data Review Protocol (15 minutes):**
1. Scan regulation trend data for any students showing persistent blue or red zone patterns
2. Review error pattern analysis to identify instructional priorities for the coming week
3. Check TRACE engagement data to identify students who need additional scaffolding
4. Note students who are consistently exceeding grade-level expectations for enrichment planning
5. Update flexible groups based on current data

**Monthly Data Review Protocol (30 minutes):**
1. Compare growth trajectories to expected progress
2. Analyze reasoning move profiles to plan targeted instruction
3. Review self-assessment alignment data to plan metacognitive lessons
4. Prepare portfolio conference talking points for upcoming family meetings
5. Identify students who may need referral for additional evaluation or support

### The Garden Showcase

An end-of-year celebration that integrates academic presentation, community connection, and growth recognition:

**Event Structure (2.5 hours):**

| Station | Description | Student Role | Academic Domains |
|---------|-------------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Portfolio Gallery** | Student portfolios displayed with guided viewing path | Students present selected work to visitors, explaining their growth and reasoning | All domains |
| **Garden Tour** | Guided walking tour of the school garden | Student docents explain garden design decisions, scientific investigations, and ecosystem features | Science, math, speaking |
| **Data Wall Presentation** | Large-format display of year-long garden data | Students interpret graphs and charts for visitors | Math, science, literacy |
| **Recipe Book Display and Tasting** | Published class recipe books with samples of garden produce dishes | Students serve food and explain the math behind recipe scaling | Math, literacy, cultural studies |
| **Ecosystem Report Symposium** | Research reports displayed as conference-style posters | Students present findings and answer questions | Science, literacy |
| **Business Plan Pitch** | Garden business plans presented as entrepreneurial pitches | Teams present revenue projections and design rationale | Math, speaking, persuasive writing |
| **Community Impact Display** | Documentation of food bank donations, community partnerships, and outreach activities | Students share impact data and personal reflections | Math, social studies, SEL |
| **Regulation Toolkit Demonstration** | Interactive stations where visitors try regulation strategies | Students teach box breathing, grounding exercises, and mindful observation to visitors | SEL |
| **Seed Library** | Seeds saved from the school garden, packaged and labeled by students | Students distribute seeds and explain growing instructions | Science, literacy |

**Inclusion Practices:**
- Invite all family configurations without assumption
- Provide food accommodating common dietary restrictions (information gathered in advance)
- Offer a virtual tour option for families unable to attend in person
- Translate key signage into the most common home languages represented in the school
- Ensure full physical accessibility for all stations
- Provide a quiet room for families or students who become overwhelmed
- Schedule multiple time slots to accommodate diverse work schedules
- Celebrate effort, growth, and contribution -- not just achievement

### Growth Recognition

Students receive a "Garden Growth Certificate" documenting:
- Academic skills mastered during the year
- Reasoning moves demonstrated with proficiency
- TRACE protocol milestones
- Community contributions (pounds of produce donated, hours of community service, younger students mentored)
- Personal regulation growth (described qualitatively, not numerically)
- A personalized teacher narrative highlighting the student's unique growth and contributions

The certificate concludes with: "Like every plant in our garden, you grew in your own way, at your own pace, with your own unique beauty. Your roots are strong. Keep growing."
