# 6-8 Reconnect Phase: Community Application Projects

## Overview

The Reconnect phase for grades 6-8 addresses the essential question: **"How does this connect to my world?"** At this developmental stage, students are ready to apply their learning beyond the classroom through community-connected projects. The Reconnect phase transforms garden-based learning into food justice awareness, enterprise skills, cross-curricular synthesis, and mentoring younger students. This is where academic knowledge becomes purpose-driven action, and where students see themselves as contributors to their community rather than passive recipients of instruction.

**Duration:** 5-10 minutes per session (closing); extended project time as scheduled
**Core Principle:** Learning is not complete until it is connected -- to self, to community, to the world, and to the future.

---

## Community-Connected Projects

### Project Design Framework

Every Reconnect project follows the GROW structure, connecting garden knowledge to community impact.

| GROW Step | Question | Student Action | Reasoning Move |
|-----------|----------|---------------|---------------|
| **G - Ground** | What did we learn? | Synthesize learning from the TRACE cycle | GENERALIZE, CONNECT |
| **R - Relate** | Who else needs to know this? | Identify a community audience or need | QUESTION |
| **O - Organize** | How will we share it? | Plan a project, product, or presentation | DECOMPOSE, TRANSFORM |
| **W - Work** | How do we make it happen? | Execute the project with quality and purpose | VERIFY, COMPARE |

### Project Menu for Grades 6-8

| Project Type | Description | Duration | Community Connection | Standards |
|-------------|-------------|----------|---------------------|-----------|
| Garden Guide for Families | Create a multilingual planting guide for families in the school community | 3-4 weeks | Distributed at family nights and community centers | CCSS.W.6-8.2, CCSS.W.6-8.4 |
| Neighborhood Soil Map | Test and map soil quality in the school neighborhood; present findings | 4-6 weeks | Shared with community garden organizations | NGSS MS-ESS3-3, MGSE6.SP.1-5 |
| Farm Stand Business Plan | Design and operate a school garden farm stand with pricing, marketing, and accounting | Ongoing | Weekly or monthly sales to school community | MGSE7.RP.1-3, MGSE7.EE.3 |
| Food Desert Research Project | Investigate food access in the school neighborhood; present data-driven findings | 4-5 weeks | Presented to school board or community leaders | CCSS.W.6-8.1, MGSE8.SP.1-4 |
| Garden Mentoring Program | 6-8 students teach garden skills and regulation strategies to K-2 students | Ongoing | Cross-grade partnership within school | CCSS.SL.6-8.4, CASEL relationship skills |
| Cooking and Nutrition Series | Develop and teach healthy cooking lessons using garden produce | 3-4 weeks | Family cooking nights; recipe booklets | CCSS.W.6-8.2, GA Health standards |

## Food Justice Connections

### Connecting Garden Work to Systemic Understanding

Middle school students are developmentally ready to engage with issues of equity and justice. The garden provides a concrete entry point into food systems thinking.

**Key Food Justice Concepts:**

| Concept | Definition | Garden Connection | Discussion Prompt |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|------------------|
| Food desert | An area with limited access to affordable, nutritious food | "Why does our school have a garden? What would our community look like without it?" | QUESTION |
| Food sovereignty | The right of communities to define their own food systems | "Who decides what grows in our garden? Who should decide what food is available in our neighborhood?" | QUESTION, CONNECT |
| Seed saving | Preserving heirloom and culturally significant plant varieties | "What stories do seeds carry? Whose ancestors grew these same varieties?" | CONNECT, GENERALIZE |
| Environmental racism | Disproportionate environmental burdens on communities of color | "Why is soil quality different in different neighborhoods? Is that an accident?" | QUESTION, COMPARE |
| Agricultural labor | The people who grow our food and the conditions they work in | "Who grows the food at the grocery store? How does their experience compare to ours in the garden?" | COMPARE, CONNECT |

### Educator Language Models for Food Justice

- "The garden is not just about science and math. It is about power. Who has access to healthy food and who does not? That is a question worth investigating."
- "When we grow food, we are participating in a tradition that goes back thousands of years across every culture. We are also making a statement about our right to feed ourselves and our community."
- "Food justice does not mean everyone gets the same thing. It means everyone gets what they need. Sound familiar? That is how we run our classroom too."

### Sample Food Justice Investigation

**Project: Mapping Food Access in Our Neighborhood**

| TRACE Step | Application | Reasoning Move |
|-----------|------------|---------------|
| Think | "What do we already know about where people in our neighborhood buy food?" | CONNECT |
| Reason | Students collect data: distance to nearest grocery store, fast food restaurants vs. fresh food options, price comparisons, transportation access | DECOMPOSE, COMPARE, TRANSFORM |
| Articulate | Students create data visualizations (maps, charts, graphs) and write analysis | GENERALIZE |
| Check | Students verify data accuracy; compare findings to published food access data | VERIFY |
| Extend | "What could change this? What role can our garden play? Who needs to hear these findings?" | QUESTION, CONNECT |

## Garden Enterprise: The Farm Stand

### Farm Stand Math

Operating a school garden farm stand integrates multiple math standards in an authentic business context.

**Core Math Applications:**

| Business Task | Math Content | Grade Level | Standards |
|--------------|-------------|-------------|-----------|
| Pricing produce | Unit rates, markup percentages, profit margins | 6-7 | MGSE6.RP.3, MGSE7.RP.3 |
| Tracking inventory | Data collection, tables, frequency | 6 | MGSE6.SP.1-5 |
| Calculating revenue and expenses | Operations with decimals, positive/negative numbers | 6-7 | MGSE6.NS.3, MGSE7.NS.1-3 |
| Projecting future sales | Linear relationships, graphing, prediction | 8 | MGSE8.F.1-4, MGSE8.SP.1-3 |
| Making change | Decimal operations, mental math | 6 | MGSE6.NS.3 |
| Scaling recipes for value-added products | Ratios, proportions, measurement conversion | 6-7 | MGSE6.RP.1-3, MGSE7.RP.1 |
| Analyzing which crops are most profitable | Data analysis, cost-benefit comparison | 7-8 | MGSE7.SP.1-4, MGSE8.SP.1-4 |

### Farm Stand Marketing

Students design marketing materials that integrate ELA standards with business communication.

**Marketing Tasks:**

| Task | ELA Connection | Product |
|------|---------------|---------|
| Write product descriptions | Informational writing, precise language | Signage, labels, social media posts |
| Create a farm stand name and logo | Audience awareness, persuasive appeal | Brand identity materials |
| Design a flyer for the school community | Layout, visual communication, persuasive writing | Printed and digital flyers |
| Write a pitch for the principal or school board | Argumentative writing, oral presentation | Formal proposal document and presentation |
| Document the farm stand story | Narrative writing, documentation | Blog posts, photo essays, newsletter articles |

### Educator Language Models for Enterprise

- "This is real math for a real business. The numbers matter because they determine whether we can buy seeds for next season."
- "You are not just students right now. You are entrepreneurs, accountants, marketers, and community food providers. Those are real roles with real impact."
- "When you make a pricing error at the farm stand, that is a computational error with real consequences -- and a real learning opportunity."

## Presentation Skills

### Building Public Speaking Through Garden Presentations

| Skill | Scaffold (first presentations) | Independent (later presentations) | Standards |
|-------|-------------------------------|----------------------------------|-----------|
| Organization | Provide presentation template with sections | Students create their own structure | CCSS.SL.6-8.4 |
| Evidence use | Highlight which data points to include | Students select and justify evidence choices | CCSS.SL.6-8.2 |
| Visual aids | Provide slide templates with data display options | Students design original visual supports | CCSS.SL.6-8.5 |
| Delivery | Practice in small groups first; use note cards | Present to larger audiences; reduce reliance on notes | CCSS.SL.6-8.4, 6 |
| Q&A response | Pre-generate possible questions and rehearse answers | Respond to unrehearsed questions with evidence | CCSS.SL.6-8.1c, 1d |

### Presentation Venues (Graduated Audience)

1. **Classroom partner** -- lowest stakes; practice articulation
2. **Small group (4-6 students)** -- build confidence with peer feedback
3. **Whole class** -- standard classroom presentation
4. **Cross-grade audience** -- present to younger students during mentoring
5. **Family night or community event** -- authentic audience; real stakes
6. **School board, community organization, or public forum** -- highest impact; professional context

## Digital Portfolios

### Portfolio Structure for Grades 6-8

Students maintain digital portfolios that document their growth across all 5Rs phases throughout the year.

| Portfolio Section | Content | 5Rs Connection | What It Shows |
|------------------|---------|---------------|---------------|
| About Me | Regulation scale history, growth goals, personal garden philosophy | Root, Regulate | Self-awareness development over time |
| My Learning Journey | Selected TRACE examples with reasoning move annotations | Reflect | Academic growth and metacognitive development |
| Error Analysis Showcase | 2-3 significant errors with full analysis protocol | Restore | Ability to learn from mistakes; growth mindset evidence |
| Community Impact | Project documentation, photos, data, reflections | Reconnect | Real-world application and community contribution |
| Growth Reflection | End-of-semester narrative reflecting on change and growth | All 5Rs | Synthesis and self-assessment |

### Educator Language Models for Portfolios

- "Your portfolio is the story of your growth. It is not a collection of your best work -- it is a collection of your most important learning moments, including the hard ones."
- "When you look back at your portfolio in June, you should be able to see a different version of yourself from September. That difference is growth."
- "This portfolio is yours. You choose what goes in it. You decide what story it tells. I will guide you, but you are the author."

## Mentoring Younger Students

### Cross-Grade Garden Mentoring Program

Sixth through eighth graders mentor K-2 or 3-5 students in garden skills and regulation strategies. This deepens their own learning while building community.

**Mentoring Structure:**

| Component | Frequency | Duration | Mentor Responsibility |
|-----------|-----------|----------|----------------------|
| Garden buddy sessions | Biweekly | 30 min | Lead a garden activity; model regulation strategies |
| Teaching a mini-lesson | Monthly | 10-15 min | Teach one concept from TRACE to younger students |
| Shared garden workday | Quarterly | 45-60 min | Plan and lead a collaborative garden work session |
| End-of-year celebration | Annual | 60 min | Co-present garden growth with mentee |

### Mentoring Skill Development

| Skill | How It Develops Through Mentoring | Reasoning Move |
|-------|----------------------------------|---------------|
| Teaching | Explaining a concept to a younger student requires deep understanding | GENERALIZE, DECOMPOSE |
| Patience | Working with younger learners builds frustration tolerance | Regulation skills in action |
| Leadership | Planning and leading activities builds confidence and responsibility | TRANSFORM (adapting content for audience) |
| Empathy | Understanding a younger student's struggle builds perspective-taking | CONNECT, COMPARE |
| Communication | Adjusting language for a younger audience builds communication skill | TRANSFORM |

### Educator Language Models for Mentoring

- "When you teach something to a younger student, you learn it twice. You learn it once for yourself and once to explain it."
- "Your mentee looks up to you. Not because you are perfect, but because you show up, you try, and you keep growing. That is what a mentor does."
- "If your mentee makes an error, use the same language we use in this classroom. Help them classify it. Show them that mistakes are data, not disasters."

## Celebration of Growth

### Recognizing Growth, Not Just Achievement

The Reconnect phase includes intentional celebration that honors effort, growth, and contribution -- not just grades or performance.

**Celebration Formats:**

| Format | Frequency | Focus | Example |
|--------|-----------|-------|---------|
| Growth shout-outs | Weekly | Specific growth moments | "This week, Jaylen moved from needing prompts for TRACE to running it independently." |
| Error victory wall | Ongoing | Best error analyses | Students post their most instructive errors (voluntarily) with full analysis |
| Garden harvest celebration | Seasonal | Community contribution | Share harvest with school community; acknowledge everyone's role in growing the food |
| Portfolio share day | Semester | Individual growth journey | Students present selected portfolio pieces to peers and families |
| Mentor recognition | End of year | Impact on younger students | Mentees share what they learned from their mentor |

### Educator Language Models for Celebration

- "I am not celebrating because you got a perfect score. I am celebrating because you used DECOMPOSE on a problem that would have stumped you in September."
- "Growth is not always dramatic. Sometimes it is quiet. Sometimes it is just showing up when you did not want to and doing the work anyway. That counts."
- "Our garden did not grow overnight. Neither did you. But look at where we are compared to where we started."

## Educator Implementation Notes

- **Community projects must be genuine.** Students know the difference between busywork and real impact. If the farm stand is not actually selling produce, or if the presentations have no real audience, students will disengage. Make the work matter.
- **Food justice conversations require safety.** These topics touch on race, class, and systemic inequality. Establish discussion norms. Center student voices and lived experiences. Do not lecture -- facilitate.
- **Mentoring requires preparation.** Do not send 6-8 students to mentor younger students without explicit training. Teach them how to teach, how to listen, how to respond to a frustrated young learner. Model it first.
- **Digital portfolios are longitudinal.** Start portfolios early in the year and build throughout. Do not treat them as an end-of-year project. The power is in the accumulation over time.
- **Celebrate authentically.** Forced celebrations feel hollow. Let celebrations emerge from genuine growth moments. A quiet acknowledgment can mean more than a loud ceremony.
- **Connect back to Root.** The Reconnect phase closes the loop. End each session by returning briefly to Root: "Check your regulation number. How are you ending today compared to how you started? That is data too."
