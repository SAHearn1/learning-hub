# 6-8 Grade Band: Assessment, Service Learning & Portfolio Development

## Assessment Framework for 6-8

### Assessment Philosophy

Assessment in the 6-8 RootWork curriculum serves three purposes: informing instruction, documenting growth, and developing student self-assessment capacity. Middle school students are at a developmental stage where they can -- and should -- become active agents in their own assessment, understanding where they are, where they are headed, and what steps they need to take to get there. This metacognitive engagement with assessment is itself a powerful learning tool and aligns directly with the TRACE protocol's emphasis on self-monitoring (Check) and self-directed learning (Extend).

For trauma-affected students, traditional assessment practices can be deeply triggering. Timed tests activate fight-or-flight responses. Public grading creates shame. Competitive ranking reinforces hierarchies that feel threatening. The RootWork assessment framework deliberately avoids these practices, replacing them with:

1. **Standards-based grading** that reports what students know and can do, not how they compare to peers
2. **Performance tasks** embedded in authentic garden contexts that demonstrate competence through application
3. **Portfolio development** that makes growth visible and puts students in control of their narrative
4. **Peer assessment protocols** that build collaboration and communication skills while reducing reliance on teacher-as-judge
5. **Socratic seminars** that assess reasoning and argumentation through dialogue rather than written tests
6. **Student self-reflection** as a required component of every assessment event

### Standards-Based Grading

#### Proficiency Scale

The 6-8 RootWork curriculum uses a four-level proficiency scale aligned with Georgia Standards of Excellence:

| Level | Label | Description | Garden Metaphor |
|-------|-------|-------------|-----------------|
| **4** | Extending | Demonstrates understanding beyond the standard; applies learning to novel situations; teaches others; makes connections across disciplines | "The plant that not only grows strong but produces seeds for new growth" |
| **3** | Meeting | Demonstrates understanding of the standard independently; can explain reasoning and apply concepts to grade-level problems | "A healthy plant producing fruit -- this is what we're growing toward" |
| **2** | Approaching | Demonstrates partial understanding; can perform with scaffolding or support; shows key concepts but with errors or gaps | "A growing plant that needs more sunlight or water to reach full potential" |
| **1** | Beginning | Demonstrates minimal understanding of the standard; requires significant support; foundational skills are developing | "A newly planted seed -- the potential is there, but growth requires time and care" |

**Critical Principles:**
- A student's grade reflects their most consistent, recent level of performance -- not an average of all attempts. A student who struggled in September but meets the standard in December earns a "3," because the purpose of assessment is to capture what they know now, not to punish them for the learning process.
- A "4" is not about doing more work -- it is about demonstrating deeper understanding. Extending means applying knowledge to new contexts, making unexpected connections, or teaching others effectively.
- All students can reach "3" given appropriate time, support, and instruction. The curriculum is designed to ensure this.
- Grades are separated into academic achievement and learning behaviors (effort, participation, collaboration). A student who works hard but has not yet mastered the standard receives recognition for their effort without inflating their achievement level.

#### Standards Tracking Document (Student-Facing)

Students maintain a standards tracker in their garden journals, updated after each assessment event:

| Standard | Description | Assessment 1 | Assessment 2 | Assessment 3 | Current Level | My Goal |
|----------|-------------|-------------|-------------|-------------|--------------|---------|
| 7.RP.2 | Proportional relationships | 2 | 2 | 3 | 3 | Maintain 3; extend to new contexts |
| 7.EE.4 | Solve equations/inequalities | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 | Reach 3 by next assessment |
| 7.G.4 | Area and circumference of circles | - | 2 | 3 | 3 | Apply to garden design project |
| MS-LS1-6 | Photosynthesis | - | - | 3 | 3 | Extend to ecosystem-level application |
| W.7.1 | Argumentative writing | 2 | 2 | 3 | 3 | Strengthen use of evidence |

"This tracker helps you see your own growth. Look at the pattern -- not the single number. Where are you growing? Where do you need more support? What's your plan?"

### Performance Tasks

Performance tasks are the primary summative assessment tool in the 6-8 RootWork curriculum. Each task is embedded in an authentic garden context, requires the application of multiple skills, and is evaluated using a transparent rubric shared with students in advance.

#### Sample Performance Task: "The Garden Consultant" (7th Grade, End of Unit 1)

**Scenario:** "You have been hired as a garden consultant for a community organization that wants to start a garden on a 20 ft x 30 ft lot. They have a budget of $500 and want to grow vegetables for a food pantry. Your job is to create a complete garden plan."

**Required Components:**

| Component | Skills Assessed | Standards |
|-----------|----------------|-----------|
| **Scale drawing** of the garden layout (1 inch = 3 feet) | Proportional reasoning, scale, geometry | 7.RP.2, 7.G.1 |
| **Planting plan** with crop types, quantities, and spacing | Area calculation, division, ratios | 7.G.4, 7.RP.1 |
| **Budget** with itemized costs and unit rate comparisons from at least 2 suppliers | Equations, unit rates, percent markup | 7.RP.1, 7.EE.3 |
| **Watering schedule** based on crop needs and available water | Proportional reasoning, constraint analysis | 7.RP.2, 7.EE.4 |
| **Yield projection** estimating the total pounds of produce | Ratio reasoning, data analysis using prior garden data | 7.RP.3, 7.SP.1 |
| **Written rationale** (1-2 pages) explaining and justifying all decisions | Argumentative writing, evidence-based reasoning | W.7.1, SL.7.4 |
| **Presentation** (5 minutes) to a panel (teacher, peers, and/or community member) | Oral communication, visual presentation, responding to questions | SL.7.1, SL.7.4 |

**Rubric (abbreviated):**

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Mathematical Accuracy** | Multiple significant errors; calculations incomplete | Some errors; most calculations present but with gaps | All calculations correct and complete | Calculations are correct, efficient, and include analysis of alternatives |
| **Proportional Reasoning** | Does not apply ratios or proportions | Applies ratios with support or with errors | Applies ratios and proportions accurately throughout | Uses proportional reasoning flexibly; identifies and explores proportional relationships beyond what's required |
| **Design Quality** | Scale drawing is incomplete or inaccurate | Scale drawing is present but with significant errors in proportion | Scale drawing is accurate and complete | Scale drawing is precise, aesthetically thoughtful, and includes creative design elements |
| **Written Rationale** | Minimal explanation; no evidence | Some explanation with limited evidence | Clear explanation with evidence supporting each decision | Compelling argument with strong evidence; anticipates counterarguments; considers multiple perspectives |
| **Presentation** | Unable to present or respond to questions | Presents with significant support; limited responses to questions | Presents clearly and responds to most questions | Engages the audience; responds thoughtfully to all questions; demonstrates deep understanding |

**Differentiation:**

| Level | Modification |
|-------|-------------|
| **Approaching** | Provide a partially completed template; reduce to 3 required components; allow extended time; pair with a peer for the presentation |
| **On Level** | Complete all components as described; present individually |
| **Advanced** | Add constraints (irregular lot shape, accessibility requirements, organic certification); present to an authentic community audience; include a sustainability analysis |

#### Sample Performance Task: "The Ecosystem Health Report" (8th Grade, End of Ecology Unit)

**Scenario:** "The school board has asked for a scientific report on the health of the school garden ecosystem. Your team will conduct a comprehensive assessment and present your findings with data-supported recommendations."

**Required Components:**
1. Biodiversity survey with Simpson's Diversity Index calculation
2. Soil analysis (pH, N-P-K, organic matter, texture)
3. Water quality testing (if applicable) or water usage analysis
4. Food web model with at least 12 organisms
5. Sustainability assessment using a rubric the team develops
6. Data visualizations (at least 3: scatter plot, box plot, and one student-selected graph type)
7. Written report (3-5 pages) with introduction, methods, results, discussion, and recommendations
8. Oral presentation with visual aids

**Assessment:** Evaluated on scientific accuracy, data analysis quality, strength of evidence-based arguments, quality of communication, and collaborative process (peer and self-evaluation of teamwork).

### Socratic Seminars

Socratic seminars provide a discussion-based assessment format that values reasoning, evidence use, and respectful intellectual engagement. They are particularly powerful for trauma-affected students because they distribute the cognitive load (students build on each other's ideas rather than performing solo) and because they reward thinking processes rather than memorized answers.

#### Socratic Seminar Protocol for 6-8

**Before the Seminar:**
- Students receive the discussion text or data set 2-3 days in advance
- Students prepare by annotating the text and writing at least 3 questions and 3 claims with evidence
- Students review the Socratic Seminar norms (posted prominently):
  - "Support your claims with evidence from the text or data"
  - "Build on others' ideas: 'Adding to what ___ said...'"
  - "Respectfully challenge: 'I see it differently because...'"
  - "Invite others in: 'What do you think, ___?'"
  - "Monitor your air time: speak enough to contribute, not so much that you dominate"

**During the Seminar:**
- Inner circle (8-10 students) discusses while outer circle observes and takes notes
- Teacher facilitates with open-ended questions but does not evaluate or correct during the discussion
- After 15-20 minutes, circles switch roles
- Closing whole-group reflection: "What was the strongest argument made today? What question remains unanswered?"

**Assessment:**

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Preparation** | Did not prepare or brought no notes | Minimal preparation; few annotations | Thorough preparation; annotations and questions/claims | Exceptional preparation; deep engagement with text; multiple perspectives considered |
| **Evidence Use** | No evidence cited | Some evidence but weak or vague | Specific evidence from text/data to support claims | Strong, precise evidence; identifies and reconciles conflicting evidence |
| **Reasoning** | Claims without reasoning | Some reasoning but surface-level | Clear reasoning connecting evidence to claims | Sophisticated reasoning; identifies assumptions; considers counterarguments |
| **Engagement** | Did not speak or spoke off-topic | Spoke once or twice with prompting | Contributed multiple times; responded to others | Led key moments; drew others in; advanced the discussion significantly |
| **Respect** | Interrupted or dismissed others | Mostly respectful but inconsistent | Consistently respectful; used academic discourse norms | Modeled respectful disagreement; helped others feel safe to share |

**Sample Socratic Seminar Topics for 6-8:**

| Content Area | Seminar Text/Data | Essential Question |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Math | Garden growth data from class experiment | "Is our garden data strong enough to make recommendations for next year? What would make us more confident?" |
| Science | Competing articles on organic vs. conventional agriculture | "Which farming approach is better for communities and the environment? What evidence is most compelling?" |
| ELA/Social Studies | Excerpt from *Farming While Black* by Leah Penniman | "How does the history of land ownership and agriculture affect food access in communities today?" |
| Interdisciplinary | Student-collected data on food access in the school neighborhood | "Is our community a food desert? What does our data say, and what should be done?" |

### Portfolio Development

#### Portfolio Philosophy

The garden portfolio is the most comprehensive assessment tool in the 6-8 RootWork curriculum. It serves as a living document of student growth across academic, social-emotional, and practical domains. Unlike a folder of completed assignments, the portfolio is curated by the student with educator guidance, includes reflective commentary on each artifact, and tells a story of learning that the student owns and can share with authentic audiences.

**Portfolio Metaphor:** "Your portfolio is like a garden journal -- it documents what you planted, how it grew, what challenges you faced, and what you harvested. It's not just a collection of your best work; it's the story of your growth as a learner and a person."

#### Portfolio Structure

| Section | Contents | Student Reflection Prompt |
|---------|----------|--------------------------|
| **Introduction** | Student-written introduction to themselves as a learner; updated each quarter | "Who am I as a learner? What are my strengths? What am I working on?" |
| **Academic Growth** | 2-3 selected work samples per content area per quarter, representing growth from earliest to most recent | "Why did I choose this piece? What does it show about my growth? What would I do differently now?" |
| **TRACE Reasoning** | Evidence of TRACE protocol engagement: journal entries, problem-solving documentation, reasoning explanations | "Which reasoning move (Decompose, Compare, Question, Verify, Generalize, Transform, Connect) am I strongest in? Which am I developing?" |
| **Error Analysis** | 2-3 error journal entries that demonstrate learning from mistakes | "What was my error? What did I learn? How has this error made me a stronger thinker?" |
| **Garden Documentation** | Photos, data logs, experimental results, garden design work | "What has the garden taught me this quarter? What am I most proud of in our garden?" |
| **SEL Growth** | Quarterly self-assessments, regulation tracker summaries, relationship reflections | "How have I grown socially and emotionally? What regulation strategies work best for me?" |
| **Service Learning** | Evidence of community contribution: project documentation, reflection on impact | "How has our service learning project affected our community? What have I learned about myself through service?" |
| **Goals and Plans** | Current goals, progress tracking, future plans | "What are my goals for next quarter? What specific steps will I take?" |

#### Portfolio Conferences

**Student-Led Portfolio Conferences (Twice Per Year):**

Students present their portfolios to their families and/or a community audience. This is not a parent-teacher conference with the student present -- it is a student-led presentation where the student is the expert on their own learning.

**Preparation (3-4 sessions):**
1. Curate portfolio selections with educator guidance
2. Write reflective commentary for each section
3. Practice the presentation with a peer
4. Prepare responses to anticipated questions
5. Set up a presentation space (can include garden artifacts, data displays, and digital elements)

**Conference Structure (30 minutes per student):**

| Time | Activity | Student Role |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Welcome and introduction | "Welcome to my portfolio presentation. I'm going to share my growth as a learner this semester." |
| 5-15 min | Academic growth presentation | Student walks through selected work, explaining what each piece demonstrates and how they've grown |
| 15-20 min | Garden and service learning showcase | Student shares garden photos, data, and project outcomes |
| 20-25 min | SEL and personal growth reflection | Student shares regulation strategies, relationship growth, and identity development |
| 25-28 min | Goals and future plans | Student shares goals and asks family for input |
| 28-30 min | Questions and celebration | Family asks questions; educator adds brief affirming comments |

**Trauma-Informed Conference Practices:**
- Never require family attendance for student success. Some students have complex family situations -- provide alternative audiences (a mentor, another educator, a community partner).
- Prepare students for the emotional experience of presenting to family. Some students may experience anxiety, pride, shame, or grief. Practice regulation strategies before the conference.
- Provide a quiet space for students or families who become overwhelmed.
- Celebrate every student's growth, regardless of proficiency level. The conference is about growth, not grades.
- Follow up individually with students whose conferences were difficult.

### Peer Assessment Protocols

Peer assessment develops critical evaluation skills, communication competence, and a community of learners who support each other's growth. However, it must be structured carefully to prevent social dynamics from undermining the process.

#### Protocol: "Warm and Cool Feedback"

**Structure:**
1. **Warm feedback** (specific, genuine praise): "One thing I think is really strong about your work is ___ because ___"
2. **Cool feedback** (specific, constructive suggestion): "One thing that might make your work even stronger is ___ because ___"
3. **Question** (genuine curiosity): "I'm wondering about ___. Can you explain your thinking about ___?"

**Rules:**
- Feedback must be specific (not "it's good" but "your use of ratio evidence in paragraph 2 is convincing because...")
- Feedback must reference the rubric criteria
- Both warm and cool feedback are required -- neither can be skipped
- The person receiving feedback listens first, then may ask clarifying questions. No defending or explaining during feedback delivery.
- All feedback is written on a feedback form that is signed by the reviewer. Accountability builds responsibility.

**Scaffolding:**
- Provide sentence stems: "I notice that your [specific element] demonstrates [rubric criterion] because [evidence]"
- Model the process with a sample piece of work (not from any current student) before peers assess each other
- Start with warm feedback only for the first cycle; add cool feedback once students have built trust and skill

## Service Learning Projects

### Philosophy

Service learning in the 6-8 RootWork curriculum is not volunteerism or community service added to the academic program. It is a pedagogical strategy that uses community engagement as a vehicle for academic learning, SEL development, and civic identity formation. Students do not simply help others -- they investigate community needs, apply academic skills to address those needs, and reflect on the experience through the lens of their own growth and the community's well-being.

For trauma-affected students, service learning is particularly powerful because it transforms the narrative from "I am someone who needs help" to "I am someone who can help." This shift in identity -- from recipient to contributor -- can be profoundly healing.

### Project 1: Community Garden Design and Installation

**Duration:** One semester (integrated across subjects)

**Community Partner:** A local community organization, food bank, senior center, or housing development that has identified a need for a garden

**Phase Sequence:**

| Phase | Duration | Activities | Academic Skills Applied |
|-------|----------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Investigation** | 3 weeks | Meet with community partner; survey the site; interview potential users about needs and preferences; research what grows well in the area | Interviewing skills; data collection; reading informational texts; note-taking |
| **Design** | 3 weeks | Create garden design using scale drawings; calculate materials needed; develop a planting plan based on community preferences and growing conditions; create a budget | Proportional reasoning; geometry; expressions and equations; persuasive writing (grant-style proposal) |
| **Implementation** | 4 weeks | Build the garden with community members; plant according to the plan; install signage; document the process | Measurement; construction skills; cooperative work; photography and documentation |
| **Education** | 2 weeks | Create educational materials for community gardeners (planting guides, care calendars, recipe cards); offer a workshop | Informational writing; public speaking; teaching skills |
| **Reflection** | 2 weeks | Write a reflective essay; create a documentary; present to school and community | Reflective writing; narrative construction; presentation skills |

**Assessment:** Standards-based rubric evaluating mathematical accuracy, scientific knowledge application, writing quality, and collaborative skills. Student self-assessment of personal growth through the project. Community partner feedback on the garden's usefulness and the students' professionalism.

### Project 2: Food Justice Research and Advocacy

**Duration:** 6-8 weeks (integrated across ELA, Science, Math, and Social Studies)

**Essential Question:** "Does everyone in our community have equal access to healthy, affordable food? If not, what can we do about it?"

**Phase Sequence:**

| Phase | Activities | TRACE Connection |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Research** | Map food sources within 1 mile of school; classify as grocery, convenience, fast food, garden; analyze nutritional quality and cost; survey community members about food access | Think: What do we know? Question: What needs investigating? |
| **Analysis** | Calculate statistics on food access; create data visualizations; compare neighborhood data to citywide and national data; identify patterns and disparities | Reason: What does the data show? Compare: How does our community compare? |
| **Argumentation** | Write evidence-based arguments about food justice; prepare testimony for a school board or local government meeting; create infographics for community distribution | Articulate: How do we communicate our findings? Generalize: What broader principles apply? |
| **Action** | Propose concrete solutions (expand school garden, advocate for a farmer's market, partner with food bank, create a community cookbook); implement at least one | Extend: How do we apply what we've learned? Transform: How can we change the situation? |
| **Reflection** | Portfolio documentation; personal narrative connecting the project to identity and values; assessment of impact | Check: Did our actions make a difference? Connect: How does this experience shape who I am? |

**Standards Addressed:**
- ELA: W.7.1 (argumentative writing), RI.7.1-9 (reading informational texts), SL.7.1-6 (speaking and listening)
- Math: 7.RP (ratios and proportional relationships), 7.SP (statistics and probability)
- Science: MS-ESS3-3 (human impact on environment), MS-LS2-4 (changes to ecosystems)
- Social Studies: Economics, civics, community geography

### Project 3: Environmental Advocacy Campaign

**Duration:** 4-6 weeks

**Essential Question:** "What environmental issue in our community could our garden help address, and how do we persuade others to take action?"

**Student-Selected Focus Areas:**
- Reducing the school's food waste through composting
- Creating pollinator habitat on school grounds
- Advocating for green infrastructure (rain gardens, bioswales) in the neighborhood
- Starting a seed library for the community
- Developing a plan for a school greenhouse to extend the growing season

**Campaign Requirements:**
1. **Research brief** (2 pages): Evidence-based explanation of the issue and proposed solution
2. **Data collection**: Original data supporting the campaign (surveys, measurements, calculations)
3. **Persuasive materials**: At least two of the following -- a presentation, a video, an infographic, a letter to an official, a social media campaign plan, a petition
4. **Implementation plan**: Timeline, budget, responsibilities, and metrics for success
5. **Presentation to decision-makers**: School administration, school board, community leaders

## RootGuide AI Tutor: 6-8 Adaptation

### Interaction Design for Middle School

The RootGuide AI tutor for 6-8 students employs Socratic questioning, multi-step reasoning chains, and student-directed exploration. It is designed to build independent thinking rather than dependence on the tool.

**Core Principles:**
- Never give answers directly. Guide students toward discovery through questions.
- Match the student's current reasoning level and scaffold upward.
- Use garden contexts for all academic content whenever possible.
- Monitor regulation and offer breaks when signs of frustration or disengagement appear.
- Encourage multiple solution strategies and representation methods.
- Celebrate reasoning process, not just correct answers.

### Socratic Questioning Sequences

**Example: Math -- Proportional Reasoning**

```
RootGuide: Welcome back to the garden lab! How are you today?
           [Regulation check: Blooming / Growing / Wilting]

Student:   Growing

RootGuide: Good to hear. Let's pick up where we left off.

           Our garden produced 24 pounds of tomatoes from 8 plants.
           A community garden wants to match our yield
           but they have 20 plants.

           What's your first thought about how to approach this?

Student:   Multiply 24 by 20?

RootGuide: Interesting start. Let's think about what that would mean.
           24 x 20 = 480 pounds. Does that feel right
           for 20 tomato plants?

Student:   That seems like a lot.

RootGuide: Good instinct. Let's reconsider.
           We got 24 pounds from 8 plants.
           What's the first thing you might want to figure out
           before scaling up?

Student:   How much each plant produced?

RootGuide: Exactly! That's called finding the unit rate.
           How would you calculate that?

Student:   24 divided by 8... 3 pounds per plant.

RootGuide: Perfect. Now if each plant produces about 3 pounds,
           and the community garden has 20 plants...

Student:   3 times 20 = 60 pounds.

RootGuide: 60 pounds. Let's check that. Does 60 pounds from
           20 plants make sense if 8 plants produced 24?
           What's another way to verify?

Student:   I could set up a proportion: 24/8 = x/20

RootGuide: Excellent! You just used two different methods
           -- unit rate AND proportion -- and got the same answer.
           That's the Verify reasoning move.

           Now here's an extension: Would every plant
           really produce exactly 3 pounds?
           What factors might cause variation?

Student:   Some might get more sun, or more water,
           or the soil could be different.

RootGuide: Exactly. That 60 pounds is an estimate based on
           an average. In real gardens, there's always variability.
           That's why we study statistics -- to understand
           and account for that variation.

           Ready for the next challenge, or would you like
           to practice more with unit rates?
```

### Multi-Step Reasoning Chains

**Example: Science -- Ecosystem Analysis**

```
RootGuide: Let's analyze our garden ecosystem data.
           Here's what we observed last week:

           Aphid population: increasing rapidly
           Ladybug population: stable
           Plant health: declining in the pepper bed

           What connections can you make between these
           three observations?

Student:   The aphids are hurting the plants.

RootGuide: Good start. That's one connection.
           Can you be more specific about HOW aphids
           harm plants? Think about what we learned
           about plant physiology.

Student:   They suck the sap from the plants, which has
           the nutrients and water the plant needs.

RootGuide: Yes -- aphids feed on phloem sap, which carries
           the sugars produced by photosynthesis.
           So when aphids feed heavily, the plant loses
           the energy it produced.

           Now, what about the ladybug population?
           What role do ladybugs play in this system?

Student:   Ladybugs eat aphids. They're predators.

RootGuide: Right. So if ladybugs are aphid predators and
           the aphid population is increasing, what does
           the stable ladybug population tell us?

Student:   Maybe there aren't enough ladybugs to control
           the aphids?

RootGuide: That's a strong hypothesis. In ecology, we'd say
           the predator population hasn't caught up with
           the prey population. This is related to the
           concept of carrying capacity.

           Based on your analysis, what would you recommend
           for the garden? Consider at least two options
           and evaluate the tradeoffs.
```

### Student-Directed Exploration Mode

For students who have mastered the core content and want to explore further, RootGuide offers a student-directed mode:

```
RootGuide: You've demonstrated strong understanding of
           proportional reasoning. Would you like to:

           A) Explore a challenge problem connecting proportions
              to our garden data
           B) Investigate a "what if" scenario
              (e.g., "What if we doubled our garden area?")
           C) Design your own garden math problem
              for the class to solve
           D) Explore a career that uses proportional reasoning

Student:   C -- I want to design a problem.

RootGuide: Great! A well-designed problem should:
           1. Be set in a real garden context
           2. Require proportional reasoning to solve
           3. Have at least two possible solution strategies
           4. Include enough information to solve
              (but not so much that it's obvious)

           What garden context would you like to use?
```

### Regulation Monitoring in RootGuide

The AI tutor monitors for signs of dysregulation and responds proactively:

| Signal | Possible Meaning | RootGuide Response |
|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Long pause (>30 seconds) | Confusion, frustration, or distraction | "Take your time. Would a hint help, or do you need a moment?" |
| Rapid incorrect responses | Rushing, anxiety, or disengagement | "Let's slow down. Take a breath. Which part feels tricky?" |
| "I don't know" repeatedly | Frustration, shame, or genuine confusion | "That's okay. Let's go back to what you DO know and build from there." |
| Negative self-talk ("I'm dumb") | Shame, fixed mindset activation | "I hear you're frustrated. Remember: struggling means your brain is growing. Let's try a different approach." |
| Request to quit | Overwhelm, dysregulation | "Let's take a garden break. You can come back anytime. Your progress is saved." |
| Consistently correct, fast responses | Mastery; may need more challenge | "You're making this look easy! Ready for a challenge that might stretch your thinking?" |

## Family Engagement for Adolescents

### The Adolescent Engagement Challenge

Family engagement shifts dramatically in middle school. Students who eagerly brought home garden drawings in elementary school may now balk at family involvement, viewing it as an intrusion on their developing independence. Simultaneously, some families may step back, assuming their middle schooler needs less involvement or feeling unwelcome in the increasingly academic secondary environment.

The RootWork curriculum addresses this by:
1. Making family engagement about the garden and community, not about surveillance of the student
2. Respecting adolescent autonomy while maintaining family connection
3. Providing multiple engagement formats that accommodate diverse family situations
4. Using the garden as a low-stakes, welcoming entry point for families who may have negative associations with school

### Engagement Opportunities

**Low-Barrier (Anytime, Minimal Time Commitment):**
- Receive weekly "Garden Update" emails with photos and a brief summary of learning
- Complete a "family recipe" card using garden-grown ingredients (contributed to the class cookbook)
- Respond to a monthly "ask your family" prompt (e.g., "Ask someone in your family about a garden or farm they remember")
- Follow the school garden's social media or digital portfolio (with appropriate permissions)

**Moderate Involvement:**
- Attend quarterly "Harvest Dinner" events where students cook garden-grown food for families
- Participate in seasonal "Family Garden Work Days" (Saturday mornings, 2 hours)
- Attend student-led portfolio conferences (twice yearly)
- Contribute garden knowledge, cultural food traditions, or professional expertise to the class

**High Involvement:**
- Join the Garden Advisory Committee (meets quarterly, helps plan the garden program)
- Mentor students in garden design, construction, or cooking
- Support service learning projects (community garden installation, food pantry coordination)
- Assist with grant writing or fundraising for garden expansion

### Harvest Dinner Events (Quarterly)

**Format:** Students plan, prepare, and serve a meal made primarily from garden-grown produce to their families and community members.

| Element | Student Role | Academic/SEL Integration |
|---------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Menu planning** | Select recipes based on available harvest; consider dietary restrictions | Reading informational text; measurement; planning |
| **Shopping list and budget** | Calculate quantities needed; compare costs; manage budget | Ratios; multiplication; budgeting |
| **Cooking** | Prepare dishes following recipes with adult supervision | Measurement; sequencing; chemistry of cooking |
| **Table setting and service** | Set up, serve, and clean up | Responsibility; hospitality; organization |
| **Presentation** | Each table has a card explaining what they grew, how they prepared it, and what they learned | Writing; public speaking; pride in accomplishment |
| **Reflection** | Post-dinner journal entry about the experience | Self-awareness; gratitude; community connection |

**Trauma-Informed Family Event Principles:**
- Events are always invitational, never mandatory. A student's grade or standing is never affected by family attendance.
- Provide food for all attendees, not just what students cooked (some families may be food-insecure, and the event should not inadvertently cause embarrassment).
- Welcome all family configurations without assumption or judgment.
- Have quiet spaces available for families or students who become overwhelmed.
- Translate materials and provide interpreters for families with limited English proficiency.
- Never ask families to contribute money. If costs are involved, they are covered by the program.
- Follow up personally with families who don't attend -- not to pressure, but to share photos and offer alternative connections.

## Year-End Celebrations and Transitions

### The Garden Showcase (Year-End Event)

**Structure:** A public exhibition combining a garden tour, portfolio presentations, service learning displays, and a harvest celebration.

| Station | Description | Student Role |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| **Garden Tour** | Guided walk through the garden with signage explaining each bed, experiment, and system | Students serve as docents, explaining the science, math, and design behind each element |
| **Portfolio Gallery** | Selected portfolio pieces displayed on tables and walls | Students stand by their portfolios and engage visitors in conversation |
| **Service Learning Showcase** | Displays documenting community garden projects, food justice research, and environmental advocacy | Student teams present their projects and outcomes |
| **Data Wall** | Year-long data displays showing garden productivity, biodiversity, and student growth | Students explain the data and what it reveals |
| **Sensory Garden** | Open access to the student-designed sensory meditation garden | Students guide visitors through the sensory experience |
| **Harvest Table** | Garden-grown food prepared by students for visitors | Students serve food and share recipes |
| **Legacy Installation** | Current year's class installs their legacy element (tree, perennial bed, garden art) | Students explain the significance of their legacy piece |

### Transition Support: 8th Grade to High School

For 8th graders transitioning to high school, the final quarter includes:

- **Transfer of garden knowledge**: 8th graders teach 6th and 7th graders their specialized garden skills (ensuring continuity)
- **Regulation plan for transition**: Students develop a personalized regulation toolkit for the high school transition, identifying strategies they can use in a new environment
- **Portfolio preparation**: Students select key pieces from their 6-8 portfolio to carry forward, creating a "Garden Learning Legacy" document that they can share with high school teachers
- **Identity reflection**: Final journal entry: "Who was I when I started this garden? Who am I now? Who do I want to become?"
- **Connection maintenance**: Students are invited to return as mentors, garden volunteers, or guest presenters, maintaining the relationship with the garden community even as they move on

**Garden Metaphor for Transition:** "A plant that has grown strong in one garden can be transplanted to another. It may experience transplant shock -- a period of wilting and adjustment. But its root system carries everything it needs to take hold in new soil and grow again. You carry your root system with you."
