# 6-8 Grade Band: Mathematics Curriculum

## Overview

Mathematics instruction in the 6-8 RootWork curriculum harnesses the garden as both a context for authentic problem-solving and a generator of real data that demands genuine mathematical analysis. Middle school mathematics represents a pivotal transition: students move from arithmetic to algebraic thinking, from concrete measurement to abstract spatial reasoning, from simple data description to inferential analysis, and from following procedures to constructing mathematical arguments. The garden provides the concrete-to-abstract bridge that this transition demands.

For trauma-affected students, mathematics often carries the heaviest burden of academic shame. By middle school, many students have internalized identities as "not math people" based on years of timed tests, public correction, and procedural instruction that disconnected computation from meaning. The RootWork mathematics curriculum disrupts this pattern by embedding every mathematical concept in purposeful garden work -- students are not doing math problems, they are solving real problems that require mathematical thinking. This reframing is not merely motivational; it is structurally different, because the garden provides immediate, concrete feedback that no worksheet can replicate. When a student miscalculates the area of a garden bed, the plants do not fit. When a student miscalculates a fertilizer ratio, the plants show the result. Mathematics becomes a tool for understanding and shaping the living world.

The full TRACE protocol structures every mathematics lesson, and the seven reasoning moves are explicitly taught, practiced, and assessed. Error analysis is a central learning strategy rather than a source of shame. Multiple solution pathways are expected, valued, and compared for efficiency and elegance.

## Unit 1: Ratios & Proportional Relationships (Weeks 1-8)

### Essential Questions
- How do gardeners use ratios to make decisions about planting, feeding, and harvesting?
- What does it mean for two quantities to be proportional?
- How can proportional reasoning help us scale our garden plans?

### Standards Alignment
- **6.RP.1**: Understand ratio concepts and use ratio language
- **6.RP.2**: Understand unit rate concepts
- **6.RP.3**: Use ratio and rate reasoning to solve problems
- **7.RP.1**: Compute unit rates with complex fractions
- **7.RP.2**: Recognize and represent proportional relationships
- **7.RP.3**: Use proportional relationships to solve multi-step percent problems

### Learning Objectives
- Define and identify ratios in garden contexts (seed-to-plant, compost-to-soil, water-to-area)
- Calculate and interpret unit rates (cost per plant, yield per square foot, growth per week)
- Determine whether two quantities are proportionally related using tables, graphs, and equations
- Scale garden designs up and down using proportional reasoning
- Solve percent problems in garden economics (germination rates, yield percentages, budget allocations)

### Week-by-Week Progression

#### Weeks 1-2: Introduction to Ratios Through Garden Recipes

**Lesson: Compost Ratios**

*Root Phase (3 min):* Students complete 1-5 self-assessment and observe the compost bins. "What has changed since last week? What do you notice about the color, texture, and smell?"

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think (4 min):** Present the problem: "Master composters recommend a ratio of 3 parts 'brown' material (dried leaves, cardboard, straw) to 1 part 'green' material (food scraps, fresh grass clippings, coffee grounds). We have collected 45 pounds of brown material. How many pounds of green material do we need?"

Students individually record what they notice and wonder. Reasoning moves: **Decompose** (identify the quantities and the relationship), **Question** ("What does 3:1 mean practically?")

**Reason (12 min):** Students work in pairs to solve the problem using at least two different representations:

- Tape diagram: Draw 3 units for brown, 1 unit for green. If 3 units = 45 lbs, then 1 unit = 15 lbs.
- Table: Build a ratio table showing the relationship (Brown: 3, 6, 9, 12, ... 45 | Green: 1, 2, 3, 4, ... 15)
- Equation: If b/g = 3/1, then 45/g = 3/1, so g = 15 lbs.
- Double number line: Align brown and green quantities on parallel number lines

*Differentiation:*

| Level | Task Modification |
|-------|------------------|
| **Approaching** | Provide the tape diagram template; use simpler ratios (2:1) with smaller numbers; allow manipulatives (dried beans and fresh leaves) |
| **On Level** | Solve the stated problem using two representations; explain why the representations show the same relationship |
| **Advanced** | "Our compost also needs to be about 60% moisture. If we add 15 lbs of green material (which is about 80% water) and 45 lbs of brown material (which is about 20% water), what is the overall moisture percentage? Do we need to add water?" |

**Articulate (6 min):** Gallery walk -- pairs post their solutions. Students visit two other solutions and write a sticky note identifying one strength of the approach. Whole class discussion: "Which representation helped you understand the problem best? Why?" Reasoning move: **Compare** (evaluate representations for clarity and efficiency)

**Check (3 min):** "Let's verify. If we mix 45 lbs of brown and 15 lbs of green, what is the total? (60 lbs) What fraction of the total is brown? (45/60 = 3/4). What fraction is green? (15/60 = 1/4). Does 3/4 to 1/4 match our 3:1 ratio?" Reasoning move: **Verify**

**Extend (3 min):** "A community garden donated 20 lbs of green material. How much brown material should we add to maintain our 3:1 ratio? What if we wanted to make enough compost to fill a 4-foot by 4-foot by 2-foot bin? How much of each material would we need?" Reasoning move: **Transform** (apply the ratio to a new context involving volume)

*Reconnect (4 min):* "Composting is a form of recycling that happens naturally. Where else in life do you encounter ratios that need to stay balanced? Think about recipes, sports, music, or anything else." Journal entry: "Today I learned about ratios by..."

**Lesson: Seed-to-Yield Ratios**

*Root Phase (3 min):* Peer check-in + garden observation.

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think (4 min):** Present data from last season's garden:

| Crop | Seeds Planted | Plants Harvested | Yield (lbs) |
|------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Tomatoes | 24 | 18 | 54 |
| Peppers | 30 | 20 | 30 |
| Beans | 100 | 85 | 25.5 |
| Lettuce | 50 | 42 | 21 |

"What can ratios tell us about our garden's performance?"

**Reason (12 min):** Students calculate multiple ratios from the data:
1. Germination/survival rate: plants harvested / seeds planted (as a fraction, decimal, and percent)
2. Yield ratio: lbs harvested / plants harvested (yield per plant)
3. Seed efficiency: lbs harvested / seeds planted (yield per seed)

Students organize findings in a table and identify which crop was most efficient by different measures.

**Articulate (6 min):** Small group discussion: "If we could only grow two crops next season and wanted to maximize total yield, which two would you choose? Use ratio evidence to support your argument."

**Check (3 min):** "Review your partner's calculations. Do you agree with their ratios? Check one calculation using a different method." Reasoning move: **Verify**

**Extend (3 min):** "How might these ratios change if we improved our soil quality? If we changed our watering schedule? What variables should we test?" Reasoning move: **Question**

#### Weeks 3-4: Unit Rates in Garden Economics

**Lesson: The Garden Budget**

*Reflect Phase Core Problem:*

"Our class has been given a $250 budget to expand the school garden. Here are the prices from three garden supply stores:"

| Item | Green Thumb Supply | Garden Central | The Plant Place |
|------|-------------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Bag of soil (2 cu ft) | $5.49 | $4.99 | $5.25 |
| Tomato starts (6-pack) | $3.99 | $4.49 | $3.75 |
| Seed packets | $1.89 | $2.29 | $1.99 |
| Compost (1 cu ft) | $6.99 | $5.49 | $7.25 |
| Garden gloves (pair) | $4.50 | $3.99 | $5.00 |

**TRACE Protocol Application:**

*Think:* "What unit rates do we need to calculate to make the best purchasing decisions? What factors besides price should we consider?"

*Reason:* Students calculate unit rates (cost per cubic foot, cost per plant, cost per seed). They then plan a complete garden expansion within the $250 budget, justifying every purchase with unit rate comparisons.

*Articulate:* Groups present their proposed budgets to the class as "garden board proposals." Class votes on the most convincing proposal.

*Check:* Student teams review another team's budget for calculation errors and logical consistency. "Does their plan actually stay within budget? Are their unit rate comparisons accurate?"

*Extend:* "What if the budget were cut to $175? What would you change? What if we received a 15% discount from one store? How would that change the best deal?"

**Error Analysis Problem (Restore Phase Integration):**

"A student calculated the unit rate for soil as follows: '$5.49 per bag / 2 bags = $2.75 per bag.' Find and explain the error. What is the correct unit rate, and what was the student confusing?"

This "My Favorite No" exercise helps students distinguish between unit rate (cost per unit of measure: $5.49 / 2 cu ft = $2.745 per cu ft) and other calculations. The error is celebrated as a learning opportunity: "This is a common and completely understandable mistake. The student divided by 2, thinking of bags, but the unit rate we need is per cubic foot."

#### Weeks 5-6: Proportional Relationships -- Tables, Graphs, and Equations

**Lesson: Is Growth Proportional?**

*Reflect Phase Core Investigation:*

Students use data from their own garden growth tracking to investigate whether plant growth is proportional.

**Data Set A: Sunflower Height Over Time**

| Week | Height (inches) |
|------|----------------|
| 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 5 |
| 3 | 9 |
| 4 | 14 |
| 5 | 20 |
| 6 | 27 |

**Data Set B: Water Used Per Garden Bed**

| Number of Beds | Water (gallons per week) |
|---------------|------------------------|
| 1 | 7 |
| 2 | 14 |
| 3 | 21 |
| 4 | 28 |
| 5 | 35 |

*Think:* "Which of these relationships is proportional? How can you tell from the table? How would you see it on a graph?"

*Reason:* Students test for proportionality by:
1. Checking for a constant ratio (y/x should be the same for all pairs)
2. Graphing both relationships and checking for a straight line through the origin
3. Attempting to write an equation of the form y = kx

*Key Discovery:* Data Set A is NOT proportional (the height/week ratio changes: 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5), while Data Set B IS proportional (water/beds = 7 for all pairs). The equation for B is w = 7b.

*Articulate:* "Why might plant growth NOT be proportional while water usage IS? What does that tell us about the biology of plant growth versus the mechanics of irrigation?"

This connects mathematics to scientific reasoning: plant growth is nonlinear because growth rates accelerate as the plant develops more leaf surface area for photosynthesis -- a concept students will explore in depth in the science curriculum.

*Error Analysis:* "A student says Data Set A is proportional because 'it keeps going up by more each time.' What is correct and incorrect about this statement? What mathematical test would help the student refine their thinking?"

#### Weeks 7-8: Scaling Garden Designs

**Culminating Project: Garden Design Scale-Up**

Students apply proportional reasoning to design an expanded school garden using scale drawings.

**Project Parameters:**
- Current garden: 10 ft x 12 ft (120 sq ft)
- Proposed expansion: triple the growing area
- Must include: at least 4 different crop areas, pathways for access, a composting station, and a sensory garden section for the regulation space

**Mathematical Requirements:**
1. Create a scale drawing using a scale of 1 inch = 2 feet
2. Calculate actual dimensions from the scale drawing
3. Determine planting density for each crop using ratios from seed catalogs
4. Calculate total seeds/starts needed using proportional reasoning
5. Create a budget using unit rate analysis from the garden economics lesson
6. Present the design with mathematical justification

**Differentiation:**

| Level | Modification |
|-------|-------------|
| **Approaching** | Provide a partially completed scale drawing template; limit to 2 crop areas; offer a simplified budget with fewer items |
| **On Level** | Complete the full project as described; present to a partner pair |
| **Advanced** | Include irregular shapes in the garden design (circles, trapezoids); calculate water needs using proportional reasoning; present to the school administration with a formal proposal |

## Unit 2: Expressions & Equations (Weeks 9-16)

### Essential Questions
- How can we use variables and expressions to model garden situations?
- What does it mean to solve an equation, and how does that help us in the garden?
- How can we represent constraints and make optimal decisions using inequalities?

### Standards Alignment
- **6.EE.1-9**: Expressions, equations, and inequalities
- **7.EE.1-4**: Expressions, equations, and inequalities with rational numbers
- **8.EE.1-8**: Linear equations, systems, and exponents

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Modeling Plant Growth with Expressions (6th Grade Focus)

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** "A tomato plant is 3 inches tall when transplanted. It grows approximately 2.5 inches per week. How can we write an expression that tells us the plant's height after any number of weeks?"

**Reason:** Students develop the expression h = 2.5w + 3, where h = height in inches and w = weeks since transplanting.

Practice problems (garden contexts):
- "The garden club has $200. Each meeting, they spend $12 on supplies. Write an expression for the remaining budget after m meetings."
- "A compost bin starts with 50 lbs of material. Each week, 5 lbs decompose and the class adds 8 lbs. Write an expression for the amount after w weeks."

**Articulate:** Students explain their expressions to a partner: "What does each part of the expression represent in the real garden situation?"

**Check:** Students substitute specific values and verify against actual garden data. "After 4 weeks, our expression predicts 13 inches. Let's measure our actual tomato plants. How close was our model?"

**Extend:** "What are the limitations of this model? Will the plant really grow 2.5 inches per week forever? When might the model break down?"

---

#### Lesson: Solving Equations Through Garden Planning (7th Grade Focus)

*Reflect Phase Core Problem:*

"The garden club wants to build raised beds. Cedar boards cost $4.50 per linear foot. The budget for materials is $180. Each rectangular raised bed requires boards for its perimeter. If each bed is 4 feet wide, what lengths are possible?"

**TRACE Protocol:**

*Think:* "What do we know? What do we need to find? What equation represents this situation?"

Students develop: 4.50 x 2(4 + L) = 180, which simplifies to 4.50(8 + 2L) = 180

*Reason:* Students solve the equation using multiple strategies:
- **Strategy 1 (Algebraic)**: Distribute, combine, isolate the variable
  - 36 + 9L = 180
  - 9L = 144
  - L = 16 feet
- **Strategy 2 (Reasoning backward)**: $180 / $4.50 = 40 linear feet of board. Perimeter = 40 ft. 2(4 + L) = 40. 4 + L = 20. L = 16 ft.
- **Strategy 3 (Table/guess-and-check)**: Try different lengths and calculate costs until hitting $180.

*Articulate:* Compare strategies: "Which method was most efficient? Which gave you the best understanding of the problem?" Reasoning move: **Compare**

*Check:* "Does L = 16 make sense? Perimeter = 2(4+16) = 40 ft. Cost = 40 x $4.50 = $180. Yes!" Reasoning move: **Verify**

*Extend:* "What if we wanted to build TWO identical beds from the same budget? Set up and solve a new equation." (4.50 x 2 x 2(4 + L) = 180; L = 6 ft)

"What if we changed the width to 3 feet? How does that affect the possible length? Which configuration gives us more growing area?"

#### Lesson: Inequalities and Garden Constraints (7th-8th Grade)

*Reflect Phase:*

**Core Problem:** "The school garden can support at most 8 hours of watering per week from our rain barrel. Each tomato bed needs 1.5 hours, each herb bed needs 0.5 hours, and each flower bed needs 1 hour of watering. We must have at least 2 tomato beds. What combinations of beds can we support?"

**Mathematical Modeling:**
- Let t = tomato beds, h = herb beds, f = flower beds
- Constraint 1: 1.5t + 0.5h + 1f <= 8
- Constraint 2: t >= 2
- Objective: Maximize total beds (or total growing area, depending on lesson focus)

Students explore feasible combinations using tables, then graph the constraint for two variables (fixing t = 2 and exploring h and f):
- 1.5(2) + 0.5h + f <= 8
- 0.5h + f <= 5

This introduces systems of linear inequalities through a real planning problem. Students plot the inequality and identify all integer solutions that represent possible garden configurations.

**Error Analysis Problem:**
"A student says we can have 2 tomato beds, 6 herb beds, and 3 flower beds. Check this claim."
- 1.5(2) + 0.5(6) + 1(3) = 3 + 3 + 3 = 9 > 8
- "The student's plan requires 9 hours of watering but we only have 8 available. What could they adjust?"

## Unit 3: Geometry (Weeks 17-24)

### Essential Questions
- How do we measure and calculate the space in our garden?
- How can geometric thinking help us design more effective and beautiful gardens?
- How do we apply geometric reasoning to three-dimensional garden structures?

### Standards Alignment
- **6.G.1-4**: Area, surface area, volume
- **7.G.1-6**: Scale drawings, cross-sections, area, circumference, angles
- **8.G.1-9**: Transformations, congruence, similarity, Pythagorean theorem

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Garden Area and Planting Density (6th Grade Focus)

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** Present a garden map showing irregularly shaped beds:
- Bed A: An L-shaped bed (can be decomposed into two rectangles: 8ft x 3ft and 4ft x 3ft)
- Bed B: A triangular bed (base = 6 ft, height = 4 ft)
- Bed C: A trapezoidal bed (parallel sides = 5 ft and 9 ft, height = 4 ft)

"Calculate the area of each bed. Then determine how many plants can fit in each bed if tomatoes need 4 square feet per plant and lettuce needs 0.5 square feet per plant."

**Reason:** Students decompose the complex shapes, calculate areas, and apply planting density ratios.

- Bed A: (8 x 3) + (4 x 3) = 24 + 12 = 36 sq ft. Tomatoes: 36/4 = 9 plants. Lettuce: 36/0.5 = 72 plants.
- Bed B: (1/2)(6)(4) = 12 sq ft. Tomatoes: 12/4 = 3 plants. Lettuce: 12/0.5 = 24 plants.
- Bed C: (1/2)(5+9)(4) = 28 sq ft. Tomatoes: 28/4 = 7 plants. Lettuce: 28/0.5 = 56 plants.

Reasoning move: **Decompose** (break complex shapes into simpler components)

**Articulate:** "Which bed is the most space-efficient? If you had to choose one bed for tomatoes and one for lettuce, which would you choose and why?"

**Error Analysis:** "A student calculated Bed C's area as 5 x 9 x 4 = 180 sq ft. What went wrong? How would you explain the correct trapezoid formula to this student using a concrete model?"

Students use physical models (cutting a trapezoid from graph paper and rearranging it into a rectangle) to demonstrate why the formula works.

---

#### Lesson: Circular Garden Design -- Circumference and Area (7th Grade Focus)

*Reflect Phase:*

**Core Problem:** "We want to build a circular herb spiral -- a common permaculture design. The spiral will have a 5-foot radius. We need to know the circumference (to buy edging material) and the area (to order soil)."

**TRACE Protocol:**

*Think:* "What do we know about circles? What formulas do we need? What is pi, and why does it appear in these formulas?"

*Reason:*
- Circumference = 2(pi)(r) = 2(3.14)(5) = 31.4 feet of edging needed
- Area = (pi)(r^2) = 3.14(25) = 78.5 sq ft of planting space
- If soil is sold in cubic feet and we need the bed to be 1 foot deep: Volume = 78.5 x 1 = 78.5 cu ft of soil

Students also investigate why pi works by measuring circular objects in the garden (round pots, tree trunks, stepping stones) and calculating the ratio of circumference to diameter:

| Object | Circumference (in) | Diameter (in) | C/d |
|--------|-------------------|---------------|-----|
| Clay pot | 31.5 | 10.0 | 3.15 |
| Bucket | 47.1 | 15.0 | 3.14 |
| Tree trunk | 62.8 | 20.0 | 3.14 |
| Stepping stone | 37.7 | 12.0 | 3.14 |

"No matter the size of the circle, C/d is always approximately 3.14. This constant is what we call pi."

Reasoning move: **Generalize** (from specific measurements to a universal mathematical constant)

---

#### Lesson: The Pythagorean Theorem in Garden Construction (8th Grade Focus)

*Reflect Phase:*

**Core Problem:** "We need to build a rectangular raised bed that is exactly 4 feet by 8 feet. How can we make sure our corners are perfect right angles? Construction workers use a technique called the '3-4-5 method.' Let's discover why it works."

**Investigation:**
1. Students measure a diagonal of their intended 4 x 8 rectangle.
2. Calculate the expected diagonal: d = sqrt(4^2 + 8^2) = sqrt(16 + 64) = sqrt(80) = 8.94 feet
3. Measure the actual diagonal. If it matches, the rectangle is true. If not, adjust until diagonals match.

**Deepening:** "The 3-4-5 method works because 3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2 (9 + 16 = 25). Any multiple also works: 6-8-10, 9-12-15. Measure 3 feet along one side and 4 feet along the adjacent side. If the diagonal between those points is exactly 5 feet, you have a right angle."

**Application Problems:**
- "A garden trellis is built as a right triangle with legs of 6 feet (vertical) and 3 feet (horizontal). How long is the diagonal support beam?"
- "A pathway runs diagonally across a 12 ft by 16 ft garden plot. How long is the pathway?"
- "Can a 10-foot pole fit diagonally in a garden shed that is 6 feet wide and 8 feet long?"

**Error Analysis:** "A student says the diagonal of a 5 x 12 rectangle is 5 + 12 = 17 feet. Explain the error and help the student understand why we can't simply add the sides."

## Unit 4: Statistics & Probability (Weeks 25-32)

### Essential Questions
- What stories do our garden data tell?
- How can we use statistics to make better decisions about our garden?
- How do we design fair experiments and draw valid conclusions?

### Standards Alignment
- **6.SP.1-5**: Statistical variability, distribution, measures of center and spread
- **7.SP.1-8**: Sampling, probability, comparative analysis
- **8.SP.1-4**: Scatter plots, lines of best fit, two-way tables

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Describing Garden Data (6th Grade Focus)

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** Present the class with harvest data from the fall season:

"Tomato Yield Per Plant (in pounds): 4.2, 3.8, 5.1, 2.9, 4.5, 6.0, 3.5, 4.8, 3.2, 4.0, 5.5, 4.3, 3.7, 4.1, 4.6"

"What can we say about our tomato harvest? What number best represents the 'typical' yield?"

**Reason:** Students calculate:
- Mean: (sum of all values) / 15 = 4.28 lbs
- Median: Arrange in order, find middle value = 4.2 lbs
- Range: 6.0 - 2.9 = 3.1 lbs
- Interquartile Range (IQR): Q3 - Q1 = 4.8 - 3.5 = 1.3 lbs

Students create a dot plot and a box plot to visualize the distribution.

**Articulate:** "Which measure of center (mean or median) better represents the 'typical' tomato plant in our garden? Why? What does the range tell us about consistency?"

**Extend:** "If we added one plant that yielded 12 pounds (an outlier), how would that change the mean? The median? Which measure is more resistant to outliers?"

**Reasoning Moves in Action:**
- **Decompose**: Break the data set into quartiles
- **Compare**: Compare mean and median; compare this season to last season
- **Question**: "Why did some plants yield so much more than others? What variables might explain the variation?"
- **Generalize**: "Median is more resistant to outliers than mean -- this is true for any data set, not just garden data"

---

#### Lesson: Designing a Garden Experiment (7th Grade Focus)

*Reflect Phase:*

**Core Problem:** "We want to test whether adding compost tea improves tomato plant growth compared to water alone. How do we design a fair experiment?"

**Experimental Design Components:**

| Component | Student-Designed Plan |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **Research Question** | Does compost tea increase tomato plant growth compared to regular water? |
| **Hypothesis** | Plants treated with compost tea will grow taller and produce more fruit than those treated with water alone |
| **Independent Variable** | Type of treatment (compost tea vs. water) |
| **Dependent Variables** | Plant height (cm), number of fruits, total yield (g) |
| **Controlled Variables** | Soil type, sunlight exposure, plant variety, planting date, amount of liquid applied |
| **Sample Size** | 10 plants per group (20 total) |
| **Random Assignment** | Number plants 1-20; use random number generator to assign to groups |
| **Data Collection** | Weekly measurements of height; fruit count and weight at harvest |
| **Duration** | 8 weeks |

**Statistical Analysis Plan:**
- Calculate mean and median for each group
- Create comparative box plots
- Calculate the difference in means
- Discuss whether the difference is meaningful (introduction to the concept of statistical significance without formal hypothesis testing)

**Probability Connection:** "If compost tea has no effect, what is the probability that the treatment group would have a higher mean just by chance? We'll use simulation to explore this."

Students conduct a hands-on simulation: combine all 20 measurements, randomly split them into two groups of 10, and calculate the difference in means. Repeat 20 times. Compare their actual difference to the distribution of random differences.

#### Lesson: Scatter Plots and Prediction (8th Grade Focus)

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** Present data collected over the growing season:

| Week | Avg. Temp (F) | Rainfall (in) | Tomato Growth (cm) | Sunlight Hours |
|------|---------------|---------------|-------------------|----------------|
| 1 | 62 | 0.5 | 2.1 | 10.5 |
| 2 | 65 | 1.2 | 3.4 | 11.0 |
| 3 | 70 | 0.8 | 4.8 | 11.5 |
| 4 | 73 | 0.3 | 5.2 | 12.0 |
| 5 | 78 | 1.5 | 7.1 | 12.5 |
| 6 | 82 | 0.2 | 6.8 | 13.0 |
| 7 | 85 | 0.0 | 5.5 | 13.2 |
| 8 | 88 | 0.1 | 4.0 | 13.0 |
| 9 | 83 | 0.9 | 6.2 | 12.5 |
| 10 | 75 | 1.1 | 5.8 | 11.5 |

"Create scatter plots comparing temperature to growth and rainfall to growth. What patterns do you see? Can we use these patterns to make predictions?"

**Reason:**
1. Temperature vs. Growth: Students observe a curved (nonlinear) relationship -- growth increases with temperature up to a point, then decreases. This is an opportunity to discuss why linear models do not always apply and why biological systems have optimal ranges.
2. Rainfall vs. Growth: Moderate positive association, with more scatter.

For the linear portion of the temperature-growth relationship (weeks 1-5), students construct an informal line of best fit and use it to predict growth for a given temperature.

**Articulate:** "Why does the temperature-growth relationship curve? What is happening biologically that creates an optimal temperature range?"

Reasoning move: **Connect** (link the mathematical pattern to the biological concept of optimal temperature ranges -- connects math to the science curriculum)

**Error Analysis:** "A student draws a line of best fit through all 10 data points for temperature vs. growth and predicts that at 100F, tomato growth would be 10 cm per week. What's wrong with this prediction? What concept does this illustrate about the limitations of linear models?"

## Unit 5: Pre-Algebra Concepts (Weeks 25-36, integrated with Units 3-4)

### Essential Questions
- What is a function, and how do gardens help us understand input-output relationships?
- How can we represent patterns algebraically?
- How do linear functions help us model and predict garden phenomena?

### Standards Alignment
- **8.EE.5-6**: Proportional relationships, slope
- **8.F.1-5**: Functions, linear models, comparing functions

### Core Lesson Sequence

#### Lesson: Functions in the Garden

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** "Consider this garden scenario: The cost of buying seedlings depends on how many you buy. Each seedling costs $2.50, and there is a flat delivery fee of $5.00. Create an input-output table, write a function rule, and graph the relationship."

**Reason:**

| Seedlings (x) | Cost (y = 2.50x + 5.00) |
|---------------|--------------------------|
| 0 | $5.00 |
| 2 | $10.00 |
| 4 | $15.00 |
| 6 | $20.00 |
| 10 | $30.00 |
| 20 | $55.00 |

Students identify:
- The slope (rate of change) = $2.50 per seedling (the cost per additional seedling)
- The y-intercept = $5.00 (the delivery fee, which exists even if you buy zero seedlings)
- The function: f(x) = 2.50x + 5.00

**Articulate:** "What does the slope mean in this garden context? What does the y-intercept mean? If a friend said 'I'll just multiply the number of seedlings by $2.50 to get the cost,' what error are they making?"

**Garden Investigation Extension:**

Students compare two function models using actual garden data:

- **Model A (linear):** Plant height after transplanting: h(t) = 3.2t + 8, where t = weeks and h = height in cm
- **Model B (nonlinear):** Seed germination over time: g(t) = starts slow, accelerates, then levels off (an S-curve)

"Graph both models. Which is linear? How can you tell from the equation? From the graph? From the table? Which model better represents reality, and why?"

Reasoning move: **Transform** (move between representations: equation, table, graph, verbal description)

---

#### Lesson: Comparing Linear Functions (8th Grade)

*Reflect Phase:*

**Core Problem:** "Two students are growing sunflowers in different conditions. Their growth data produces the following linear models:

- Student A (full sun): h = 4.5t + 2 (h = height in inches, t = weeks)
- Student B (partial shade): h = 2.8t + 5

When will the plants be the same height? Which will be taller after 10 weeks?"

**Graphical Solution:** Students graph both lines on the same coordinate plane and identify the intersection point.

**Algebraic Solution:** Set equations equal: 4.5t + 2 = 2.8t + 5. Solve: 1.7t = 3. t = 1.76 weeks.
At t = 1.76 weeks: h = 4.5(1.76) + 2 = 9.94 inches.

**Interpretation:** "Student B's plant started taller (5 inches vs. 2 inches at transplant), but Student A's plant grows faster (4.5 inches per week vs. 2.8). By about week 2, Student A's plant overtakes Student B's."

After 10 weeks: Student A: 4.5(10) + 2 = 47 inches. Student B: 2.8(10) + 5 = 33 inches.

**Extend:** "What real-world factors explain the difference in growth rates? How might the models change after week 10? Do plants really grow linearly forever?"

This connects to the science curriculum's exploration of growth curves and limiting factors in ecosystems.

## Multiple Pathway Approaches: Summary

Every lesson in the 6-8 mathematics curriculum supports multiple solution strategies:

| Pathway | Description | Best For |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| **Concrete/Physical** | Using garden materials, manipulatives, physical models | Students still building conceptual understanding; kinesthetic learners; students who need grounding |
| **Visual/Graphical** | Graphs, diagrams, tape diagrams, number lines, scale drawings | Visual learners; students developing proportional reasoning; making abstract relationships visible |
| **Tabular/Numerical** | Tables, organized lists, systematic guess-and-check | Students who need structure; building toward algebraic reasoning; data-oriented thinkers |
| **Algebraic/Symbolic** | Equations, expressions, formal algebraic manipulation | Students ready for abstraction; building fluency with mathematical notation |
| **Verbal/Narrative** | Explaining reasoning in words, writing about mathematics, presenting arguments | Students with strong verbal skills; building mathematical communication; supporting language development |

Students are expected to use at least two pathways for each major problem, developing the capacity to **Transform** between representations and deepening their understanding through multiple lenses. The garden provides the concrete anchor that makes all pathways accessible, even for students who have previously experienced mathematics as an abstract, disconnected discipline.
