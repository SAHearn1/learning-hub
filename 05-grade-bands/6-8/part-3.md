# 6-8 Grade Band: Science & Interdisciplinary Projects

## Science Curriculum Through the Garden

### Overview

Science instruction in the 6-8 RootWork curriculum transforms the school garden into a living laboratory where students engage in authentic scientific inquiry at the molecular, organismal, ecosystem, and earth systems levels. Middle school science marks a critical transition: students move beyond observation and description toward mechanistic explanation, controlled experimentation, data-driven argumentation, and model-based reasoning. The garden provides an ideal context for this transition because it generates real phenomena that demand explanation, real data that resists oversimplification, and real problems that require engineering solutions.

For trauma-affected students, science in the garden offers a particular form of healing: it externalizes attention. Students who are consumed by internal distress -- rumination, hypervigilance, shame -- can find relief in the discipline of careful observation. "What is happening in this leaf?" is a question that draws the mind outward, into the present moment, into the tangible world. This externalization of attention is itself a regulation strategy, and when combined with the sensory richness of the garden environment, it creates conditions where deep learning and nervous system regulation occur simultaneously.

The TRACE protocol structures all science instruction, and the seven reasoning moves map directly onto scientific practices: Decompose aligns with analyzing systems; Compare supports controlled experimentation; Question drives inquiry; Verify underpins replication and evidence evaluation; Generalize connects to theory building; Transform supports model development; and Connect bridges science to mathematics, literacy, and social contexts.

## Unit 1: Cell Biology & Plant Physiology (Weeks 1-10)

### Essential Questions
- What are all living things made of, and how do cells function as the basic unit of life?
- How do plants convert sunlight into food, and why does that matter for our garden and our planet?
- How do cells work together to keep a plant alive and growing?

### Standards Alignment
- **MS-LS1-1**: Conduct an investigation to provide evidence that living things are made of cells
- **MS-LS1-2**: Develop and use a model to describe the function of a cell as a whole and the ways parts of cells contribute to the function
- **MS-LS1-6**: Construct a scientific explanation based on evidence for the role of photosynthesis in the cycling of matter and flow of energy
- **MS-LS1-7**: Develop a model to describe how food is rearranged through chemical reactions to release energy (cellular respiration)

### Lesson Sequence

#### Weeks 1-3: Discovering Cells

**Lesson: What Are We Made Of? (Introduction to Cell Theory)**

*Root Phase (3 min):* Self-assessment + garden observation. Students examine a leaf from the garden with the naked eye. "You can see veins, edges, color variations. But what would you see if you could look a thousand times closer?"

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think (4 min):** "Scientists discovered that all living things are made of tiny units called cells. Some organisms are a single cell. Others, like us and the plants in our garden, are made of trillions of cells. Today we'll see plant cells for the first time."

**Reason (20 min):** Laboratory Investigation -- Preparing and Observing Plant Cell Slides

*Materials per pair:* Microscope, blank slides, cover slips, iodine stain, water dropper, thin onion membrane, Elodea leaf (aquatic plant), razor blade (teacher use only for thin sections)

*Procedure:*
1. Peel a thin layer of onion epidermal cells. Place on a slide with a drop of water. Add cover slip.
2. Observe under low power (40x), then medium (100x), then high (400x).
3. Add a drop of iodine stain at the edge of the cover slip. Observe how visibility changes.
4. Sketch and label: cell wall, cell membrane, nucleus, cytoplasm, vacuole.
5. Repeat with Elodea leaf. Observe chloroplasts (small green discs within cells).
6. Compare onion cells (no chloroplasts -- underground, no photosynthesis) to Elodea cells (chloroplasts present -- aquatic plant that photosynthesizes).

**TRACE Protocol Application:**

*Think:* "What do you already know about cells? What do you expect to see?"
*Reason:* Students investigate, observe, draw, and label what they see under the microscope
*Articulate:* Partners compare drawings: "What did you see that your partner didn't? What structures did you both observe?"
*Check:* "Compare your drawing to the textbook diagram of a plant cell. Did you capture the key structures? What might you have missed?"
*Extend:* "Why do you think onion cells lack chloroplasts while Elodea cells have them? What does this tell us about where photosynthesis occurs?"

Reasoning move: **Compare** (onion cells vs. Elodea cells reveals functional differences based on environment)

**Differentiation:**

| Level | Modification |
|-------|-------------|
| **Approaching** | Provide pre-prepared slides alongside student preparations; offer a labeled diagram for reference while drawing; pair with a peer mentor |
| **On Level** | Prepare slides independently; draw and label from observation; compare two cell types |
| **Advanced** | Prepare slides of three or more cell types (add a garden leaf cross-section); calculate approximate cell size using the microscope's scale; research why plant cells have structures animal cells lack |

#### Weeks 4-6: Photosynthesis -- The Garden's Power Source

**Lesson: Capturing Sunlight**

*Root Phase (3 min):* Regulation check + garden observation. "Every green surface in our garden is doing something remarkable right now. Today we discover what."

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think (5 min):** Present two equations:

Photosynthesis: 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy --> C6H12O6 + 6O2

"This equation tells the story of how every plant in our garden stays alive. Let's decode it."

Students identify the reactants (carbon dioxide, water, light) and products (glucose, oxygen). Reasoning move: **Decompose** (break the equation into its component parts and identify what each represents)

**Reason (20 min):** Multi-station investigation

*Station 1: Light Requirement*
- Cover half of a large leaf with aluminum foil. Leave for 5 days.
- After 5 days, remove foil and test both halves with iodine (iodine turns blue-black in the presence of starch, which is stored glucose).
- Prediction: The uncovered half will test positive for starch; the covered half will not.
- Conclusion: Light is required for photosynthesis.

*Station 2: CO2 Requirement*
- Place one Elodea sprig in water with baking soda (CO2 source) and another in plain water.
- Place both under bright light and count oxygen bubbles per minute.
- Prediction: More bubbles in the CO2-enriched water.
- Conclusion: CO2 is a reactant in photosynthesis.

*Station 3: Oxygen Production*
- Submerge Elodea in water under a bright light. Collect gas in an inverted test tube.
- Test the collected gas with a glowing splint (it should reignite, indicating oxygen).
- Conclusion: Oxygen is produced during photosynthesis.

*Station 4: Rate Variables*
- Place Elodea sprigs at different distances from a light source.
- Count bubbles per minute at each distance.
- Graph the relationship between light intensity (distance) and photosynthesis rate (bubble count).
- Connect to mathematics: Is this relationship linear or nonlinear?

**Articulate (8 min):** Jigsaw sharing -- students from each station teach their findings to a mixed group. Together, the group constructs a complete explanation of photosynthesis.

Reasoning move: **Connect** (each station's evidence contributes one piece to the complete photosynthesis model)

**Check:** "Return to the equation. Can you now explain what every part means based on the evidence from your investigations?"

**Extend:** "Our garden depends on photosynthesis. What would happen to the garden -- and to us -- if photosynthesis stopped worldwide? How does photosynthesis connect to the oxygen we breathe and the food we eat?"

**Garden Connection:** "When we move our seedlings from the shaded cold frame to the full-sun garden, their growth accelerates. Now you know why -- more light means more photosynthesis means more glucose for growth."

#### Weeks 7-8: Cellular Respiration -- The Other Side of the Coin

**Lesson: How Plants (and We) Use Energy**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** "Photosynthesis makes glucose. But how do plants actually use that glucose for energy? And here's a surprise -- the process is almost the reverse of photosynthesis."

Cellular Respiration: C6H12O6 + 6O2 --> 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy (ATP)

Students compare the two equations side by side. Reasoning move: **Compare**

**Reason:** Students create a Venn diagram or comparison table:

| Feature | Photosynthesis | Cellular Respiration |
|---------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Reactants** | CO2, H2O, light | Glucose, O2 |
| **Products** | Glucose, O2 | CO2, H2O, energy |
| **Location in plant** | Chloroplasts (leaves) | Mitochondria (all cells) |
| **When it occurs** | Only in light | All the time (day and night) |
| **Energy** | Absorbed (stored in glucose) | Released (for cell work) |
| **Organisms** | Plants, algae, some bacteria | All living organisms |

**Key Insight:** "The products of one process are the reactants of the other. They form a cycle. The carbon and oxygen atoms cycle between CO2 in the air and glucose in living things."

**Garden Investigation:** "At night, when photosynthesis stops but respiration continues, do plants release CO2? Let's test it." Students place a plant in a sealed container overnight with a CO2 indicator solution. In the morning, the indicator shows increased CO2 -- evidence of respiration without photosynthesis.

**Extend:** "When we compost our garden waste, the decomposing organisms are performing cellular respiration on the plant material. The heat you feel in a compost pile? That's the energy being released. Composting is cellular respiration at the ecosystem level."

#### Weeks 9-10: From Cells to Systems -- Plant Organization

**Lesson: How Water Travels from Root to Leaf**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Core Investigation:** Students trace the path of water through a plant by placing a white carnation or celery stalk in colored water and observing the color traveling through the vascular system over 24-48 hours.

**Deepening:** Students examine cross-sections of stems under the microscope to identify xylem (water transport) and phloem (sugar transport) tissue.

**Model Building:** Students create a poster or digital model showing the levels of organization:
- Cells (root hair cells, xylem cells, leaf cells)
- Tissues (dermal tissue, vascular tissue, ground tissue)
- Organs (roots, stems, leaves, flowers)
- Organ systems (root system, shoot system)
- Organism (the whole plant)

**Garden Connection:** "When we water the garden, we're providing raw material that will travel from the roots, through the xylem, all the way to the leaves where photosynthesis happens. Understanding this system helps us know WHERE to water (at the roots, not the leaves) and HOW MUCH (enough to reach the root zone)."

Reasoning move: **Generalize** (the hierarchical organization of cells into tissues into organs into systems is a pattern that applies to all multicellular organisms)

## Unit 2: Ecology & Environmental Science (Weeks 11-22)

### Essential Questions
- What makes our school garden an ecosystem, and how do the living and nonliving components interact?
- How can we measure and increase the biodiversity of our garden?
- What does sustainability mean, and how can our garden model sustainable practices?

### Standards Alignment
- **MS-LS2-1**: Analyze and interpret data to provide evidence for the effects of resource availability on organisms and populations
- **MS-LS2-2**: Construct an explanation that predicts patterns of interactions among organisms
- **MS-LS2-3**: Develop a model to describe the cycling of matter and flow of energy among living and nonliving parts of an ecosystem
- **MS-LS2-4**: Construct an argument supported by evidence that changes to physical or biological components of an ecosystem affect populations
- **MS-ESS3-3**: Apply scientific principles to design a method for monitoring and minimizing a human impact on the environment

### Lesson Sequence

#### Weeks 11-13: The Garden Ecosystem

**Lesson: Ecosystem Inventory and Mapping**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think (5 min):** "An ecosystem includes all the living things (biotic factors) and nonliving things (abiotic factors) in an area, plus the interactions between them. Our school garden is an ecosystem. What biotic and abiotic factors can you identify?"

**Reason (25 min):** Outdoor field investigation

Students work in teams of 3-4, each assigned a 1-meter-square quadrat within the garden.

*Data Collection Protocol:*

| Data Type | Method | Tool |
|-----------|--------|------|
| **Plant species** | Identify and count all plant species in the quadrat | Field guide, plant ID app |
| **Invertebrates** | Careful observation for 10 minutes; turn over leaves and small stones | Hand lens, collection jar, field guide |
| **Soil organisms** | Take a soil sample; examine with magnifying glass | Trowel, white tray, hand lens |
| **Soil temperature** | Insert thermometer 2 inches into soil | Soil thermometer |
| **Soil moisture** | Use moisture meter or feel test | Moisture meter |
| **Soil pH** | Use pH test kit or meter | pH kit |
| **Light level** | Use light meter or phone app | Light meter |
| **Wind exposure** | Observe wind flag or feel | Wind meter or observation |

Students record all data in field notebooks with date, time, weather conditions, and quadrat location.

**Articulate:** Each team presents their quadrat findings. Class compiles data into a whole-garden ecosystem inventory.

**Check:** "How confident are we in our data? What sources of error might exist? How could we improve our sampling next time?"

**Extend:** "How does our garden ecosystem compare to a natural ecosystem? What's missing from our garden that a forest or meadow would have? What does that tell us about biodiversity?"

---

**Lesson: Biodiversity Assessment**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Core Activity:** Using the quadrat data, students calculate a Simpson's Diversity Index for their garden:

D = 1 - [sum of (n/N)^2]

Where n = number of individuals of each species and N = total number of individuals.

*Example calculation from student data:*

| Species | Count (n) | n/N | (n/N)^2 |
|---------|-----------|-----|---------|
| Tomato plants | 12 | 0.24 | 0.058 |
| Basil | 8 | 0.16 | 0.026 |
| Lettuce | 15 | 0.30 | 0.090 |
| Marigold | 6 | 0.12 | 0.014 |
| Earthworms | 5 | 0.10 | 0.010 |
| Ladybugs | 2 | 0.04 | 0.002 |
| Bees (observed) | 2 | 0.04 | 0.002 |
| **Total (N)** | **50** | | **Sum = 0.202** |

D = 1 - 0.202 = 0.798

"A diversity index of 0.798 means our garden has moderately high diversity. A monoculture (one species) would score 0. A perfectly diverse ecosystem would score close to 1."

**Math Connection:** This activity integrates ratios, decimals, squaring, and summation -- concepts from the mathematics curriculum -- in an authentic scientific context.

**Extend:** "How could we increase our garden's biodiversity index? What species could we add? How would adding a pollinator garden or a native plant section change our score?"

Reasoning move: **Transform** (convert raw count data into a mathematical index that represents a complex ecological concept)

---

#### Weeks 14-16: Ecosystem Interactions and Food Webs

**Lesson: The Garden Food Web**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** "Every organism in our garden is connected to others through feeding relationships. Let's map these connections."

**Reason:** Students build a garden food web using organisms from their ecosystem inventory:

*Producers:* Tomato plants, basil, lettuce, marigolds, clover, grass
*Primary Consumers:* Aphids, caterpillars, grasshoppers, rabbits, slugs
*Secondary Consumers:* Ladybugs, praying mantis, garden spiders, birds
*Decomposers:* Earthworms, fungi, bacteria

Students create the food web on a large poster using yarn to connect organisms. Each student holds a card with an organism and yarn connections show feeding relationships.

**Simulation:** "What happens if we remove the pollinators?" Students holding bee and butterfly cards drop their connections. Trace the cascade: no pollination means reduced fruit production for tomatoes and peppers, which means less food for primary consumers, which means less food for secondary consumers.

**Articulate:** Socratic seminar: "Is there one organism in our food web that, if removed, would cause the most disruption? Defend your answer with evidence from our web model."

**Connect to Service Learning:** "Our garden food web connects to the larger community. The vegetables we grow feed people. The pollinators we support serve neighboring gardens and farms. Ecosystems don't stop at property lines."

#### Weeks 17-19: Sustainability and Human Impact

**Lesson: Carbon Footprint of a Tomato**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Core Investigation:** Students compare the carbon footprint of a store-bought tomato versus a garden-grown tomato.

*Research and Calculation:*

| Factor | Store-Bought Tomato | Garden-Grown Tomato |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Growing** | Industrial agriculture: synthetic fertilizers (produced with fossil fuels), pesticides, heavy machinery | Compost-based soil amendment; hand tools; no synthetic inputs |
| **Harvesting** | Machine-harvested | Hand-harvested |
| **Processing** | Washed, sorted, packaged in factory | None (eaten fresh or simply washed) |
| **Transportation** | Average 1,500 miles by refrigerated truck | 50 feet from garden to kitchen |
| **Refrigeration** | Weeks of cold storage | None needed (consumed fresh) |
| **Packaging** | Plastic clamshell or cardboard box | None |

Students estimate carbon emissions at each stage using provided conversion factors and calculate the total for each tomato.

**Articulate:** Students write a scientific argument: "Based on our analysis, growing tomatoes in the school garden produces approximately X% less carbon emissions than purchasing store-bought tomatoes. This matters because..."

Reasoning move: **Generalize** (from this specific comparison, what can we say about local food systems and sustainability more broadly?)

**Extend:** "Is the answer always simple? What about in winter, when we can't grow tomatoes outdoors? What if we used a greenhouse -- how would the heating costs change the calculation?"

#### Weeks 20-22: Ecosystem Modeling and Sustainability Design

**Culminating Project: Sustainable Garden Redesign**

Students work in teams to redesign the school garden for maximum sustainability:

**Project Requirements:**
1. **Water management system**: Design a rainwater collection and drip irrigation system. Calculate water needs based on plant types and garden area. (Engineering design + math)
2. **Soil health plan**: Develop a composting system and crop rotation schedule to maintain soil fertility without synthetic inputs. (Biology + chemistry)
3. **Biodiversity enhancement**: Add a native plant section, pollinator habitat, and companion planting scheme. Predict the effect on the biodiversity index. (Ecology + math)
4. **Carbon reduction analysis**: Calculate the carbon savings of the redesigned garden compared to the current design. (Environmental science + math)
5. **Written proposal**: Formal scientific proposal with introduction, methods, expected results, and budget. (Literacy)
6. **Presentation**: Present to an authentic audience (school board, community partners, or a panel of local gardeners). (Speaking/listening)

**Engineering Design Process Integration:**

| Phase | Activity |
|-------|----------|
| **Define the problem** | Identify specific sustainability challenges in the current garden |
| **Research** | Investigate sustainable gardening practices; consult with local experts |
| **Brainstorm solutions** | Generate multiple design ideas for each challenge |
| **Select and plan** | Evaluate options using criteria (cost, effectiveness, feasibility); develop a detailed plan |
| **Build a prototype** | Create a scale model of the redesigned garden |
| **Test and evaluate** | Present to peers for feedback; revise based on critique |
| **Communicate results** | Formal presentation with scientific justification |

## Unit 3: Earth Science Connections (Weeks 23-28)

### Essential Questions
- How does water move through our garden and the larger water cycle?
- What is soil really made of, and how does soil chemistry affect plant growth?
- How do climate and weather patterns determine what we can grow?

### Standards Alignment
- **MS-ESS2-4**: Develop a model to describe the cycling of water through Earth's systems
- **MS-PS1-2**: Analyze and interpret data on the properties of substances to identify them (soil chemistry)
- **MS-ESS3-5**: Ask questions to clarify evidence of the factors that have caused the rise in global temperatures

### Lesson Sequence

#### Weeks 23-25: The Water Cycle Through the Garden

**Lesson: Tracing Water Through Our Ecosystem**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** "When we turn on the garden hose, where does that water go? Follow a single water molecule from the hose nozzle through the garden ecosystem and beyond."

**Reason:** Students create a detailed model tracing water through every stage:

1. **Irrigation**: Water applied to soil surface
2. **Infiltration**: Water seeps into soil pores (rate depends on soil type -- clay vs. sand)
3. **Root uptake**: Root hairs absorb water through osmosis
4. **Xylem transport**: Water moves up through the plant's vascular system
5. **Transpiration**: Water evaporates from leaf stomata (over 90% of water taken up by roots is lost through transpiration)
6. **Evaporation**: Water evaporates from soil surface
7. **Condensation**: Water vapor rises, cools, and forms clouds
8. **Precipitation**: Water returns as rain
9. **Runoff and groundwater**: Excess water flows over land or percolates deep into aquifers

**Investigation: Measuring Transpiration**
- Seal a plastic bag around a branch with leaves (in the garden).
- After 24 hours, observe and measure the water collected in the bag.
- Calculate: If one branch transpires X ml per day, and the tree has approximately Y branches, estimate total daily transpiration for the tree.

**Math Connection:** This calculation involves estimation, multiplication, and unit conversion -- reinforcing mathematical skills in a scientific context.

**Extend:** "Climate change is affecting the water cycle. How would increased temperatures affect transpiration rates? What would that mean for our garden's water needs?"

---

#### Weeks 26-28: Soil Chemistry and the Nutrient Cycle

**Lesson: What's in Our Soil?**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Core Investigation:** Comprehensive soil analysis

Students collect soil samples from multiple garden locations and conduct the following tests:

| Test | Method | What It Reveals |
|------|--------|----------------|
| **pH** | pH meter or test strips | Acidity/alkalinity; affects nutrient availability. Most vegetables prefer 6.0-7.0 |
| **Nitrogen (N)** | Chemical test kit | Essential for leaf growth; deficiency causes yellowing |
| **Phosphorus (P)** | Chemical test kit | Essential for root development and flowering |
| **Potassium (K)** | Chemical test kit | Essential for overall plant health and disease resistance |
| **Texture** | Jar test (shake soil with water; observe settling layers) | Sand, silt, clay proportions; affects drainage and nutrient retention |
| **Organic matter** | Visual observation and burn test | Humus content; affects water retention and microbial life |

**Data Analysis:** Students create a "soil report card" for each garden bed, identifying strengths and deficiencies.

**Chemical Reasoning:** "Soil pH affects nutrient availability because the chemical bonds between nutrients and soil particles change at different pH levels. In very acidic soil (pH < 5.5), some nutrients become toxic, while others become unavailable. This is why we test pH and amend with lime (to raise pH) or sulfur (to lower pH)."

**TRACE Protocol:**
*Think:* "What do you predict about our soil quality? What evidence from plant growth patterns supports your prediction?"
*Reason:* Conduct tests, record data, analyze patterns
*Articulate:* "Based on our soil analysis, what amendments does each bed need? How much of each amendment should we apply?"
*Check:* "Cross-reference our soil data with the growth performance of plants in each bed. Does the soil data explain the growth differences?"
*Extend:* "How does the N-P-K ratio on a fertilizer bag connect to what we've learned? When the bag says '10-10-10,' what does that mean?"

Reasoning move: **Connect** (link chemical properties of soil to observable plant growth; link soil science to agricultural practice)

## Unit 4: Physical Science in the Garden (Weeks 29-34)

### Essential Questions
- How do chemical reactions drive the living processes in our garden?
- How does light interact with plants, and why does it matter for garden design?
- How can understanding physical science make us better gardeners?

### Standards Alignment
- **MS-PS1-2**: Analyze properties of substances before and after chemical reactions
- **MS-PS1-5**: Develop and use a model to describe conservation of matter in chemical reactions
- **MS-PS4-2**: Develop and use a model to describe how waves are reflected, absorbed, or transmitted

### Lesson Sequence

#### Weeks 29-31: Chemistry of Composting and Decomposition

**Lesson: Composting as a Chemical Process**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** "When we put food scraps and yard waste into our compost bin, something transforms them into rich, dark soil. What chemical processes are driving this transformation?"

**Reason:** Students investigate composting as a multi-stage chemical process:

1. **Initial breakdown**: Bacteria and fungi break down simple compounds (sugars, starches) through aerobic respiration. Temperature rises to 130-160F in the center of an active pile.
2. **Thermophilic phase**: Heat-loving bacteria dominate, breaking down complex proteins, fats, and cellulose. This is when pathogens and weed seeds are killed.
3. **Cooling phase**: Temperature drops as easily digestible material is consumed. Fungi, actinomycetes, and larger organisms (worms, beetles) continue breakdown.
4. **Maturation**: Material stabilizes into humus -- stable organic matter that enriches soil.

**Investigation: Monitoring Compost Chemistry**
- Temperature: Insert a compost thermometer daily and graph the temperature curve over weeks
- pH: Test compost pH at different stages (starts acidic, becomes neutral as it matures)
- CO2 production: Place a candle near the compost surface -- if it goes out, CO2 (from respiration of decomposer organisms) is being produced at high rates
- Mass: Weigh the compost pile at the start and after 8 weeks. Where did the mass go? (Conservation of matter: mass was not destroyed but converted to CO2, H2O, and heat energy that left the system)

**Key Concept -- Conservation of Matter:** "The atoms that went into the compost pile as apple cores and leaves are the same atoms that come out as humus, carbon dioxide, and water. Matter is not created or destroyed -- it is transformed."

Reasoning move: **Transform** (understand how matter changes form while being conserved in total quantity)

---

#### Weeks 32-34: Light and the Garden

**Lesson: Why Are Plants Green? Light and Photosynthesis**

*Reflect Phase:*

**Think:** "Plants are green. But why green specifically? The answer involves the physics of light."

**Reason:** Investigation of light and color

*Experiment 1: Prism Decomposition*
- Use a prism to split white light into the visible spectrum
- Identify the colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet
- "White light contains all these colors. When light hits a leaf, what happens to each color?"

*Experiment 2: Light Absorption by Leaves*
- Extract chlorophyll from garden leaves using rubbing alcohol (teacher-led extraction)
- Shine white light through the chlorophyll solution
- Observe: red and blue light are absorbed; green light passes through
- "Plants are green because chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light for photosynthesis and reflects green light back to our eyes"

*Experiment 3: Growing Under Different Light Colors*
- Grow identical plants under red, blue, green, and white light (using colored cellophane filters)
- Measure growth after 3 weeks
- Prediction: Plants under red and blue light will grow best (these wavelengths are most useful for photosynthesis); plants under green light will grow poorly (green light is reflected, not absorbed)

**Articulate:** "How does understanding light absorption help us design better gardens? Think about placement of plants relative to shade structures, the use of reflective mulch, and the design of greenhouses."

**Extend:** "Some specialized greenhouses use red and blue LED lights instead of full-spectrum lights. Based on what we've learned, why would this be more energy-efficient?"

Reasoning move: **Connect** (link physics of light to biology of photosynthesis to engineering of greenhouse design)

## Engineering Design Challenges

### Challenge 1: Irrigation System Design (Weeks 9-10, integrated with water cycle unit)

**Problem Statement:** "Our garden currently relies on hand-watering with hoses, which wastes water and requires daily human labor. Design, build, and test a drip irrigation system for one garden bed that delivers water efficiently and evenly."

**Criteria:**
- Must deliver water to all plants in the bed
- Must use at least 30% less water than hand-watering (measured over one week)
- Must be buildable from available materials (PVC pipe, drip emitters, connectors, timer)
- Must require no more than 5 minutes of daily human interaction

**Constraints:**
- Budget: $50 maximum
- Must connect to existing water supply
- Must not create standing water (mosquito prevention)

**Engineering Design Process:**

| Phase | Student Activities | TRACE Connection |
|-------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Define | Research water needs of crops in the bed; calculate daily water requirements | Think: What do we need? |
| Design | Sketch at least 3 possible designs; evaluate using criteria matrix | Reason: What approaches could work? |
| Build | Construct prototype of best design | Reason: Execute the plan |
| Test | Run the system for one week; measure water usage and plant health | Check: Does it work? |
| Evaluate | Compare water usage to hand-watering; assess evenness of distribution | Verify: Did we meet our criteria? |
| Redesign | Modify based on test results; retest | Extend: How can we improve? |
| Present | Share design, data, and conclusions with class and garden partners | Articulate: Explain our reasoning |

---

### Challenge 2: Season Extension Structure (Weeks 20-22)

**Problem Statement:** "Design and build a structure that extends the growing season by at least 4 weeks in fall and/or spring by protecting plants from frost."

**Options to explore:** Cold frames, row covers, hoop houses, water-wall thermal mass systems

**Scientific Principles Applied:**
- Heat transfer (conduction, convection, radiation)
- Greenhouse effect (trapping infrared radiation)
- Thermal mass (water stores heat during the day and releases it at night)
- Insulation (reducing heat loss)

**Testing Protocol:** Students place identical thermometers inside and outside their structure. Graph temperature differences over 24-hour cycles. Calculate the degree of frost protection provided.

## Interdisciplinary Projects

### Project 1: The Food Justice Investigation (Weeks 6-10)

This project integrates science, mathematics, literacy, and social studies through an investigation of food access in the school's community.

**Science Component:**
- Nutritional analysis of garden-grown produce versus processed food alternatives
- Investigation of food preservation methods (dehydrating, canning -- chemical and physical changes)
- Soil quality comparison between community garden areas and vacant lots

**Mathematics Component:**
- Map food sources within walking distance of the school (GIS mapping)
- Calculate the cost per nutrient of garden food versus fast food
- Analyze demographic data and food access statistics for the neighborhood
- Create data visualizations (graphs, charts, infographics)

**Literacy Component:**
- Read and analyze informational texts about food deserts and food justice
- Conduct interviews with community members about food access
- Write an argumentative essay or produce a documentary about food justice in the community
- Research the history of community gardens as social justice initiatives

**Social Studies Component:**
- Investigate the historical and economic factors that create food deserts
- Explore cultural food traditions in the community
- Analyze policy solutions (zoning, subsidies, community programs)
- Connect to civil rights history and environmental justice movements

**TRACE Protocol Across Disciplines:**

| Phase | Science | Math | Literacy | Social Studies |
|-------|---------|------|----------|---------------|
| **Think** | What nutritional differences exist? | What data do we need to collect? | What do we already know about food access? | What historical factors are at play? |
| **Reason** | Design nutritional analyses | Calculate and compare costs | Read and analyze sources | Investigate economic and policy factors |

### Project 2: The Climate Garden (Weeks 15-20)

Students investigate how climate change affects agriculture and design a garden adapted to projected future conditions.

**Phase 1 -- Research (Weeks 15-16):**
- Study climate projections for the local region (temperature, precipitation, growing season length)
- Research which crops are adapted to current versus projected conditions
- Investigate agricultural adaptations being used globally (drought-resistant varieties, shade structures, water conservation)

**Phase 2 -- Design (Weeks 17-18):**
- Select crops adapted to projected conditions
- Design irrigation and water conservation systems
- Plan for extreme weather events (heavy rain, heat waves, early/late frosts)
- Create a mathematical model projecting garden yield under different climate scenarios

**Phase 3 -- Implementation and Communication (Weeks 19-20):**
- Plant a "climate garden" test plot with adapted varieties
- Write a scientific report with data-based recommendations
- Present findings at a school or community event
- Connect to larger conversations about climate adaptation and mitigation

### Project 3: The Garden Ecosystem Documentary (Weeks 28-34)

Students produce a short documentary film about the school garden ecosystem, integrating all scientific concepts from the year.

**Required Segments:**

| Segment | Content | Scientific Concepts |
|---------|---------|-------------------|
| "The Invisible World" | Microscopy footage and narration about plant cells and soil organisms | Cell biology, microscopy |
| "Capturing Sunlight" | Explanation of photosynthesis with garden demonstrations | Photosynthesis, light physics |
| "The Web of Life" | Food web visualization using garden organisms | Ecology, biodiversity |
| "Underground Chemistry" | Soil science and composting investigation | Chemistry, decomposition |
| "Water's Journey" | Tracing water through the garden and water cycle | Earth science, water cycle |
| "Growing a Future" | Sustainability practices and climate adaptation | Environmental science, engineering |

**Skills Developed:**
- Scientific communication and accuracy
- Narrative structure and storytelling
- Technical skills (filming, editing, graphics)
- Collaborative project management
- Public speaking and presentation

**Assessment:** The documentary is evaluated using a rubric that assesses both scientific accuracy and communication effectiveness. It is screened at a school assembly or community event, providing an authentic audience for student work.

## Differentiation Across Science Units

| Strategy | Approaching Learners | On-Level Learners | Advanced Learners |
|----------|---------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Vocabulary** | Pre-teach key terms with visual supports; provide glossaries | Learn vocabulary in context; use academic language in explanations | Research and use advanced terminology; explain concepts to peers |
| **Lab Work** | Provide step-by-step procedural guides; pair with peer mentors; offer simplified data sets | Follow investigation procedures with some independence; collect and analyze own data | Design their own investigations; identify and control additional variables |
| **Reading** | Adapted texts at accessible reading level; graphic organizers for comprehension | Grade-level scientific articles and texts | Primary scientific literature (adapted); independent research |
| **Writing** | Sentence starters and paragraph frames for lab reports; oral explanations accepted | Complete lab reports with structure provided; argumentative paragraphs | Extended scientific arguments; grant proposals; publishable-quality reports |
| **Mathematics** | Calculator use for all calculations; simplified data sets; focus on concepts over computation | Integrate mathematical analysis throughout; multiple representations | Advanced statistical analysis; mathematical modeling; connect to high school content |
| **Assessment** | Demonstrate understanding through models, diagrams, or oral explanation | Performance tasks requiring written scientific arguments | Design and conduct independent investigations; present at science fairs |
