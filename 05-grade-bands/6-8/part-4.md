# 6-8 Grade Band: Social-Emotional Learning & Identity Development

## Overview

Social-emotional learning in the 6-8 RootWork curriculum addresses the most complex and consequential developmental terrain of early adolescence: the construction of identity. Middle school students are not simply learning to manage their emotions or get along with peers -- they are building a sense of self that will shape their trajectories for decades. They are asking, consciously and unconsciously, "Who am I? Where do I belong? What am I good at? Do I matter?" For students with trauma histories, these questions carry additional weight, because trauma often provides its own devastating answers: "I am damaged. I don't belong anywhere. I am not good enough. I don't matter."

The RootWork 6-8 SEL curriculum provides a counternarrative through the garden. The garden is a place where every student can contribute, where growth is visible and undeniable, where setbacks are natural and recoverable, and where caring for something living generates a sense of purpose and competence that no worksheet or pep talk can replicate. The curriculum systematically develops each of the five CASEL competencies through garden-based activities, while simultaneously building the advanced regulation skills that adolescents need to navigate an increasingly complex emotional landscape.

Critically, the SEL curriculum is not a separate program added to academic instruction. It is woven into every aspect of the 5Rs Framework -- the Root phase builds self-awareness, the Regulate phase develops self-management, the Reflect phase cultivates responsible decision-making, the Restore phase builds resilience and growth mindset, and the Reconnect phase strengthens relationship skills and social awareness. Every academic lesson is also an SEL lesson, because every academic interaction is a social-emotional experience.

## CASEL Framework Alignment for 6-8

### 1. Self-Awareness

**Definition:** The ability to accurately recognize one's own emotions, thoughts, values, and their influence on behavior. Includes an accurate assessment of one's strengths and limitations, a well-grounded sense of confidence, and a growth-oriented mindset.

**Developmental Context:** Adolescents are developing a more sophisticated understanding of their inner lives. They can recognize mixed emotions ("I'm excited about the project but nervous about presenting"), identify patterns in their emotional responses ("I always get anxious before tests"), and begin to understand how their emotions influence their behavior ("When I'm frustrated, I tend to shut down instead of asking for help").

**6-8 Garden Integration:**

| Skill | Activity | 5Rs Connection |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Emotion identification (complex) | "Emotion Wheel" journal entries -- students identify their emotion from a wheel with 72+ options, moving beyond basic categories to nuanced states (apprehensive vs. terrified, content vs. elated, irritated vs. enraged) | Root Phase |
| Self-assessment accuracy | Regulation self-rating (1-5 scale) calibrated against observable indicators; students learn to match their self-assessment to their actual functioning | Root Phase |
| Strength recognition | "Garden Strengths Inventory" -- students identify their contributions to the garden community (the careful observer, the strong digger, the creative designer, the patient researcher, the encouraging partner) | Reconnect Phase |
| Growth mindset deepening | Error journals that track mistakes, analysis, and subsequent learning; "Compost Portfolios" where students transform failures into growth | Restore Phase |
| Values exploration | "What matters to me?" journal reflections connecting garden experiences to personal values (sustainability, community, fairness, beauty, hard work, creativity) | Reconnect Phase |
| Identity mapping | "Root System" identity maps -- students draw their identity as a root system, with each root representing a dimension of who they are (cultural background, interests, relationships, values, strengths, challenges) | Reconnect Phase |

**Detailed Activity: The Root System Identity Map**

This activity uses the metaphor of a plant's root system to explore the multiple dimensions of identity.

*Materials:* Large paper (18x24), colored markers or pencils, garden journals

*Procedure:*
1. Draw a plant above ground -- this represents what others see (name, appearance, public self)
2. Draw the root system below ground -- each root represents a dimension of identity that may not be visible
3. Label roots:
   - Cultural/family roots: "Where my family comes from; traditions we keep; languages we speak"
   - Relationship roots: "People who matter to me; communities I belong to"
   - Interest roots: "Things I care about; what I'm passionate about"
   - Strength roots: "What I'm good at; skills I'm developing"
   - Challenge roots: "What's hard for me; what I'm working on" (optional -- never required)
   - Value roots: "What I believe is important; how I want to treat others"
   - Dream roots: "What I hope for; who I'm becoming"
4. Share selected roots with a partner (students choose which roots to share -- nothing is mandatory)
5. Class discussion: "A plant's strength comes from its root system. The deeper and more diverse the roots, the stronger the plant. How is that true for people too?"

*Trauma-Informed Considerations:*
- Students are never required to share personal information. Choice and control are paramount.
- Some students may have complicated relationships with family or cultural roots. Offer alternatives: "You can include chosen family, mentors, or communities you want to be part of."
- The "challenge roots" dimension is always optional. For trauma-affected students, identifying challenges publicly can feel threatening. They may share privately with the educator or journal about it instead.
- Students can revisit and revise their root maps throughout the year as their identities develop.

### 2. Self-Management

**Definition:** The ability to successfully regulate one's emotions, thoughts, and behaviors in different situations -- effectively managing stress, controlling impulses, and motivating oneself. Includes setting and working toward personal and academic goals.

**Developmental Context:** Adolescents have the cognitive capacity to understand regulation strategies but are still developing the executive function to consistently implement them. The gap between "knowing what to do" and "doing it in the moment" is wide during early adolescence. The curriculum bridges this gap through explicit instruction, repeated practice, and the development of personalized regulation plans.

**Advanced Regulation Strategies for 6-8:**

#### Mindfulness Meditation

**Progressive Sequence:**

| Week | Practice | Duration | Garden Connection |
|------|----------|----------|-------------------|
| 1-2 | Guided breathing meditation (count breaths, focus on a garden object) | 2 min | Focus on a single plant; notice its details as an anchor |
| 3-4 | Body scan meditation (notice sensations from feet to head without judgment) | 3 min | Connect body awareness to the body's role in garden work |
| 5-6 | Mindful observation (sit in the garden and notice without labeling or judging) | 4 min | Garden observation becomes the meditation practice itself |
| 7-8 | Loving-kindness meditation (send good wishes to self, someone close, someone neutral, someone difficult) | 5 min | Connect to the garden community; "Just as we tend the garden with care, we can tend our relationships with care" |
| 9-12 | Student-selected practice (choose from the above based on personal preference) | 3-5 min | Students develop a personal mindfulness practice using garden as anchor |

*Implementation Notes:*
- Mindfulness is always invitational, never required. Students who are uncomfortable closing their eyes may keep a soft gaze on a garden object.
- For trauma-affected students, body scan meditations can sometimes trigger distress (drawing attention to the body may activate body-based trauma memories). Offer alternatives: external focus meditations (garden sounds, visual details) instead of internal focus.
- Students are never asked to "empty their minds." Instead, they are asked to notice what arises and gently return attention to the anchor.

#### Cognitive Reframing

**Teaching Sequence:**

*Week 1: Introduce the concept*
"Our thoughts create our feelings. The same event can produce different feelings depending on how we think about it."

*Garden demonstration:* "Imagine you plant 20 seeds and only 12 sprout. Here are two ways to think about that:"
- Thought A: "I'm a terrible gardener. I can't even grow seeds." --> Feeling: Shame, discouragement
- Thought B: "A 60% germination rate gives me 12 plants to work with, and I can investigate why some seeds didn't sprout." --> Feeling: Curiosity, determination

"Both thoughts are about the same event. But they produce very different feelings and very different next steps."

*Weeks 2-3: Practice with garden scenarios*

| Situation | Automatic Thought | Reframed Thought |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Your plant experiment failed | "I'm stupid" | "Now I know one approach that doesn't work. What can I change?" |
| Your partner disagrees with your garden design | "They don't respect me" | "They have a different perspective. I can listen and find a compromise" |
| You got a low score on the science test | "I'll never be good at science" | "This tells me what I need to study more. I can grow from this" |
| Your garden bed produced less than others | "I'm the worst at everything" | "What variables were different? This is a real scientific question" |

*Weeks 4-8: Apply to real situations*
Students identify automatic thoughts in their error journals and practice reframing them. The key insight: "Reframing doesn't mean pretending everything is fine. It means finding the most accurate and most useful way to think about what happened."

#### Stress Inoculation

**Concept:** Gradually exposing students to manageable levels of stress while teaching them to apply coping strategies, building resilience for higher-stress situations.

**Garden-Based Implementation:**

| Level | Stressor | Regulation Strategy Practiced |
|-------|----------|------------------------------|
| **Low** | Present garden observations to a partner | Deep breathing before speaking; self-talk ("I know this material") |
| **Medium** | Present experiment results to the class | Preparation strategies; visualization; positive self-talk |
| **High** | Present the sustainability project to a community panel | Full regulation toolkit: breathing, cognitive reframing, physical grounding, peer support |

"We practice in progressively more challenging situations, just as a plant gradually hardens off when moved from the greenhouse to the outdoor garden. We don't throw seedlings into a storm -- we give them increasing exposure to wind and sun so they develop strength."

#### Body-Based Regulation

**Strategies Specific to Adolescents:**

| Strategy | Description | When to Use | Garden Connection |
|----------|-------------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Progressive muscle relaxation** | Tense and release muscle groups systematically | High tension, before assessments | "Squeeze the soil like making a seed ball, then release" |
| **Bilateral tapping** | Alternate tapping knees or crossing arms and tapping shoulders | Anxiety, racing thoughts | "Like alternating hands while planting seeds in a row" |
| **Cold water regulation** | Splash cold water on face or hold a cold water bottle | Acute distress, anger escalation | "The garden hose on a hot day -- instant reset" |
| **Vigorous movement** | Digging, carrying, wheelbarrow work, garden construction | Excess energy, agitation | Built into garden work days |
| **Slow, rhythmic movement** | Raking patterns, careful weeding, seed sorting | Hyperarousal, racing thoughts | Built into garden maintenance |
| **Grounding through senses** | 5-4-3-2-1 (name 5 things you see, 4 you touch, 3 you hear, 2 you smell, 1 you taste) | Dissociation, flashbacks, derealization | Garden provides rich sensory input for all five senses |

### 3. Social Awareness

**Definition:** The ability to take the perspective of and empathize with others, including those from diverse backgrounds and cultures. Includes understanding social and ethical norms for behavior and recognizing family, school, and community resources and supports.

**6-8 Garden Integration:**

| Skill | Activity | 5Rs Connection |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Perspective-taking | Role-play garden scenarios from different perspectives (farmer, consumer, policymaker, pollinator) | Reflect Phase |
| Empathy development | Interview community members about food access; listen to understand, not to respond | Reconnect Phase |
| Cultural appreciation | Research and grow crops from students' cultural backgrounds; explore the role of food in cultural identity | Reconnect Phase |
| Community awareness | Map food resources in the neighborhood; identify who has access and who doesn't | Reconnect Phase |
| Environmental empathy | Consider the needs of non-human organisms in garden design (pollinators, soil organisms, birds) | Reflect Phase |
| Systemic awareness | Investigate food systems -- who grows our food, under what conditions, and who benefits? | Reconnect Phase |

**Detailed Activity: The Three Perspectives Garden Debate**

*Format:* Structured academic controversy

*Scenario:* "A vacant lot in the neighborhood could be used for three purposes: a community garden, a parking lot for a new grocery store, or affordable housing. Each option addresses a real community need."

*Procedure:*
1. Divide into three groups, each assigned a position
2. Research the position thoroughly (each group receives a fact sheet)
3. Present the strongest case for your position (5 minutes per group)
4. Other groups ask questions (3 minutes)
5. Groups switch positions -- argue for a different option (forces perspective-taking)
6. Final discussion: "What is the best solution? Is there a way to address all three needs? What values are in conflict?"

*SEL Learning:* Students practice taking perspectives they may not personally hold. They discover that reasonable people can disagree based on different values and priorities. They practice listening to understand rather than listening to argue.

*Trauma-Informed Note:* Some students may have personal connections to housing insecurity or food insecurity. The educator monitors for emotional activation and provides regulation support. No student is asked to share personal experiences related to the topic unless they choose to.

### 4. Relationship Skills

**Definition:** The ability to establish and maintain healthy and supportive relationships and to effectively navigate settings with diverse individuals and groups. Includes clear communication, active listening, cooperation, conflict resolution, and constructive negotiation.

**6-8 Garden Integration:**

| Skill | Activity | 5Rs Connection |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Active listening | Structured partner shares with reflection ("What I heard you say was...") | Root/Reflect Phase |
| Constructive feedback | Peer assessment of garden projects using rubric-based feedback protocols | Restore Phase |
| Conflict resolution | "Garden Agreements" mediation process for peer disputes | Regulate Phase |
| Collaborative problem-solving | Team-based garden design challenges requiring consensus | Reflect Phase |
| Healthy boundary-setting | Choosing which identity roots to share; saying "I pass" in sharing circles | Root Phase |
| Mentorship | Peer mentorship program (described below); teaching younger students | Reconnect Phase |

#### Peer Mentorship Program Design

**Structure:** Each 7th or 8th grader is paired with a 6th grader (or a K-2/3-5 student in cross-grade schools) as a "Garden Mentor."

**Mentor Training (4 sessions):**

| Session | Content | Skills Practiced |
|---------|---------|-----------------|
| **Session 1: What Is Mentoring?** | Discuss the difference between helping and mentoring; identify mentor qualities; set expectations | Active listening role-plays; "What would you do if..." scenarios |
| **Session 2: Regulation Support** | How to recognize when someone is dysregulated; what to do and what not to do; when to get adult help | Practice co-regulation techniques; role-play dysregulation scenarios |
| **Session 3: Teaching and Learning** | How to explain without taking over; how to ask guiding questions instead of giving answers; patience strategies | Practice "ask, don't tell" approaches with garden tasks |
| **Session 4: Relationship Building** | How to build trust; respecting boundaries; managing the mentor relationship when it's difficult | Practice conversation starters; discuss confidentiality and its limits |

**Ongoing Mentor Activities:**
- Weekly mentoring sessions during garden time (20 minutes)
- Joint garden projects (planting, maintaining a shared bed)
- Mentor journal reflections: "What did I learn about teaching? About patience? About myself?"
- Monthly mentor circle (all mentors meet to share experiences, troubleshoot challenges, celebrate successes)

**SEL Outcomes:**
- Mentors develop leadership, empathy, patience, and communication skills
- Mentees receive additional relational support, academic scaffolding, and a sense of belonging
- Both develop a sense of purpose and contribution to the community

**Trauma-Informed Considerations for Mentorship:**
- Mentors are never responsible for another student's emotional well-being -- they are support, not therapists
- Clear protocols for when to involve an adult (signs of distress, disclosure of harm, safety concerns)
- Pairings are made thoughtfully with input from counselors and teachers
- Mentors who are themselves trauma-affected may find the experience healing (contributing to others builds self-efficacy) but may also need additional support (regular check-ins with the program coordinator)

### 5. Responsible Decision-Making

**Definition:** The ability to make caring and constructive choices about personal behavior and social interactions across diverse situations. Includes considering ethical standards, safety concerns, social norms, the realistic evaluation of consequences, and the well-being of self and others.

**6-8 Garden Integration:**

| Skill | Activity | 5Rs Connection |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Ethical reasoning | Garden dilemmas (pesticide use, water conservation vs. plant survival, sharing harvest equitably) | Reflect Phase |
| Consequence evaluation | "If-then" analysis of garden management decisions | Reflect/Reconnect |
| Evidence-based decision-making | Using data to make garden planning decisions (what to plant, where, how much) | Reflect Phase |
| Community impact consideration | Service learning projects require analyzing who benefits and who might be affected | Reconnect Phase |
| Self-advocacy | Students identify their own learning needs and communicate them to educators | Root/Restore Phase |
| Future orientation | Career exploration through garden connections; goal-setting for personal growth | Reconnect Phase |

**Detailed Activity: The Pesticide Dilemma**

*Scenario:* "Aphids are destroying our pepper plants. We have several options:"

1. **Synthetic pesticide**: Fast, effective, but may harm beneficial insects and contaminate soil
2. **Organic pesticide (neem oil)**: Slower, less harmful, but still kills some beneficial insects
3. **Biological control**: Release ladybugs (natural predators). Slow, uncertain, but harmless to the ecosystem
4. **Companion planting**: Plant marigolds and basil nearby to repel aphids. Preventive, not a cure for current infestation
5. **Do nothing**: Accept some crop loss; the ecosystem may self-correct over time

*Decision-Making Framework:*

| Option | Effectiveness | Ecological Impact | Cost | Time | Ethical Considerations |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|------|------|----------------------|
| Synthetic pesticide | High | High negative | Medium | Immediate | Harms other organisms; chemical residue |
| Neem oil | Medium | Low negative | Low | 1-2 weeks | Some collateral damage to beneficials |
| Ladybugs | Medium | Positive | Medium | 2-4 weeks | Supports biodiversity; uncertain outcome |
| Companion planting | Low (preventive) | Positive | Low | Long-term | Best for future prevention; won't save current crop |
| Do nothing | Variable | Neutral | None | Variable | Accepts loss; trusts ecosystem |

Students analyze the options, discuss in groups, present their recommendations with evidence and reasoning, and vote as a class. The class implements the chosen solution and monitors results.

*Key SEL Learning:* "Good decisions consider multiple factors -- not just what's fastest or cheapest, but what's best for the whole community, including the non-human members of our garden ecosystem."

## Trauma-Responsive Practices for Adolescence

### Understanding Re-Enactment

Adolescents with trauma histories may unconsciously re-create familiar relational dynamics from their trauma experiences. This phenomenon, called "re-enactment," can manifest as:

- **Provoking rejection**: A student who has experienced abandonment may push away caring adults or peers, testing whether they will leave. If the adult responds with rejection (removing the student from class, expressing frustration, giving up), the student's belief that "people always leave" is confirmed.
- **Seeking conflict**: A student accustomed to chaotic or violent environments may create conflict in calm settings, because chaos feels more familiar (and therefore more predictable) than peace.
- **Parentification reversal**: A student who was forced to be a caretaker may either continue caretaking (over-functioning for peers, trying to "fix" the teacher) or reject all responsibility (under-functioning as a reaction against the earlier role).
- **Power dynamics**: A student who has been controlled or dominated may either become highly controlling (micromanaging group projects, refusing to compromise) or highly submissive (agreeing to everything, never expressing preferences).

**Curriculum Response to Re-Enactment:**

| Re-Enactment Pattern | Educator Response | Garden-Based Intervention |
|----------------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Provoking rejection | Stay consistent. "I'm not going anywhere. I'm still here tomorrow." | Assign the student a long-term garden responsibility (a perennial plant that needs year-round care) -- modeling that some relationships endure |
| Creating chaos | Maintain calm and structure without escalation. "I can see something is hard right now. Let's take a garden walk." | Channel energy into vigorous garden work (digging, building); the garden's natural order provides counter-evidence to chaos |
| Over-functioning / caretaking | Gently redirect: "It's wonderful that you want to help. Right now, it's your turn to focus on your own work." | Distinguish between "your plant" (personal responsibility) and "the community garden" (shared responsibility with limits) |
| Controlling behavior | Offer structured choices within boundaries: "You get to decide A or B. I'm deciding C because that's my job as the educator." | Garden roles rotate; everyone leads sometimes and follows sometimes |
| Extreme submission | Create low-stakes opportunities for voice: "What do you want to plant this season?" Build toward larger expressions of preference | Start with choices about personal garden space; build toward group voice |

### Addressing Secondary Trauma

Educators working with trauma-affected populations are at risk for secondary traumatic stress (STS) -- the emotional toll of witnessing others' suffering. The RootWork curriculum includes educator wellness protocols:

**Prevention Strategies:**
- Regular reflective supervision with a trained mental health professional
- "Garden time" for educators -- personal time in the garden for their own regulation and restoration
- Clear boundaries around educator-student emotional engagement (caring without carrying)
- Professional development on recognizing STS symptoms (exhaustion, hypervigilance, emotional numbness, cynicism)
- Peer support among educators implementing the curriculum

**Signs of Secondary Trauma in Educators:**
- Dreading going to work or dreading interactions with specific students
- Difficulty leaving work at work; intrusive thoughts about students outside of school hours
- Emotional numbness or heightened emotionality that is atypical for the individual
- Physical symptoms: headaches, stomach problems, sleep disruption, fatigue
- Cynicism about the curriculum, the students, or the possibility of change

**Response Protocol:**
- Non-judgmental acknowledgment: "This work is hard. What you're feeling is a normal response to an abnormal amount of exposure to others' pain."
- Referral to employee assistance programs or personal therapy
- Temporary redistribution of support responsibilities
- Reconnection to the purpose of the work: "Walk through the garden. Look at what your students have grown."

### Building Mentor Relationships

For trauma-affected adolescents, a relationship with one reliable, caring adult can be the most powerful protective factor. The RootWork curriculum intentionally builds these relationships:

**Educator Practices:**
- Learn every student's name and something about their life outside school within the first two weeks
- Remember and reference what students share: "Last week you mentioned your grandmother's garden. Did you get to visit this weekend?"
- Show up consistently: same schedule, same rituals, same expectations, same warmth
- Repair after rupture: "I was short-tempered yesterday, and I'm sorry. That wasn't about you. I'm committed to doing better."
- Provide unconditional positive regard: separate behavior from identity. "You made a choice that didn't work out. That doesn't change who you are in this community."
- Celebrate growth publicly and address challenges privately

**Garden-Specific Relationship Building:**
- Work alongside students in the garden (not directing from the sideline)
- Share your own experiences with growth and failure in the garden: "I killed three tomato plants last summer by overwatering. I had to learn the hard way."
- Create traditions: "Remember last year when we grew the biggest pumpkin in the school's history? That was your class."
- Involve students in real decisions: "We have this budget for spring planting. What do you think we should prioritize?"

## Garden-Based Therapeutic Activities

These activities are designed to be used within the curriculum as part of regular instruction, not as therapy sessions. However, they have therapeutic value and are informed by horticultural therapy principles.

### Activity 1: The Resilience Garden

**Purpose:** Explore the concept of resilience through the observation of resilient plants.

**Procedure:**
1. Walk the garden and find plants that have survived adversity -- a tomato plant that grew back after being broken, a weed pushing through a crack, a tree that grew around an obstacle.
2. Document these "resilient plants" with photos and written observations.
3. Journal prompt: "This plant shows resilience because ___ . A time I showed resilience was when ___ ." (Second prompt is optional.)
4. Discuss: "What conditions help plants bounce back? What conditions help people bounce back?"

**Garden Metaphor:** "Resilience isn't about avoiding damage. It's about growing back, sometimes in a new direction. The strongest wood in a tree is found where the tree healed from a wound."

### Activity 2: The Propagation Project

**Purpose:** Use plant propagation as a metaphor for starting over and creating new growth from existing material.

**Procedure:**
1. Teach students to propagate plants from cuttings (succulents, herbs like rosemary or basil, pothos).
2. Each student takes a cutting and roots it in water or soil.
3. Journal prompt: "A cutting starts from a piece of something that already exists. It's not starting from nothing -- it carries the DNA and strength of the parent plant. But it also has the chance to grow in its own way, in a new container, in new soil."
4. Students care for their propagation for the semester, documenting growth.

**Therapeutic Value:** For students who feel defined by their past, the propagation metaphor offers a powerful reframe: "You carry strengths from where you came from. And you also have the chance to grow in your own way."

### Activity 3: The Sensory Meditation Garden

**Purpose:** Create a dedicated garden space designed for regulation and emotional processing.

**Student-Designed Elements:**
- **Touch**: Lamb's ear (soft leaves), ornamental grasses (flowing texture), smooth stones
- **Smell**: Lavender, rosemary, mint, chamomile
- **Sound**: Wind chimes, water feature (small fountain or drip system), rustling grasses
- **Sight**: Flowers in calming colors (blues, purples, whites), interesting foliage, focal points
- **Taste**: Edible herbs for mindful tasting (mint, lemon balm, basil)

Students research, design, install, and maintain the sensory garden. It becomes a permanent regulation resource for the entire school community.

### Activity 4: The Legacy Plot

**Purpose:** Explore the concept of leaving a positive mark and contributing to something larger than oneself.

**Procedure:**
1. Each class plants a perennial bed, a fruit tree, or a permanent garden structure that will outlast their time at the school.
2. Create a "legacy marker" -- a garden stone, plaque, or art piece that identifies the class and what they contributed.
3. Visit and maintain legacy plots from previous years' classes.
4. Journal prompt: "What do you want to leave behind? Not just in the garden, but in the world?"

**Therapeutic Value:** For students who feel insignificant or invisible, the legacy plot provides concrete evidence that their actions have lasting impact. They can return years later and see what they planted still growing.

## Progress Monitoring for SEL: 6-8

### Self-Report Instruments

**Weekly Regulation Tracker (Student Self-Report):**

Students complete a brief weekly check-in in their garden journals:

| Question | Scale | Frequency |
|----------|-------|-----------|
| "How well did I manage my emotions this week?" | 1 (struggled significantly) to 5 (managed well) | Weekly |
| "How well did I work with my peers?" | 1-5 | Weekly |
| "Did I use a regulation strategy when I needed one?" | Yes / Sometimes / No | Weekly |
| "What was my most common emotion this week?" | Open-ended (from emotion wheel) | Weekly |
| "What regulation strategy worked best for me?" | Open-ended | Weekly |
| "What's one thing I'm proud of?" | Open-ended | Weekly |

**Quarterly SEL Self-Assessment:**

Students rate themselves on each CASEL competency using garden metaphors:

- **Seed** (just starting): "I'm beginning to develop this skill"
- **Sprout** (emerging): "I can do this sometimes with support"
- **Growing** (developing): "I can usually do this on my own"
- **Blooming** (proficient): "This is a strength of mine and I can help others"

| Competency | Indicators (Student-Friendly Language) | Self-Rating |
|------------|---------------------------------------|------------|
| **Self-Awareness** | I can name my emotions accurately. I know my strengths. I notice when I'm getting overwhelmed. | Seed / Sprout / Growing / Blooming |
| **Self-Management** | I can calm myself down when I'm upset. I can focus on tasks even when I'm distracted. I set goals and work toward them. | Seed / Sprout / Growing / Blooming |
| **Social Awareness** | I can understand how others are feeling. I respect people who are different from me. I notice when someone needs help. | Seed / Sprout / Growing / Blooming |
| **Relationship Skills** | I can work well in groups. I can solve conflicts without making them worse. I can give and receive feedback. | Seed / Sprout / Growing / Blooming |
| **Responsible Decision-Making** | I think about consequences before I act. I consider how my choices affect others. I make ethical choices even when no one is watching. | Seed / Sprout / Growing / Blooming |

### Educator Observation Tools

**Monthly SEL Observation Record:**

Educators observe and document evidence of SEL competency development for each student. This is not a checklist completed for every student every month -- it is a running record of notable observations, positive or concerning, gathered during regular instruction.

| Date | Student | Observation | CASEL Domain | Context | Follow-Up Needed? |
|------|---------|-------------|-------------|---------|-------------------|
| 10/3 | Marcus | Used deep breathing independently before presenting his garden design. Self-regulated without prompting. | Self-Management | Sustainability project presentation | No -- celebrate growth |
| 10/5 | Aisha | Noticed that Jaylen was upset during group work and quietly checked in with him. | Social Awareness | Ecology lab | No -- note empathy development |
| 10/8 | Devon | Shut down when receiving peer feedback on his lab report. Left the room for 10 minutes. | Self-Management | Restore phase, peer review | Yes -- check in individually; review regulation plan |
| 10/12 | Sophia | Mediated a disagreement between two group members about garden design choices. Used compromise language. | Relationship Skills | Design challenge | No -- consider for mentor program |

### Regulation Pattern Tracking

For students with identified regulation challenges (IEP/504, known trauma history, or emerging concerns), educators maintain a more detailed regulation tracking log:

| Date | Time | Trigger (if identifiable) | Regulation State | Strategy Used | Outcome | Duration |
|------|------|--------------------------|-----------------|---------------|---------|----------|
| | | | Hyper / Hypo / Regulated | | Returned to baseline / Partially regulated / Needed additional support | |

This data is used for:
- Identifying patterns (time of day, triggers, effective strategies)
- Informing individualized regulation plans
- Documenting progress for IEP/504 meetings
- Communicating with families and therapeutic supports

## Family Engagement for Adolescent SEL

### Communication About SEL

Families of middle school students need to understand what SEL is and why it matters, particularly because some may perceive SEL as intrusive or as a distraction from academic content.

**Key Messages for Families:**
- "SEL is not therapy. It is the development of skills that research shows are critical for academic success, career readiness, and healthy relationships."
- "Your child's emotional development is as important as their academic development. They are inseparable -- a child who can manage their emotions learns more effectively."
- "The garden provides a safe, natural context for practicing skills like communication, problem-solving, and perseverance."
- "You are your child's most important teacher of social-emotional skills. The curriculum builds on what families already teach."

**Family SEL Resources:**
- Monthly newsletter including "conversation starters" that connect garden SEL themes to home discussions
- Quarterly "family garden days" that model regulation strategies and collaborative problem-solving
- Resource list for families experiencing stress (community mental health, food assistance, housing support)
- Optional parent workshops on adolescent brain development and regulation support
