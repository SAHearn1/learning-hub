# 6-8 Restore Phase: Systematic Error Analysis

## Overview

The Restore phase for grades 6-8 addresses the essential question: **"What can I learn from this mistake?"** At this developmental stage, error analysis becomes a distinct, teachable skill. Students move beyond simply correcting errors to systematically classifying, analyzing, and learning from them. The five error types -- computational, procedural, conceptual, strategic, and reading/interpretation -- become a shared vocabulary that destigmatizes mistakes and reframes them as data. Garden troubleshooting provides rich, authentic context for error analysis where the consequences are visible and the recovery process is tangible.

**Duration:** 5-10 minutes per session
**Core Principle:** Errors are not failures. They are diagnostic information that reveals what the brain is doing and where to adjust.

---

## The Five Error Types

### Classification Framework

| Error Type | Definition | What It Reveals | Example (Math) | Example (Garden) |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Computational** | Arithmetic or calculation mistake; the method was right but a number was wrong | Attention, fatigue, or rushing | 3/4 + 1/2 = 4/6 (added numerators and denominators) | Measured 18 inches but recorded 18 cm |
| **Procedural** | Steps were done in the wrong order or a step was skipped | Incomplete internalization of process | Solved for x before distributing the negative sign | Added fertilizer before testing soil pH |
| **Conceptual** | Fundamental misunderstanding of the underlying idea | A gap in foundational knowledge | Believes that multiplying always makes a number bigger | Believes all plants need the same amount of water |
| **Strategic** | Chose the wrong approach or method entirely | Difficulty with problem selection and planning | Used addition when the problem required proportional reasoning | Tried to propagate from seed when stem cutting would be faster |
| **Reading/Interpretation** | Misread the problem, data, or instructions | Comprehension or attention to detail | Solved for perimeter when the problem asked for area | Read "6.5 pH" as "65 pH" on the test strip |

### Educator Language Models for Introducing Error Types

- "Today we are going to learn the five types of errors. Not so we can feel bad about making them -- so we can get better at diagnosing what happened and fixing it. Like a plant doctor diagnosing what is wrong with a sick plant."
- "When a doctor says you have a cold, that is not a judgment. It is a diagnosis that tells you what treatment to use. Error classification works the same way."
- "Knowing your error type is powerful. A computational error needs a different fix than a conceptual error. You would not treat a nitrogen deficiency with more water."

## The Error Analysis Protocol

### Step-by-Step Process

Students use this protocol whenever they encounter an error during the Check step of TRACE or when reviewing returned work.

| Step | Name | Action | Question to Ask | Reasoning Move |
|------|------|--------|----------------|---------------|
| 1 | **Identify** | Circle or highlight the specific error | "Where exactly did things go wrong?" | DECOMPOSE |
| 2 | **Classify** | Determine which of the 5 error types it is | "What kind of error is this?" | COMPARE |
| 3 | **Diagnose** | Explain why the error occurred | "What was my brain doing when this happened?" | QUESTION |
| 4 | **Correct** | Redo the work correctly | "What is the right approach?" | VERIFY |
| 5 | **Prevent** | Identify a strategy to avoid this error in the future | "What will I do differently next time?" | GENERALIZE |

### Educator Language Models for the Protocol

- **Identify:** "Do not just say 'I got it wrong.' Point to the exact spot. Where is the crack in the foundation?"
- **Classify:** "Is this a computational error, a procedural error, a conceptual error, a strategic error, or a reading error? Name it."
- **Diagnose:** "Why did this happen? Were you rushing? Did you skip a step? Did you misunderstand something? Be honest -- this is data, not judgment."
- **Correct:** "Now do it right. Show me the corrected version alongside the original so you can see the difference."
- **Prevent:** "What is your plan to catch this next time? A checklist? Slower reading? Checking units? Give me something specific."

## Error Journals

### Structure and Purpose

Each student maintains an Error Journal section within their Growth Journal. This is a running record of errors, their classifications, and the patterns that emerge over time.

### Error Journal Entry Template

```
Date: ___________  Subject: ___________  Assignment: ___________

ERROR ENTRY:
The problem/task: ___________
My original answer/approach: ___________
The error (be specific): ___________

CLASSIFICATION: [ ] Computational  [ ] Procedural  [ ] Conceptual
               [ ] Strategic  [ ] Reading/Interpretation

DIAGNOSIS:
Why this error happened: ___________
What my brain was doing: ___________

CORRECTION:
The correct approach: ___________
The correct answer: ___________

PREVENTION PLAN:
Next time I will: ___________

PATTERN CHECK (monthly):
My most common error type this month: ___________
What this tells me about my learning: ___________
My goal for next month: ___________
```

### Educator Language Models for Error Journaling

- "Your Error Journal is your most valuable study tool. It tells you exactly where your brain needs strengthening. A gardener's log of what failed is more useful than a log of what worked."
- "At the end of each month, look for patterns. If you keep making procedural errors in math, that tells you to slow down and check your steps. If you keep making conceptual errors in science, that tells you to revisit the underlying ideas."
- "I keep my own error journal. Last week I made a strategic error in my lesson planning -- I chose the wrong approach for this group. I wrote it down and planned differently this week."

## Peer Error Analysis

### Collaborative Error Review Protocol

Peer error analysis builds both analytical skills and a classroom culture where errors are normalized and valued.

**Protocol: "Error Exchange"**

| Step | Duration | Action | Language Model |
|------|----------|--------|---------------|
| 1. Share | 1 min | Student A shares their error (from Error Journal or current work) | "Here is an error I made and how I classified it." |
| 2. Analyze | 2 min | Student B examines the error and offers their analysis | "I agree/disagree with your classification because..." |
| 3. Discuss | 2 min | Partners discuss the diagnosis and prevention plan together | "I have made that kind of error too. What helped me was..." |
| 4. Record | 1 min | Both students note any new insights in their Error Journals | "From this exchange, I learned..." |

### Guidelines for Peer Error Analysis

- **Errors are shared voluntarily.** No student is forced to share an error they find embarrassing.
- **Analysis focuses on the error, not the person.** "This is a procedural error" -- not "You made a careless mistake."
- **Partners are analytical, not sympathetic.** The goal is diagnosis, not comfort. "Let us figure out what happened" rather than "It is okay, do not worry about it."
- **Celebrate diagnostic accuracy.** When a student correctly classifies an error, that is a skill worth recognizing.

### Educator Language Models for Peer Error Analysis

- "When you review your partner's error, put on your scientist hat. You are diagnosing, not judging."
- "If you disagree about the error type, that is great. Discuss it. Sometimes an error is both procedural and conceptual. The conversation is the learning."
- "The best mathematicians and scientists spend more time analyzing their errors than celebrating their successes."

## Garden-Based Troubleshooting

### Error Analysis in the Garden

The garden provides authentic, low-stakes contexts for practicing error analysis. When a plant struggles or dies, the analysis process mirrors academic error analysis exactly.

### Garden Troubleshooting Protocol

| Symptom | Possible Error Type | Diagnostic Questions | Investigation Method |
|---------|-------------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Yellowing leaves (older leaves first) | Conceptual (misunderstanding nutrient needs) or Procedural (wrong fertilizer application) | "Did we apply the right nutrients? In the right amount? At the right time?" | Soil test; compare to recommended nutrient levels |
| Seeds did not germinate | Strategic (wrong planting method) or Computational (planted too deep) | "Did we plant at the correct depth? Was soil temperature right?" | Measure planting depth; check soil thermometer records |
| Plants wilting despite watering | Conceptual (overwatering causes root rot, not just underwatering causes wilting) | "Are we watering too much or too little? Is drainage adequate?" | Check root health; test soil drainage rate |
| Pest damage | Reading/Interpretation (misidentified pest) or Strategic (wrong treatment) | "Did we correctly identify the pest? Is our treatment targeting the right organism?" | Use identification guide; compare to known pests |
| Uneven growth across beds | Procedural (inconsistent care) or Computational (unequal distribution of amendments) | "Did every bed receive the same treatment? Were measurements consistent?" | Review garden logs; compare amendment records |

### Sample Garden Troubleshooting Lesson

**Scenario:** Half the tomato plants in Bed A are thriving while half are stunted.

**Error Analysis Application:**
1. **Identify:** Students map the bed, marking which plants are thriving and which are stunted. (DECOMPOSE)
2. **Classify:** Is this a conceptual error (we do not understand what tomatoes need), a procedural error (we did something inconsistently), or a computational error (we miscalculated fertilizer or spacing)? (COMPARE)
3. **Diagnose:** Students investigate. They measure sunlight exposure at different points in the bed, test soil at multiple locations, check irrigation distribution, and review planting records. (QUESTION, VERIFY)
4. **Correct:** Based on findings, students implement a correction -- perhaps adjusting irrigation, adding shade cloth, or amending soil in specific areas. (TRANSFORM)
5. **Prevent:** Students update the garden protocol to prevent the issue next season. (GENERALIZE)

## Growth Mindset Deepening

### Beyond "Not Yet" -- Building an Error-Positive Identity

By grades 6-8, growth mindset instruction moves beyond simple affirmations to include neuroscience, self-talk analysis, and identity-level beliefs about intelligence and ability.

### Fixed vs. Growth Self-Talk Reframe Table

| Situation | Fixed Mindset Self-Talk | Growth Mindset Reframe | Error Type Connection |
|-----------|----------------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Failed a math test | "I am not a math person." | "My error pattern shows I need to strengthen my conceptual understanding of proportions." | Conceptual |
| Garden experiment failed | "I ruined everything." | "The experiment gave me data I did not expect. That is still data." | Strategic |
| Made the same error twice | "I will never get this." | "This is a pattern. Patterns are diagnosable. I need a new prevention strategy." | Procedural |
| Misread a problem | "I am so stupid." | "I made a reading error. I will underline key words next time." | Reading/Interpretation |
| Got a different answer than partner | "I must be wrong." | "We used different approaches. Let us compare and verify." | Could be any type |

### Teaching the Neuroscience of Error

**Key concepts for students:**

- **Errors activate learning.** When the brain detects an error, it generates a stronger neural signal than when everything goes right. Error is literally the brain's learning mechanism.
- **Struggle strengthens connections.** The feeling of difficulty is the feeling of neural pathways being built. Ease means you are practicing what you already know. Struggle means you are growing.
- **The brain is plastic.** Neural connections strengthen with practice and weaken without it. Intelligence is not fixed -- it is built through effort and error correction.

### Educator Language Models for Growth Mindset

- "Your brain literally learns more from errors than from getting things right on the first try. That is not motivational fluff. That is neuroscience."
- "When you feel frustrated, that is your brain saying, 'I am working hard on this.' Frustration is the feeling of growth, the same way sore muscles are the feeling of getting stronger."
- "I do not want a classroom where everyone gets everything right. I want a classroom where everyone is willing to be wrong, analyze why, and try again. That is a garden that grows."

## Assessment Indicators

| Indicator | Developing | Proficient | Advanced |
|-----------|-----------|------------|----------|
| Error identification | Cannot locate specific error; says "I got it wrong" | Points to exact location of error in work | Identifies multiple errors and prioritizes by significance |
| Error classification | Cannot distinguish error types; guesses | Correctly classifies most errors using the 5 types | Recognizes compound errors (e.g., conceptual error leading to procedural error) |
| Error diagnosis | Cannot explain why error occurred | Provides reasonable explanation for error cause | Links error to specific cognitive process or knowledge gap |
| Error correction | Needs full re-teaching to correct | Self-corrects after classification and diagnosis | Corrects and generates alternative approaches |
| Pattern recognition | Does not track or notice error patterns | Identifies most common error type over time | Uses pattern data to set specific learning goals and adjust study strategies |
| Growth mindset language | Uses fixed mindset self-talk; avoids challenges | Catches fixed mindset self-talk and reframes most of the time | Consistently uses growth-oriented language; supports peers in reframing |
| Garden troubleshooting | Notices problems but cannot diagnose | Applies error analysis protocol to garden issues | Independently identifies, classifies, and resolves garden problems |

## Transition to Reconnect Phase

The Restore phase concludes when students have processed their errors and are ready to apply their learning.

**Transition Language:**
- "You have identified what went wrong and why. You have corrected it. You have made a plan. Now let us take what you have learned and connect it to the bigger picture."
- "In the garden, once you diagnose and treat a problem, you do not just walk away. You check back. You see how the correction is working. That is what we do in Reconnect."

## Educator Implementation Notes

- **Model your own errors publicly.** Make intentional errors during instruction and walk through the error analysis protocol out loud. "I just made a procedural error. I distributed before combining like terms. Let me reclassify and correct."
- **Never use errors as evidence of laziness or carelessness.** Those are judgments that shut down learning. "Careless" is not an error type. Computational and reading errors have specific, addressable causes.
- **Error Journals are private by default.** Students choose what to share during peer error analysis. Never require a student to share an error they are not ready to discuss.
- **Celebrate error analysis, not error avoidance.** The student who finds and fixes three errors in their work has learned more than the student who got everything right on the first try.
- **Connect to garden resilience.** Plants face setbacks -- frost, pests, drought. They do not give up. They recover, adapt, and grow back. Students are the same.
- **Watch for shame responses.** If a student shuts down during error analysis, they may be experiencing a trauma response. Return to the Regulate phase. Error work requires emotional safety.
