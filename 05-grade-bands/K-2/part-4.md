# K-2 Restore Phase: Gentle Error Recovery

## Overview

The Restore phase for K-2 learners transforms mistakes from sources of shame into moments of discovery. Young children are especially vulnerable to shame responses when they make errors -- they may cry, shut down, refuse to try again, or say "I'm stupid." For children who have experienced adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), an error can trigger a disproportionate fear response rooted in past experiences where mistakes led to punishment, rejection, or unpredictability.

The K-2 Restore phase uses garden metaphors, storytelling, the "Oops and Grow" approach, and gentle adult scaffolding to help children understand that mistakes are a normal, valuable part of learning -- just as a garden experiences setbacks that ultimately strengthen the growing process.

> **Garden Metaphor**: Sometimes a seedling bends the wrong way, or a plant grows toward shade instead of sunlight. The gardener does not pull the plant up. Instead, the gardener gently redirects it, gives it a stake to lean on, and trusts that it will find the light. Every "wrong turn" in the garden is just part of growing.

---

## Purpose

1. **Normalize error** -- Help children understand that mistakes are expected, not shameful, through stories, metaphors, and consistent adult responses.
2. **Reframe error as growth** -- Connect mistakes to the idea that their brain is learning something new.
3. **Scaffold error analysis** -- Guide children to notice what happened in their work, with adult support appropriate for their developmental level.
4. **Build emotional resilience** -- Ensure that the experience of making an error strengthens rather than damages the child's willingness to try.

---

## Core Principles for K-2 Error Recovery

### The "Oops and Grow" Approach

Every error in the K-2 classroom is met with the same simple, consistent phrase and gesture: **"Oops and grow!"** The educator models this by saying it cheerfully when they themselves make a mistake, and the class uses it together when errors arise in group work.

**Introducing "Oops and Grow" to the Class**

During the first week, the educator explicitly teaches the routine:

1. "In our garden classroom, mistakes are how our brains grow. When we make a mistake, we say 'Oops!' -- and then we figure out how to grow from it."
2. Model by making a deliberate mistake: "I was going to write the number 5, but look -- I wrote it backward! Oops and grow! Let me try again."
3. Practice as a group: "Let's all say it together -- Oops and grow!"
4. Reinforce daily: Whenever a child makes an error, the educator responds with warmth: "Oops and grow! Let's see what happened."

### Never Say "Wrong"

The word "wrong" is replaced with language that preserves the child's dignity and invites curiosity:

| Instead of... | Say... |
|--------------|--------|
| "That's wrong." | "Hmm, something interesting happened here. Let's look." |
| "No, that's not right." | "You're close! Let's check together." |
| "You made a mistake." | "Oops and grow! Your brain just learned something." |
| "Try again." (flat tone) | "I bet you can figure this out. Want to try it a different way?" |
| "That's not how you do it." | "I can see you were thinking hard. Let me show you another way to look at it." |

### Separate the Child from the Error

For young children, errors feel personal. The educator consistently externalizes the error:
- The error belongs to the **work**, not the child: "The numbers got a little mixed up here."
- The error is something that **happened**, not something the child **is**: "Something tricky happened at this step."
- The error is **temporary** and fixable: "This is the kind of thing we can fix together."

## The Restore Process for K-2

### Step 1: Acknowledge with Warmth

When an error is detected during the Reflect phase, the educator's first response sets the emotional tone for everything that follows.

**Educator Language**:
- "Oh, interesting! Something happened here. Let's be detectives and figure it out."
- "Oops and grow! Your brain just did something really important -- it tried something new."
- "I love that you tried this. Let's look at what happened together."
- "You know what? I make this kind of mistake too. Let me show you."

**Body Language**: Smile, lean in with curiosity, keep voice light and warm. Never furrow brow, shake head, or sigh.

### Step 2: Locate Together (Adult-Scaffolded)

Young children cannot independently identify where an error occurred. The educator guides them through gentle comparison.

**For Counting Errors**:
- "Let's count these together, one at a time. You point, and I'll count with you."
- Place objects in a line so the child can touch each one. "Did we get the same number as before? What changed?"

**For Writing/Drawing Errors**:
- "Look at this letter. Now look at the one on our chart. Are they the same? What's different?"
- "You wrote the number 6. Let's trace it together. Which way does the curve go?"

**For Science Observation Errors**:
- "You said the plant got shorter. Let's measure again with our cubes and see."
- "Look closely with your magnifying glass. How many petals do you count now?"

### Step 3: Reframe with Brain Science (Simplified)

K-2 children can understand a simple version of the neuroscience of learning from mistakes.

**Educator Script -- "The Brain Spark Story"**:
> "Did you know that when you make a mistake, your brain makes a little spark? It's like a tiny firework inside your head. That spark means your brain is building a new road -- a road that helps you understand something you didn't understand before. So every time you go 'Oops!', your brain goes 'SPARK!' and gets a little stronger."

Reinforce this story regularly:
- "I bet your brain just made a big spark!"
- "That oops gave your brain a new road to travel on."
- "Sparks are how we grow. More sparks, more growing."

### Step 4: Guide Self-Correction

Whenever possible, the child corrects the error themselves with scaffolding rather than having the adult simply provide the right answer.

| Error Type | Scaffolding Strategy | Educator Prompt |
|------------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Counting error | Provide a number line or counting mat; have child recount touching each object | "Use the number line to help you. Touch each seed as you count." |
| Letter/number formation | Provide a model to trace; use hand-over-hand if needed | "Let's look at the chart. Trace this one first, then try on your own." |
| Sorting/classification error | Remove one item and ask "Does this one belong here?" | "Look at this group. Are all of these the same? Which one is different?" |
| Science observation error | Return to the material for re-observation | "Let's look one more time. What do you see now?" |
| Sequencing error | Lay out picture cards and walk through the order | "What happened first? What came next? Let's put them in order." |

### Step 5: Celebrate the Growth

After the error is resolved, the educator explicitly celebrates the learning that came from the mistake.

- "You fixed it! Your brain is stronger now because of that oops."
- "See? You figured it out. That's what growing looks like."
- "I'm so proud of you for sticking with it. That took real bravery."
- "Let's add a spark to your journal. Your brain grew today!"

**Spark Journal**: Children can draw a small star or spark symbol in their garden journal each time they learn from a mistake. Over time, they can count their sparks and see how much their brain has grown.

## Normalizing Mistakes Through Stories and Garden Experiences

### Story-Based Error Normalization

Read or tell stories that feature characters making mistakes and growing from them. Discuss after each story: "What was the oops? What did they learn? Did their brain make a spark?"

**Recommended Books for Error Normalization**:
- *Beautiful Oops!* by Barney Saltzberg
- *The Most Magnificent Thing* by Ashley Spires
- *After the Fall (How Humpty Dumpty Got Back Up Again)* by Dan Santat
- *Rosie Revere, Engineer* by Andrea Beaty
- *Mistakes That Worked* by Charlotte Foltz Jones (selected stories, read aloud)

### Garden Failures as Teaching Moments

The garden provides authentic, low-stakes experiences with failure that build resilience.

| Garden Failure | Teaching Moment | Educator Language |
|---------------|----------------|-------------------|
| Seeds that do not germinate | Not every attempt succeeds; we try again | "Some seeds didn't sprout. That's what happens in gardens sometimes. What should we do? Plant more!" |
| A plant that wilts from too much or too little water | Mistakes teach us what living things need | "Our plant is droopy. Oops and grow! What do you think it needs? How can we help it?" |
| Pests eating the leaves | Problems are opportunities to learn and solve | "Something nibbled our lettuce! Let's be detectives. What do you think happened?" |
| Weeds overtaking a bed | Maintenance and care are ongoing | "The weeds grew fast! That's not a mistake -- gardens need tending. Let's clear them out together." |
| A broken stem or branch | Gentle handling matters; sometimes things break and we repair | "The stem broke. That's sad, but look -- we can put it in water and watch what happens." |

These garden experiences provide a shared, concrete vocabulary for talking about errors in academic work: "Remember when our seeds didn't sprout? We didn't give up -- we planted more. Let's do the same thing with this math problem."

## Educator Role and Emotional Safety

### Monitoring for Shame Responses

When an error triggers a shame response in a child, the Restore phase pauses and the educator attends to the child's emotional state before returning to the error.

**Signs of Shame in Young Children**:
- Covering face or eyes
- Saying "I'm stupid" or "I can't do anything"
- Crying or shutting down after a small mistake
- Refusing to try again
- Destroying their own work (crumpling paper, erasing aggressively)

**Educator Response to Shame**:
1. Pause the academic task immediately.
2. Get at the child's eye level and speak softly: "I can see this feels really hard right now. That's okay."
3. Normalize: "Everyone makes mistakes. Even I do. Even grown-ups. Mistakes are how we learn."
4. Reconnect: "You are a smart, growing person. One mistake doesn't change that."
5. Offer choice: "Would you like to try again with my help, or do something else for a minute?"
6. If the child remains distressed, transition to the Regulate phase.

### When Restore Triggers Dysregulation

If the error recovery process itself causes the child to leave their window of tolerance, the educator transitions seamlessly to the Regulate phase. The error can be revisited in a later session when the child is regulated. There is no urgency to correct the error in the moment if doing so would cause harm.

## Assessment Indicators

| Indicator | Beginning | Developing | Secure |
|-----------|-----------|------------|--------|
| Tolerates making errors | Cries, shuts down, or refuses to continue after an error | Shows mild distress but recovers with adult support within 1-2 minutes | Accepts errors calmly; says "Oops and grow!" or equivalent |
| Engages in error analysis (with scaffolding) | Cannot look at or discuss the error | Points to or describes the error when guided by the educator | Begins to identify own errors: "I think I counted wrong here" |
| Attempts self-correction | Waits for adult to fix the error | Tries to correct with significant scaffolding (hand-over-hand, counting mat) | Attempts correction independently; asks for help when stuck |
| Uses growth language | No growth language; may use fixed language ("I'm dumb") | Repeats "Oops and grow" when prompted by educator | Uses growth language spontaneously: "My brain made a spark!" |
| Willingness to try again after error | Refuses to re-attempt the task | Re-attempts with encouragement and adult presence | Re-attempts willingly and shows curiosity about the error |

## Transition Out of the Restore Phase

After the error has been acknowledged, explored, and corrected (or when the child has demonstrated growth from the mistake), the educator transitions back to the Reflect phase.

- "Great job figuring that out! Your brain just grew. Ready to keep going?"
- "You did it! That spark is going to help you with the next one. Let's try."
- "I could see you thinking so hard. Now let's use what you learned."

If the error reveals a deeper gap in understanding, the educator adjusts the difficulty level or approach before continuing in the Reflect phase. If the child needs additional emotional support, the session moves to Regulate before returning to learning.
