# K-2 Grade Band: Overview & Developmental Context

## Introduction

The K-2 grade band represents the foundational years of the RootWork curriculum, serving children ages five through eight during a period of extraordinary cognitive, social-emotional, and physical development. At this stage, children are forming their foundational relationships with learning itself -- their sense of whether school is safe, whether they are capable, and whether adults can be trusted to guide them. For students who have experienced adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), these early years are a critical window for establishing corrective relational experiences through predictable, nurturing, and sensory-rich educational encounters.

The RootWork K-2 curriculum leverages the natural curiosity and sensory orientation of early childhood by grounding all academic content in garden-based experiences. Young children learn through their bodies first -- touching soil, smelling herbs, watching seeds sprout, and tasting vegetables they have grown. These concrete, multisensory experiences provide the foundation for abstract academic concepts while simultaneously creating the conditions for nervous system regulation that trauma-informed practice demands.

## Developmental Profile: Ages 5-8

### Cognitive Development

Children in the K-2 band are in Piaget's preoperational to early concrete operational stage. Key characteristics include:

- **Concrete thinking**: Learning is anchored in direct sensory experience. Abstract concepts must be represented through manipulatives, visual models, and real-world objects.
- **Emerging classification skills**: Children begin sorting, grouping, and categorizing -- skills that align naturally with garden activities like sorting seeds by size, grouping plants by color, or categorizing insects as helpful or harmful.
- **Beginning number sense**: One-to-one correspondence, counting, basic addition and subtraction, and early measurement concepts are developing. The garden provides authentic contexts for all of these (counting seeds, measuring plant growth, comparing heights).
- **Emerging literacy**: Children are developing phonemic awareness, letter-sound correspondence, sight word recognition, and beginning decoding skills. Garden journals, plant labels, and seed packets provide meaningful print-rich environments.
- **Limited working memory**: Young children can hold 2-3 pieces of information simultaneously. Instructions must be brief, visual, and repeated.
- **Magical thinking residue**: Kindergarteners in particular may hold non-scientific explanations for natural phenomena ("The plant is sad because it's drooping"). The curriculum honors these perspectives while gently introducing scientific vocabulary and observation skills.

### Social-Emotional Development

- **Attachment seeking**: Children at this age are actively seeking secure attachments with adults. The educator's role as a non-anxious, warm, predictable presence is paramount.
- **Emotion vocabulary development**: Many K-2 students have limited language for their internal states. The curriculum systematically builds feeling vocabularies through garden metaphors (wilting, blooming, growing, dormant).
- **Emerging self-regulation**: Children are developing but have not yet mastered self-regulation. Co-regulation with a trusted adult is the primary mechanism. The Regulate phase relies heavily on adult-led strategies at this level.
- **Play orientation**: Play is the primary vehicle for learning. The garden provides a natural play environment where academic learning occurs through exploration, imagination, and discovery.
- **Social skill emergence**: Sharing tools, taking turns watering, and collaborative planting activities build foundational social skills.
- **Sensitivity to shame**: Young children are particularly vulnerable to shame responses around mistakes. The Restore phase must be delivered with extreme care, focusing on normalizing errors through garden metaphors ("Even gardeners pull up the wrong plant sometimes!").

### Physical Development

- **Gross motor dominance**: Large muscle movements are more developed than fine motor skills. Garden activities like digging, raking, carrying watering cans, and walking garden paths leverage gross motor strengths.
- **Developing fine motor skills**: Planting seeds, pulling weeds, and using child-sized tools provide meaningful fine motor practice.
- **High energy needs**: Children require frequent movement breaks. The garden provides natural opportunities for physical activity embedded within academic instruction.
- **Sensory integration development**: Many children, especially those with trauma histories, may have sensory processing challenges. The garden environment offers rich, controllable sensory input (textures, smells, sounds) that can be both regulating and instructional.

## 5Rs Framework Adaptation for K-2

### Root Phase (5-8 minutes)

The Root phase in K-2 is entirely adult-led and emphasizes predictability, warmth, and sensory grounding:

**Entry Rituals:**
- Greeting song or chant that remains consistent throughout the year ("Good morning, little gardeners, it's time to grow today...")
- Weather and garden check: Children look outside or visit the garden to observe current conditions
- Emotion check-in using picture cards (weather metaphors: sunny, cloudy, rainy, stormy) or color-coded cards
- Physical grounding: Stomping feet ("plant your roots"), stretching arms up ("grow toward the sun"), deep breaths ("smell the flowers, blow out the candles")

**Regulation Assessment:**
- Teachers observe body language, facial expressions, and participation level
- Simple thumbs-up/sideways/down system for student self-report
- Individual check-ins for students with known trauma histories or IEP accommodations
- Visual schedule posted at child eye level to establish session predictability

**Garden Connection:**
- Brief visit to classroom plant or garden window
- Touching soil or holding a seed to anchor to the present moment
- Naming one thing they notice about the garden today

### Regulate Phase (as needed, 3-10 minutes)

For K-2 students, regulation is primarily co-regulation -- the adult's calm nervous system helps regulate the child's:

**Tier 1 Strategies (whole class):**
- Movement breaks: "Shake your sillies out," "Freeze dance," "Garden yoga" (tree pose, flower pose, butterfly pose)
- Breathing exercises with visual aids: "Smell the flower, blow the pinwheel," "Balloon breathing" (hands expand and contract with breath)
- Sensory bins in the calm corner: soil, seeds, smooth stones, water beads
- Singing and rhythm: Simple songs with repetitive patterns and gentle movements

**Tier 2 Strategies (small group or individual):**
- One-on-one check-in with educator at the calm corner
- Offering a specific calming tool: weighted lap pad, noise-canceling headphones, textured fidget, or garden glove to squeeze
- Drawing or coloring in growth journal
- Walking the garden path with an adult

**Tier 3 Strategies (intensive individual support):**
- Quiet time in the calm corner with a trusted adult
- Sensory garden activities (running hands through soil, smelling lavender or mint)
- Gentle rocking or swaying movements
- Parent/guardian contact if dysregulation persists beyond 15 minutes
- Documentation for IEP team review

### Reflect Phase (15-25 minutes)

The Reflect phase in K-2 uses simplified TRACE protocol elements:

**Think** ("What do you notice?"):
- Observation activities: "Look at this plant. What do you see?"
- Activating prior knowledge: "What do we already know about seeds?"
- Picture walks through garden-related texts

**Reason** ("What do you think will happen?"):
- Simple predictions: "If we plant this seed and give it water, what do you think will happen?"
- Sorting and classifying: "Can you put these seeds into groups? How did you decide?"
- Beginning problem-solving: "We have 6 plants and 3 pots. How many plants go in each pot?"

**Articulate** ("Tell me about your thinking"):
- Partner sharing: "Turn and tell your garden buddy what you think"
- Sentence starters: "I think ___ because ___"
- Drawing and labeling observations
- Group discussions with talking stick (a garden stake or decorated stick)

**Check** ("Let's see if we were right"):
- Returning to predictions after experiments
- Counting and measuring to verify
- Comparing drawings to real observations

**Extend** ("What else?"):
- "What would happen if we changed ___?"
- "Can you find something else in the garden that works the same way?"
- Taking the idea home: "Tell someone at home about what we learned"

### Restore Phase (2-5 minutes, embedded in Reflect)

For K-2, error recovery is woven into instruction rather than being a distinct phase:

**Normalizing Mistakes:**
- "Our brains are growing right now! That means we're learning!"
- Garden metaphors: "Sometimes we plant a seed upside down, and you know what? It still finds a way to grow. Our brains work the same way."
- Celebrating the process: "You tried three different ways! That's what scientists do!"
- Class agreements: "In our garden, mistakes help us grow" (posted visibly)

**Error Recovery Strategies:**
- Gentle redirection without highlighting the error publicly
- Offering a second attempt with scaffolding: "Let me show you a trick. Now you try."
- Partnering with a peer for collaborative correction
- Concrete manipulatives to make the error visible and correctable (e.g., moving counters to recount)

### Reconnect Phase (5-10 minutes)

The Reconnect phase for K-2 emphasizes sharing, celebration, and connecting learning to students' lives:

**Sharing and Celebration:**
- Show-and-tell from the day's garden or learning activities
- Gallery walks of student work
- "Growth Reports" to share with families: "Today I learned ___ in the garden"
- Verbal affirmations focused on effort and reasoning: "You kept trying even when it was hard. That's how gardeners grow strong plants!"

**Real-World Connections:**
- "Where might you see this at home? At the store? On the way to school?"
- Connecting garden observations to daily life: "Has anyone seen a plant growing through a crack in the sidewalk? That's just like what we learned about how strong roots can be!"
- Family engagement prompts: "Ask someone at home about their favorite vegetable"

**Closing Ritual:**
- Return to the garden or classroom plant
- Watering routine or observation journaling
- Preview of next session: "Next time, we'll check on our seeds and see if they've sprouted!"
- Closing song or chant

## Classroom Environment

### Physical Space

The K-2 learning environment should include:

- **Garden access**: Outdoor garden beds at child height (raised beds 18-24 inches), container gardens, or indoor growing stations with grow lights
- **Calm corner**: A designated, semi-enclosed space with soft seating, sensory tools, visual timers, and feeling charts
- **Growth journal station**: Table with journals, colored pencils, magnifying glasses, and observation prompts
- **Living wall or window garden**: Visible growing things that students can monitor daily
- **Visual schedule board**: Pictorial daily schedule showing garden and academic activities
- **Vocabulary word wall**: Garden and academic vocabulary with pictures and student-friendly definitions
- **Class agreements display**: "In our garden, we..." statements co-created with students, including "Mistakes help us grow"

### Materials and Tools

| Category | Items |
|----------|-------|
| **Garden Tools** | Child-sized trowels, watering cans, spray bottles, garden gloves, measuring tapes, magnifying glasses, collection jars |
| **Math Manipulatives** | Seeds (various sizes for counting/sorting), linking cubes, balance scales, rulers, tape measures, graph paper |
| **Science Tools** | Hand lenses, thermometers, rain gauges, soil test kits, observation journals, plant lifecycle charts |
| **Literacy Materials** | Garden-themed picture books, seed catalogs, field guides, label makers, alphabet cards with garden vocabulary |
| **Regulation Tools** | Breathing balls (Hoberman spheres), weighted lap pads, fidget tools, noise-canceling headphones, sensory bins (soil, water beads, smooth stones), visual timers, feeling charts |
| **Technology** | Tablets for photographing plants, simple graphing apps, time-lapse plant cameras (optional) |

## Trauma-Informed Considerations for K-2

### Understanding Trauma in Early Childhood

Young children may manifest trauma through:

- **Regression**: Thumb-sucking, baby talk, toileting accidents, clinginess
- **Hypervigilance**: Startling at sounds, watching doors, difficulty with transitions
- **Aggression or withdrawal**: Hitting, biting, or conversely becoming very quiet and compliant
- **Somatic complaints**: Stomachaches, headaches, fatigue with no medical cause
- **Play disruption**: Repetitive play themes involving danger, loss, or chaos
- **Attachment behaviors**: Excessive clinginess with teachers or indiscriminate friendliness with unfamiliar adults

### Curriculum Response

The K-2 RootWork curriculum addresses these presentations through:

1. **Predictability**: Every session follows the same 5Rs sequence with consistent rituals, reducing hypervigilance by making the environment knowable.
2. **Sensory regulation**: Garden activities provide controlled sensory input that helps organize a dysregulated nervous system -- the feel of warm soil, the smell of herbs, the visual beauty of flowers.
3. **Relational safety**: The educator maintains a warm, non-anxious presence. No shame-based language. No public correction of behavior. Choices are offered whenever possible.
4. **Body-based learning**: Physical activity in the garden allows children to discharge stress energy while learning. Digging, carrying, and building are inherently regulating.
5. **Narrative building**: Growth journals help children develop a coherent narrative about themselves as learners and growers, countering the fragmented self-concept that trauma can create.
6. **Community belonging**: Garden rituals create a sense of belonging and contribution. Even the youngest child can water a plant and see the result of their care.

### IEP and 504 Accommodations

The K-2 curriculum supports common accommodations:

| Accommodation | Curriculum Integration |
|--------------|----------------------|
| Extended time | Flexible pacing within the 5Rs; garden activities are naturally self-paced |
| Preferential seating | Garden circles allow flexible positioning; calm corner is always available |
| Visual supports | Picture schedules, visual timers, labeled garden tools, pictorial instructions |
| Sensory breaks | Garden time serves as a sensory break; calm corner tools always accessible |
| Reduced stimulation | Quiet garden zones designated; noise-canceling headphones available |
| Modified assignments | Differentiated journal prompts (drawing, dictation, writing); tiered math problems |
| Behavioral support | 5Rs framework inherently provides behavioral scaffolding; regulation tools embedded |
| Speech/language support | Partner sharing, sentence starters, vocabulary walls, and adult modeling support language development |

## Sample Weekly Schedule: K-2

| Day | Root (5-8 min) | Reflect (20 min) | Reconnect (5-10 min) |
|-----|----------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| **Monday** | Garden greeting + weather check + emotion check-in | Math: Counting and sorting seeds by type; estimating quantities | Share discoveries with a partner; preview the week |
| **Tuesday** | Body scan grounding + growth journal drawing | Science: Observe and draw plant changes; introduce vocabulary (root, stem, leaf) | "Tell someone at home" prompt; update class growth chart |
| **Wednesday** | Garden walk + sensory grounding (touch, smell) | Literacy: Read aloud *The Carrot Seed*; retelling with garden puppets; journal writing | Act out the story in the garden; celebrate effort |
| **Thursday** | Breathing exercise + partner check-in | Math: Measuring plant growth with non-standard units; recording data | Compare measurements; graph results together |
| **Friday** | Free garden exploration + emotional check-in | Integrated: Garden work (planting, weeding, harvesting) with embedded academic talk | Garden harvest celebration; share with school community; closing circle |

*Note: Regulate and Restore phases are embedded throughout as needed, not scheduled as fixed time blocks.*
