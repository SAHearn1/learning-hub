# Partnership Models

## Overview

Community partnerships are integral to the RootWork Learning Hub's mission of providing a comprehensive, trauma-informed, garden-based educational experience. No school can achieve this mission in isolation. Effective partnerships extend the garden's reach beyond school boundaries, connecting students to the broader community, providing resources and expertise that complement educator capacity, and building the web of relationships that supports whole-child development.

This document describes the partnership models, structures, and best practices that guide the RootWork approach to community collaboration. Each model is designed to be reciprocal—partnerships should benefit all parties, not extract value from community organizations to serve institutional goals.

## Partnership Framework

### Guiding Principles

1. **Reciprocity:** Every partnership provides genuine value to all parties. The school is not a charity case, and community partners are not free labor.
2. **Shared Vision:** Partners agree on goals, values, and expected outcomes before formalizing the relationship.
3. **Equity:** Partnerships actively address power imbalances. Community voices shape program design, not just implementation.
4. **Sustainability:** Partnerships are designed for long-term relationship, not one-time transactions.
5. **Cultural Responsiveness:** Partnerships honor the cultures, languages, and traditions of the community.
6. **Trauma Awareness:** All partners understand trauma-informed principles and commit to creating safe spaces for students and families.
7. **Accountability:** Partners hold each other accountable through regular communication, shared data, and honest feedback.

### Partnership Continuum

Community partnerships exist on a continuum of integration and commitment:

| Level | Description | Examples | Time Investment |
|-------|-------------|----------|----------------|
| **Networking** | Awareness of each other's work; information sharing | Attending community meetings; sharing resources | Low |
| **Coordination** | Aligning activities to avoid duplication; making referrals | Coordinating schedules; cross-referring families to services | Low-Medium |
| **Cooperation** | Sharing resources for mutual benefit; short-term joint projects | Guest speakers; one-time donation of materials; shared events | Medium |
| **Collaboration** | Joint planning and implementation; shared goals and accountability | Co-designed programs; shared staff; joint grant applications; integrated data | High |
| **Integration** | Merged resources and structures; unified governance; shared identity | Full-service community school model; co-located services; shared strategic plan | Very High |

Most RootWork partnerships begin at the networking or coordination level and deepen over time as trust builds and mutual value becomes clear.

## Core Partnership Models

### Model 1: Community Garden Partnerships

**Description:** Formal collaboration between the school garden and established community gardens, urban farms, or agricultural organizations.

**Structure:**

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Partner Type | Community gardens, urban farms, master gardener programs, cooperative extension services |
| Formal Agreement | MOU specifying roles, responsibilities, resource sharing, scheduling, and liability |
| Governance | Joint planning committee meets monthly; includes educator, garden manager, student representative, and community partner lead |
| Activities | Shared growing projects, knowledge exchange, cross-site visits, joint harvests, combined community events |
| Student Role | Students participate as co-gardeners and researchers, not just visitors; contribute labor, data, and knowledge |
| Assessment Connection | Community garden data incorporated into student research projects; community partner provides authentic audience for student work |

**Implementation Timeline:**

| Phase | Duration | Key Activities |
|-------|----------|---------------|
| Exploration | Months 1-2 | Identify potential partners; conduct site visits; assess alignment of mission and values |
| Development | Months 3-4 | Draft MOU; define roles and expectations; establish communication protocols; plan joint activities |
| Pilot | Months 5-8 | Implement first joint project; assess logistics, student engagement, and partner satisfaction |
| Formalization | Month 9 | Sign MOU; establish governance structure; create annual plan |
| Sustained Partnership | Ongoing | Regular joint activities; annual plan renewal; continuous improvement based on shared evaluation |

**Reciprocity Structure:**

| School Provides | Community Partner Provides |
|----------------|--------------------------|
| Student labor and enthusiasm | Expert knowledge and mentorship |
| Data collection and research capacity | Growing space and infrastructure |
| Educational programming for community events | Community network and relationships |
| Fresh perspective and innovation | Institutional memory and continuity |
| Access to school facilities for events | Access to community spaces and events |

### Model 2: Food Bank and Food Access Partnerships

**Description:** Collaboration with food banks, food pantries, and food access organizations to address food insecurity while providing students with authentic community service and social justice learning.

**Structure:**

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Partner Type | Regional food bank, local food pantries, community meals programs, mobile markets |
| Activities | Dedicated growing plots for donation; harvest delivery; volunteer service; food system education; advocacy |
| Student Role | Students manage "community plots" and track production; deliver harvest; analyze food access data; advocate for policy |
| Data Integration | Pounds donated, estimated meals provided, volunteer hours, community reach tracked as enterprise social impact metrics |
| Curriculum Connection | Food justice research (Part 3); enterprise social impact assessment (Part 5); mathematics of food production and distribution |

**Growing for Community Program:**

| Season | Crops Prioritized | Rationale |
|--------|-------------------|-----------|
| Spring | Leafy greens, radishes, peas | Fast-growing; high nutritional value; commonly requested by food banks |
| Summer | Tomatoes, peppers, squash, beans | High yield; culturally versatile; store well |
| Fall | Kale, collards, root vegetables, herbs | Cold-hardy; nutrient-dense; long harvest window |
| Year-round (greenhouse) | Microgreens, lettuce, herbs | High nutritional density; continuous production; low space requirement |

### Model 3: Higher Education Partnerships

**Description:** Collaboration with colleges and universities for research, professional development, student mentorship, and program evaluation.

**Structure:**

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Partner Type | Colleges of education, agriculture, public health, social work, environmental science |
| Activities | Pre-service teacher placements; collaborative research; guest lectures; college access programming; program evaluation |
| Student Role | High school students interact with college students as near-peer mentors; visit campuses; participate in research as co-investigators |
| Educator Role | Educators serve as mentor teachers for pre-service candidates; co-present at conferences; contribute to research publications |

**Partnership Activities:**

| Activity | Higher Ed Benefit | RootWork Benefit |
|----------|------------------|-----------------|
| Pre-service teacher placement | Authentic training site for future educators | Additional instructional support; fresh perspectives |
| Collaborative research | Access to school-based data and participants | Rigorous program evaluation; evidence for grant applications |
| Guest lectures and workshops | Community engagement and service-learning for professors | Expert knowledge for students and educators |
| College access programming | Recruitment pipeline; community relationship | Students build college familiarity and confidence |
| Joint conference presentations | Publication and presentation opportunities | Professional recognition; program visibility |
| Summer research internships | Undergraduate research opportunities | High school students gain pre-college experience |

### Model 4: Healthcare and Mental Health Partnerships

**Description:** Collaboration with healthcare providers, mental health agencies, and wellness organizations to support the trauma-informed mission of the RootWork curriculum.

**Structure:**

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Partner Type | Community mental health centers, school-based health clinics, hospital community benefit programs, private practitioners |
| Activities | School-based counseling; trauma-informed training; crisis response; wellness programming; referral pathways |
| Integration | Garden activities explicitly coordinated with therapeutic goals for students receiving services; garden as therapeutic intervention endorsed by clinicians |
| Confidentiality | All partnerships governed by FERPA, HIPAA, and organizational confidentiality agreements |

**Service Integration:**

| Service Level | Description | Provider |
|--------------|-------------|----------|
| Universal (Tier 1) | Trauma-informed environment; SEL curriculum; regulation instruction; garden-based wellness | Educators (trained in trauma-informed practice) |
| Targeted (Tier 2) | Small group interventions; check-in/check-out; mentoring; family support coordination | School counselor + community mental health partner |
| Intensive (Tier 3) | Individual therapy; crisis intervention; psychiatric consultation; foster care coordination | Licensed mental health provider (school-based or community-based) |

### Model 5: Business and Enterprise Partnerships

**Description:** Collaboration with local businesses to support the garden enterprise, provide mentorship, and create authentic learning opportunities.

**Structure:**

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Partner Type | Local restaurants, farmers markets, small businesses, entrepreneurs, business incubators |
| Activities | Market partnerships (selling produce); business mentorship; guest entrepreneurship workshops; job shadow and internship opportunities |
| Student Role | Students operate the enterprise with increasing independence; business mentors serve as advisors, not managers |
| Revenue | Revenue from enterprise partnerships supports garden program sustainability |

**Business Mentorship Program:**

| Quarter | Focus | Mentor Role |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| Q1 | Enterprise planning; business model development | Review business plan; share industry knowledge; help refine value proposition |
| Q2 | Operations management; financial systems | Teach bookkeeping basics; review financial reports; troubleshoot operational challenges |
| Q3 | Marketing and customer relations | Review marketing materials; introduce networking skills; facilitate customer connections |
| Q4 | Annual review and strategic planning | Help analyze year-end data; support strategic planning for next year; celebrate accomplishments |

### Model 6: Cultural and Faith-Based Organization Partnerships

**Description:** Collaboration with cultural organizations, faith communities, and ethnic associations that serve the families in the school community.

**Structure:**

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Partner Type | Cultural centers, faith communities, immigrant/refugee organizations, ethnic associations, historical societies |
| Activities | Cultural food and gardening knowledge exchange; multilingual support; community celebration co-hosting; oral history projects; safe space for families |
| Cultural Integration | Partners contribute cultural knowledge that enriches the curriculum (heritage crops, traditional ecological knowledge, food traditions, community values) |
| Reciprocity | School garden provides a welcoming community space; educational programming for organization members; access to school facilities |

**Cultural Partnership Activities:**

| Activity | Description | Curriculum Connection |
|----------|-------------|----------------------|
| Heritage Crop Program | Cultural organizations provide seeds, knowledge, and stories for culturally significant crops | Cultural studies; social-emotional identity |
| Cooking Traditions Workshop | Community elders teach traditional food preparation using garden produce | Math (measurement); literacy (procedural writing); cultural studies |
| Oral History Project | Students interview community elders about agricultural and food traditions | Literacy; social studies; intergenerational connection |
| Multilingual Garden Signage | Cultural organization members help create garden labels and educational materials in community languages | Literacy; cultural appreciation; inclusion |
| Seasonal Celebrations | Co-host harvest festivals, planting ceremonies, and seasonal celebrations that honor diverse cultural traditions | Community building; cultural awareness; celebration |

## Partnership Management

### Partnership Development Process

| Step | Action | Tools and Resources |
|------|--------|---------------------|
| 1. Asset Mapping | Identify community assets, organizations, and potential partners using an asset-based community development (ABCD) approach | Community asset map template; stakeholder interviews |
| 2. Relationship Building | Attend community events; introduce yourself; learn about potential partners' missions, needs, and strengths | Networking plan; relationship tracker |
| 3. Alignment Assessment | Evaluate mission alignment, capacity, mutual benefit, and potential challenges | Partnership readiness checklist |
| 4. Proposal Development | Create a partnership proposal outlining goals, activities, roles, timeline, and evaluation | Partnership proposal template |
| 5. Formal Agreement | Execute a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) or partnership agreement | MOU template with legal review |
| 6. Implementation | Launch partnership activities with clear communication, role clarity, and regular check-ins | Implementation plan; communication calendar |
| 7. Evaluation | Assess partnership outcomes, satisfaction, and areas for improvement | Partnership evaluation survey; annual review template |
| 8. Renewal or Transition | Based on evaluation, renew, adjust, or respectfully conclude the partnership | Renewal checklist; transition plan |

### Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) Elements

Every formal partnership is governed by an MOU that includes:

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Purpose | Why this partnership exists; shared goals |
| Duration | Start and end dates; renewal process |
| Roles and Responsibilities | What each partner commits to doing; who is responsible for what |
| Resources | What each partner contributes (funding, staff time, materials, space, expertise) |
| Communication | How and how often partners communicate; designated contacts |
| Confidentiality | Student data protection; FERPA compliance; information sharing protocols |
| Liability | Insurance requirements; risk management; safety protocols |
| Evaluation | How partnership effectiveness will be assessed; data sharing |
| Termination | How either party can end the partnership respectfully and responsibly |
| Signatures | Authorized signatures from both organizations |

### Communication Protocols

| Communication Type | Frequency | Method | Purpose |
|-------------------|-----------|--------|---------|
| Operational coordination | Weekly | Email or messaging | Schedule activities, share logistics, resolve immediate issues |
| Progress check-in | Monthly | Phone or video call | Review activities, address challenges, celebrate successes |
| Planning meeting | Quarterly | In-person meeting | Plan upcoming activities, review data, adjust strategy |
| Annual review | Annually | In-person meeting with written report | Comprehensive evaluation, MOU renewal, strategic planning |
| Crisis communication | As needed | Immediate phone call | Safety concerns, urgent issues, significant changes |

## Evaluating Partnership Effectiveness

### Evaluation Framework

| Dimension | Indicators | Data Source |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| Process Quality | Communication frequency and quality; role clarity; mutual respect; shared decision-making | Partner satisfaction survey; meeting notes |
| Activity Implementation | Activities completed as planned; participation rates; resource delivery | Activity log; attendance records |
| Student Outcomes | Academic growth; SEL development; engagement; research quality; career readiness | Student assessments; portfolio evidence |
| Community Impact | Food donated; families served; events attended; community awareness | Program data; community survey |
| Partner Satisfaction | Perceived value; willingness to continue; perceived equity | Annual partner survey; interview |
| Sustainability | Partnership duration; resource stability; institutional commitment | Partnership tracker; budget data |

### Annual Partnership Review Protocol

1. **Data Collection:** Gather quantitative data (activity counts, participation rates, outcomes) and qualitative data (partner interviews, student reflections, family feedback)
2. **Joint Analysis:** Partners review data together, identifying strengths, challenges, and opportunities
3. **Celebration:** Acknowledge accomplishments and contributions publicly
4. **Improvement Planning:** Identify 2-3 specific improvements for the coming year
5. **Renewal Decision:** Based on evaluation, decide whether to renew, modify, or conclude the partnership
6. **Documentation:** Record findings and decisions; update MOU as needed; share summary with stakeholders

## Building a Partnership Ecosystem

The goal is not a collection of isolated partnerships but an integrated ecosystem where partners know each other, collaborate with each other, and collectively support the mission of trauma-informed, garden-based education.

**Ecosystem Building Strategies:**

| Strategy | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| Annual Partner Convening | Host an annual meeting where all partners come together to share work, build relationships, and coordinate efforts |
| Shared Communication Platform | Create a digital space (newsletter, shared drive, or platform) where partners can share resources and updates |
| Cross-Partnership Projects | Design projects that involve multiple partners working together (e.g., a community food summit involving the food bank, local farms, healthcare providers, and cultural organizations) |
| Joint Grant Applications | Apply for funding collaboratively, with multiple partners as co-applicants |
| Community Advisory Board | Establish a board with representation from all major partners to provide governance and strategic direction |
| Storytelling and Recognition | Regularly share partnership stories through newsletters, social media, and community events; recognize partners publicly |
