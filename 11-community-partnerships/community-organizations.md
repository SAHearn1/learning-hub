# Community Organizations

## Overview

The RootWork curriculum is designed to grow beyond school walls. Community partnerships amplify the impact of trauma-informed, garden-based education by connecting students with local expertise, resources, and networks that enrich learning and sustain programs over time. Effective partnerships are reciprocal -- they benefit both the school and the community organization -- and are built on shared values, clear communication, and mutual respect.

This document identifies the types of community organizations that align with the RootWork mission, outlines partnership structures, and provides practical guidance for identifying, building, and maintaining productive partnerships.

---

## Types of Partner Organizations

### Agricultural Extension Offices

**Who They Are:** County-level offices of the University of Georgia Cooperative Extension Service that provide research-based education and resources in agriculture, natural resources, and community development.

**What They Offer:**
- Master Gardener volunteers for school garden support
- Soil testing services (often free or low cost)
- Pest and disease identification
- Seasonal planting guides specific to Georgia's climate zones
- Youth development programs (4-H clubs)
- Teacher training in agricultural science
- Access to demonstration gardens and educational farms

**Partnership Potential:** Extension offices are natural partners for RootWork because they share the commitment to experiential, garden-based learning. They can provide ongoing technical support that educators may lack, particularly in garden design, soil management, and pest control.

### Master Gardener Programs

**Who They Are:** Trained volunteers certified through the UGA Cooperative Extension Service who have completed 75+ hours of horticultural training and commit to community service hours.

**What They Offer:**
- In-garden mentorship for educators and students
- Help with garden planning, planting, and maintenance
- Workshops on specific gardening topics (composting, seed saving, container gardening)
- Year-round garden support, including summer maintenance when school is not in session
- Connections to broader gardening networks and resources

**Partnership Potential:** Master Gardeners provide the sustained, hands-on garden support that many schools need to maintain a productive garden. Their volunteer commitment model aligns well with the ongoing nature of school garden programs.

### Food Banks and Food Access Organizations

**Who They Are:** Organizations focused on addressing food insecurity in the community, including food banks, food pantries, community kitchens, and food rescue operations.

**What They Offer:**
- A meaningful purpose for garden harvests (students grow food that feeds their community)
- Educational programming on nutrition and food systems
- Connections to food access policy and advocacy
- Volunteer opportunities for students
- Data on community food needs that inform garden planning

**Partnership Potential:** Partnering with food access organizations gives the Reconnect phase tangible community impact. When students grow food that is donated to a local food bank, the abstract concept of civic responsibility becomes concrete and personal.

### Community Gardens

**Who They Are:** Shared garden spaces managed by neighborhood groups, nonprofits, or municipal parks departments where community members grow food and flowers together.

**What They Offer:**
- Extended growing space if school garden space is limited
- Intergenerational learning opportunities (students work alongside adult gardeners)
- Cultural exchange through diverse gardening practices
- Community events and harvest celebrations
- Models of cooperative management and shared responsibility

**Partnership Potential:** Community gardens provide a bridge between school and neighborhood, reinforcing the Reconnect phase's emphasis on applying learning beyond the classroom. They also offer opportunities for family engagement.

### Environmental Nonprofits

**Who They Are:** Organizations focused on environmental education, conservation, sustainability, and ecological restoration.

**What They Offer:**
- Environmental education programming (field trips, guest speakers, curriculum resources)
- Habitat restoration projects that align with science standards (NGSS)
- Biodiversity survey support and training
- Water quality monitoring equipment and expertise
- Climate science and sustainability education materials
- Connections to state and national environmental education networks

**Partnership Potential:** Environmental nonprofits extend the garden curriculum into broader ecological contexts, supporting the NGSS-aligned science instruction embedded in the RootWork model.

### Health Departments and Health Organizations

**Who They Are:** County health departments, community health centers, hospitals, and health-focused nonprofits.

**What They Offer:**
- Nutrition education programming
- School wellness policy support
- Health data that demonstrates the connection between garden-based learning and student health outcomes
- Access to health professionals for guest presentations (nutritionists, mental health counselors)
- Funding for health-related garden initiatives (nutrition education, physical activity)
- Mental health resources for trauma-informed practice support

**Partnership Potential:** Health partnerships reinforce the whole-child approach of the RootWork model, connecting garden-based learning to physical health, nutrition, and mental wellness.

### Universities and Research Institutions

**Who They Are:** Colleges and universities with programs in education, agriculture, public health, counseling, or related fields.

**What They Offer:**
- Research partnerships to evaluate program effectiveness
- Student teachers and practicum students who can support garden programs
- Graduate student researchers who can conduct studies on student outcomes
- Access to university garden and farm facilities
- Faculty expertise in trauma-informed practice, culturally responsive pedagogy, and garden-based education
- Potential for graduate credit pathways for RootWork-credentialed educators

**Partnership Potential:** University partnerships provide both the research rigor needed to demonstrate program impact and the human resources (student teachers, graduate assistants) that support implementation.

## Partnership Structures

Different partnerships require different structures depending on the level of engagement and resource commitment.

### Advisory Partnerships

**Description:** The organization provides guidance and consultation without direct program delivery. Advisory partners serve on a school or district garden advisory committee and offer expertise as needed.

**Examples:**
- A county extension agent serves on the school garden advisory committee and provides seasonal guidance
- A university faculty member reviews curriculum alignment and assessment design
- A health department representative advises on nutrition education integration

**Resource Commitment:** Low (2-4 meetings per year plus occasional consultations)
**Formalization:** Informal letter of support or advisory committee membership

### Resource Sharing Partnerships

**Description:** The organization shares physical resources, materials, or access to facilities with the school garden program.

**Examples:**
- A community garden provides growing space for school garden overflow
- A food bank provides seeds and seedlings at reduced cost
- A hardware store donates tools and materials for raised bed construction
- A nursery provides discounted plants and expert advice

**Resource Commitment:** Moderate (ongoing resource contributions)
**Formalization:** Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) specifying resource commitments, timeline, and responsibilities

### Program Delivery Partnerships

**Description:** The organization delivers educational programming directly to students, educators, or families as part of the RootWork curriculum.

**Examples:**
- Master Gardener volunteers lead weekly garden sessions with student groups
- An environmental nonprofit facilitates monthly biodiversity surveys
- A university sends student teachers to co-teach garden-integrated lessons
- A health department nutritionist leads seasonal cooking workshops for families

**Resource Commitment:** High (regular, scheduled engagement)
**Formalization:** Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) with detailed scope of work, schedule, roles, and evaluation metrics

### Research Partnerships

**Description:** The organization conducts formal research on the RootWork program's implementation and outcomes.

**Examples:**
- A university research team conducts a longitudinal study on student academic and SEL outcomes
- A public health researcher evaluates the program's impact on student nutrition behaviors
- An education policy institute studies the scalability and sustainability of the RootWork model

**Resource Commitment:** High (multi-year commitment with data sharing and reporting)
**Formalization:** Research agreement with IRB approval, data sharing protocols, and publication agreements

## Identification and Outreach Strategies

### Mapping Community Assets

Before reaching out to potential partners, conduct a community asset map to identify organizations that align with the RootWork mission.

**Asset Mapping Process:**

1. **Internal Inventory:** Survey school staff, families, and community members about existing connections to relevant organizations. Often, the strongest partnerships begin with personal relationships.

2. **Geographic Scan:** Identify organizations within a 10-mile radius of the school, including extension offices, community gardens, food banks, environmental groups, health organizations, and universities.

3. **Alignment Assessment:** For each identified organization, assess:
   - Mission alignment with RootWork values (trauma-informed, garden-based, culturally responsive)
   - Capacity to engage in a partnership (staff, resources, interest)
   - Existing relationships with the school or district
   - Potential contribution to the program (expertise, resources, volunteers, research)

4. **Priority Ranking:** Rank potential partners by alignment, capacity, and strategic value. Begin outreach with the top 3-5 organizations.

### Outreach Best Practices

1. **Lead with listening.** Before pitching a partnership, learn about the organization's mission, priorities, and current initiatives. Ask how the RootWork program might support their goals.

2. **Start small.** Begin with a low-commitment engagement (guest speaker, single event, resource donation) before proposing a formal partnership. This allows both parties to assess fit.

3. **Be specific about the ask.** Organizations are more likely to engage when they understand exactly what is needed: "We need a Master Gardener volunteer for 2 hours per week on Wednesday mornings" is more actionable than "We need garden help."

4. **Highlight mutual benefit.** Frame the partnership as reciprocal: the organization gains access to students, families, and community visibility while the school gains expertise, resources, and connections.

5. **Involve school leadership.** Partnerships are more sustainable when they have administrative support. Include the principal or a designated administrator in initial outreach conversations.

6. **Follow up consistently.** After an initial meeting, send a thank-you note, a summary of the conversation, and proposed next steps within one week.

## Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) Template

Formal partnerships should be documented in a Memorandum of Agreement that specifies:

### Essential MOA Components

1. **Parties:** Full legal names of the school/district and partner organization
2. **Purpose:** Statement of shared purpose and alignment with the RootWork mission
3. **Scope of Work:** Specific activities, services, or resources to be provided by each party
4. **Timeline:** Start date, end date, and renewal provisions
5. **Roles and Responsibilities:** Clear delineation of who does what, including a designated point of contact for each party
6. **Resource Commitments:** Specific resources (financial, material, human) committed by each party
7. **Data Sharing:** Protocols for sharing student data (if applicable), including FERPA compliance requirements
8. **Liability and Insurance:** Liability coverage for volunteers, guest instructors, and off-site activities
9. **Background Checks:** Requirements for any partner personnel working directly with students
10. **Evaluation:** How the partnership will be assessed, including metrics and reporting schedule
11. **Communication:** Frequency and format of partnership communication
12. **Termination:** Conditions under which either party may end the agreement
13. **Signatures:** Authorized signatures from both parties

## Managing Partnerships

### Partnership Coordinator Role

Designate a partnership coordinator at each school site. This person is responsible for:

- Maintaining communication with all partner organizations
- Scheduling partner activities and ensuring they align with the curriculum calendar
- Orienting partners to the school's trauma-informed practices and the 5Rs Framework
- Collecting and reporting partnership activity data
- Addressing issues or concerns as they arise
- Celebrating partnership contributions and sharing impact stories

### Orienting Partners to Trauma-Informed Practice

All community partners who interact directly with students must receive a brief orientation to the RootWork model's trauma-informed approach. The orientation covers:

- The 5Rs Framework overview (what it is and why it matters)
- Basic trauma-informed principles (felt safety, regulation, cultural responsiveness)
- Expectations for adult-student interactions (language, tone, response to dysregulation)
- Confidentiality requirements
- Emergency and safety protocols
- Who to contact with questions or concerns

This orientation protects students and empowers partners to engage effectively within the school's healing-centered culture.

### Communication Cadence

| Activity | Frequency | Format |
|----------|-----------|--------|
| Partnership check-in | Monthly | Email or brief phone call |
| Activity scheduling | As needed | Shared calendar and email confirmation |
| Progress update | Quarterly | Brief written report or meeting |
| Annual review | Annually | In-person meeting with evaluation data |
| Appreciation event | Annually | End-of-year celebration |

## Measuring Partnership Impact

### Partnership Impact Metrics

| Metric | Data Source | Frequency |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Number of partner interactions with students | Activity log | Monthly |
| Volunteer hours contributed | Sign-in sheets / tracking system | Monthly |
| Resources donated or provided | Donation records | Quarterly |
| Student participation in partner activities | Attendance records | Per event |
| Educator satisfaction with partnership | Survey | Annually |
| Student engagement during partner activities | Observation | Per event |
| Garden productivity attributable to partner support | Garden data | Seasonally |
| Community awareness of the school garden program | Community survey | Annually |
| Partner satisfaction and perceived value | Survey | Annually |

### Annual Partnership Review

At the end of each school year, conduct a formal partnership review that includes:

1. **Data Review:** Examine partnership impact metrics and compare to goals established in the MOA
2. **Stakeholder Feedback:** Gather input from educators, students, families, and partner representatives
3. **Strengths and Challenges:** Identify what worked well and what needs improvement
4. **Renewal Decision:** Decide whether to renew, modify, or conclude the partnership
5. **Celebration:** Acknowledge partner contributions publicly (school newsletter, social media, appreciation event)

Partnerships are living relationships, not static contracts. Annual review ensures they remain vibrant, relevant, and mutually beneficial.
