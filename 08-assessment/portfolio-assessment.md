# Portfolio Assessment

## Philosophy of Portfolio-Based Assessment

In the RootWork curriculum, the portfolio is the primary summative assessment instrument. It replaces the traditional test as the definitive record of what a student knows, can do, and has become over the course of a learning period. This choice is deliberate and principled.

A portfolio tells a story that a test score cannot. It shows growth over time, captures the full range of a student's expression, and honors the complexity of learning in a trauma-informed, garden-based environment. When a student selects their best work, reflects on their growth, and presents their learning to an authentic audience, they are doing something far more cognitively demanding and far more humanizing than filling in bubbles on a standardized form.

The portfolio philosophy rests on five commitments:

1. **Student agency.** Students choose what goes into their portfolio. This choice is itself an act of metacognition -- it requires students to evaluate their own work, identify what best represents their learning, and articulate why they selected each piece. In a trauma-informed context, this agency is therapeutic: students who have experienced powerlessness are given genuine control over how they are represented.

2. **Growth visibility.** Every portfolio includes early-stage and later-stage work on the same skills, making growth visible and concrete. A student who sees their September writing next to their March writing can internalize the reality that effort leads to improvement -- a growth mindset message that is shown, not merely told.

3. **Multiple domains.** Portfolios include evidence from academic, social-emotional, regulation, and garden stewardship domains. This whole-child representation ensures that a student who excels in the garden but struggles with written expression is seen in their fullness, not reduced to their weakest area.

4. **Authentic audience.** Portfolios are presented to families and community members during student-led conferences. This authentic audience raises the stakes in a healthy way -- students care about their portfolio because real people will see it -- while also strengthening the home-school connection.

5. **Reflection as learning.** The reflective writing that accompanies each portfolio artifact is where some of the deepest learning occurs. When a student writes "I chose this piece because it shows how I learned from my mistake," they are practicing metacognition, self-compassion, and academic discourse simultaneously.

## Portfolio Structure by Grade Band

The physical format and organizational structure of the portfolio evolves across grade bands, matching students' developmental capacities and increasing digital literacy.

### K-2: Physical Binder Portfolio

Young learners benefit from a tangible, hands-on portfolio they can touch, flip through, and share on their lap during conferences. The K-2 portfolio is a three-ring binder with tabbed dividers.

**Format Specifications:**
- 1.5-inch three-ring binder with clear-view front pocket for a student-decorated cover
- Five tabbed dividers (one for each portfolio section)
- Sheet protectors for original artwork and garden artifacts
- Photo sleeves for garden documentation photographs
- Pocket folders for three-dimensional items (seed packets, pressed leaves, small garden maps)

**Teacher Role:** At K-2, the teacher plays a significant role in portfolio assembly. The teacher helps students select artifacts, scribes student reflections (transitioning to student-written reflections as literacy develops), and maintains the physical organization of the binder. By the end of grade 2, students should be selecting artifacts and writing simple reflections with minimal teacher support.

### 3-5: Mixed Portfolio (Physical + Digital)

Students in grades 3-5 transition toward digital documentation while maintaining some physical elements. This grade band uses a physical binder supplemented by a digital folder.

**Format Specifications:**
- 1.5-inch three-ring binder for physical artifacts (artwork, handwritten reflections, garden samples)
- Digital folder on the school learning management system for typed work, photographs, data displays, and scanned documents
- Students begin learning to photograph and upload their own work
- Digital reflections typed using a simple template

**Teacher Role:** The teacher guides students in curating both physical and digital artifacts, teaches digital portfolio skills (photographing work, typing reflections, organizing files), and provides structured reflection prompts. By end of grade 5, students should independently manage both portfolio formats.

### 6-8: Digital Portfolio

Middle school students maintain a fully digital portfolio using the RootGuide platform or a school-approved digital portfolio tool.

**Format Specifications:**
- Digital portfolio hosted on RootGuide platform with student login
- Organized by section tabs mirroring the required components
- Multimedia capability: students may include photographs, videos, audio recordings, data visualizations, and written text
- Hyperlinked table of contents for navigation
- Reflection entries attached to each artifact as text or audio recording

**Teacher Role:** The teacher serves as a portfolio coach, helping students develop curation skills, write substantive reflections, and prepare for conferences. Students manage all technical aspects of the portfolio independently.

### 9-12: Professional Digital Portfolio

High school students maintain a portfolio that prepares them for post-secondary applications, job interviews, and community presentations.

**Format Specifications:**
- Professional digital portfolio hosted on RootGuide or a web-based portfolio platform (e.g., Google Sites, WordPress)
- Custom design reflecting student identity and aesthetic choices
- Organized by both domain sections and a curated "highlights" landing page
- Includes a personal statement, resume or skills summary, and a capstone project section
- Accessible via shareable link for college applications, scholarship submissions, and employer review

**Teacher Role:** The teacher acts as a mentor and editor, providing feedback on portfolio design, narrative voice, and professional presentation. Students own and manage the portfolio entirely.

## Required Components per Grade Band

Each portfolio must include artifacts from five required sections. The specific artifacts vary by grade band, reflecting increasing complexity and student autonomy.

### K-2 Required Components

| Section | Required Artifacts | Minimum Count | Notes |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|-------|
| **Academic Growth** | Writing samples (beginning, middle, end of year); math work samples showing reasoning; science observation drawings | 6 per year (2 per trimester) | Include at least one early and one later sample for growth comparison |
| **Garden Documentation** | Photographs of garden work; plant observation drawings; seed-to-harvest record for at least one plant | 4 per year | Teacher or student photographs with student-dictated or written captions |
| **TRACE Thinking** | Recorded examples of student reasoning (teacher-scribed or student-written TRACE responses) | 3 per year | Select examples that show thinking process, not just correct answers |
| **Regulation Growth** | Student-drawn "feelings check" samples from beginning and end of year; teacher-written narrative of regulation growth | 2 per year + 1 narrative | Focus on growth in self-awareness and strategy use |
| **Reflection** | Student-generated reflections on selected artifacts ("I chose this because...") | 1 per artifact | Teacher scribes for pre-writers; sentence starters provided for emerging writers |

### 3-5 Required Components

| Section | Required Artifacts | Minimum Count | Notes |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|-------|
| **Academic Growth** | Writing samples across genres; math problem-solving with written explanations; science investigation reports; garden data analysis | 8 per year (2 per quarter) | Must include at least two growth pairs (early + later work on same skill) |
| **Garden Documentation** | Garden journal excerpts; photographic garden timeline; data collection records (measurement, weather, yield); garden map or design | 6 per year | At least two artifacts must include data displays (tables, graphs, charts) |
| **TRACE Thinking** | Written TRACE responses showing reasoning across engagement modes; self-selected "best thinking" examples | 4 per year | Must include at least one error analysis artifact |
| **SEL and Regulation** | Goal-setting documents; self-assessment results; regulation strategy reflection; growth narrative co-written with teacher | 4 per year | Student writes the growth narrative with teacher support by grade 5 |
| **Reflection** | Substantive written reflections for each artifact explaining selection rationale, what it demonstrates, and next steps | 1 per artifact | Reflections use a structured template: What I chose / Why I chose it / What it shows about my growth / My next step |

### 6-8 Required Components

| Section | Required Artifacts | Minimum Count | Notes |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|-------|
| **Academic Growth** | Research writing; mathematical reasoning tasks with multiple solution pathways; lab reports; interdisciplinary projects connecting garden and academic content | 10 per year | Must include at least three growth pairs; at least one artifact per core subject area |
| **Garden Documentation** | Garden design projects; experimental documentation (hypothesis through conclusion); photographic essays; yield data analysis with statistical measures | 6 per year | At least two artifacts must demonstrate scientific thinking through garden investigation |
| **TRACE Thinking** | Self-recorded TRACE protocol responses (written or audio); peer feedback on reasoning; metacognitive reflection on thinking growth | 4 per year | Must include at least one example from each engagement mode used during the year |
| **SEL and Regulation** | Self-assessment with goal-setting; regulation strategy portfolio (list of personal strategies and when each works best); community contribution documentation | 4 per year | Student independently writes growth narrative |
| **Reflection** | Extended reflections (200+ words) analyzing growth, identifying patterns, and setting goals for continued development | 1 per artifact + 1 quarterly synthesis | Quarterly synthesis reflections address: What I've learned about myself as a learner this quarter |

### 9-12 Required Components

| Section | Required Artifacts | Minimum Count | Notes |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|-------|
| **Academic Growth** | Research papers; advanced mathematical modeling or data analysis; scientific investigations with publication-quality reports; cross-curricular capstone project documentation | 12 per year | Must demonstrate progression toward college or career readiness standards |
| **Garden and Community** | Garden leadership documentation; community project planning and outcomes; mentoring younger students; food justice or sustainability investigation | 6 per year | At least two artifacts must demonstrate leadership or mentorship |
| **TRACE and Critical Thinking** | Peer Socratic dialogue transcripts; self-directed inquiry documentation; evidence-based argument construction; teaching others using TRACE | 4 per year | Must include documentation of teaching TRACE to younger students |
| **SEL and Identity** | Personal growth narrative; cultural asset inventory; career or post-secondary readiness evidence; self-advocacy documentation | 4 per year | Personal statement suitable for college or scholarship applications |
| **Reflection** | Extended analytical reflections connecting specific artifacts to personal growth, academic identity, and future goals | 1 per artifact + 1 semester synthesis | Semester synthesis (500+ words) addresses: How has this learning shaped who I am becoming? |

## Student-Led Conference Protocol

Student-led conferences are the primary summative reporting event in the RootWork system. The student -- not the teacher -- leads the conference, presenting their portfolio to their family and narrating their own growth story.

### Conference Preparation (2-3 Weeks Before)

**Student Preparation Activities:**

| Week | Activity | Time | Description |
|------|----------|------|-------------|
| Week 1 | Artifact selection and organization | 30-45 min | Student reviews all work from the period and selects artifacts for each portfolio section |
| Week 1 | Reflection writing | 30-45 min | Student writes reflections for each new artifact using the grade-appropriate template |
| Week 2 | Presentation planning | 20-30 min | Student creates a simple presentation outline: opening, each section, growth highlight, goal statement, closing |
| Week 2 | Practice presentation | 15-20 min | Student practices presenting to a peer; peer provides feedback using a structured form |
| Week 3 | Final rehearsal | 15-20 min | Student does a full run-through with the teacher, who provides encouragement and gentle coaching |

**Family Preparation:**
- Conference invitation sent home 3 weeks in advance (printed and digital, translated as needed)
- Portfolio preview sent home 1 week before conference (3-5 selected artifacts with student reflections)
- Conversation starter card included: "Questions you might ask your child: What are you most proud of? What was hardest? What's your plan for next time?"
- Reminder communication 2 days before conference

### Conference Day Protocol

**Conference Structure (20-25 minutes per student):**

| Phase | Duration | What Happens | Teacher Role |
|-------|----------|-------------|-------------|
| Welcome | 2 min | Student greets family, explains the conference format, and shares the portfolio table of contents | Greet family warmly; facilitate introductions if needed; then step back |
| Academic Growth | 5-6 min | Student presents 2-3 academic artifacts, explaining what each shows about their learning and growth | Listen actively; add brief context if student gets stuck; avoid taking over |
| Garden and Community | 3-4 min | Student shares garden documentation, photographs, or projects | Offer a specific positive observation about the student's garden work |
| Thinking and SEL | 3-4 min | Student presents TRACE thinking examples and SEL growth evidence | Affirm the student's metacognitive awareness |
| Growth Highlight | 2-3 min | Student identifies their single proudest moment of growth and explains why | Celebrate genuinely; connect to specific effort or strategy the student used |
| Goals | 2-3 min | Student shares goals for the next period and invites family input | Affirm the goals; offer one concrete suggestion if appropriate |
| Family Response | 3-4 min | Family shares reactions, asks questions, and offers encouragement | Facilitate if conversation stalls; provide interpreter support if needed |
| Closing | 1-2 min | Student thanks family; teacher provides any additional context needed; family signs conference form | Add brief professional perspective; express genuine care for the student |

### Trauma-Informed Conference Adaptations

- **Flexible attendance:** If a student's family cannot attend, provide an alternative audience (another trusted adult, a counselor, an older student mentor). No student presents alone to an empty room.
- **Regulation support:** Regulation tools are available during the conference. If a student becomes dysregulated, the teacher gently pauses and offers support before continuing.
- **Content sensitivity:** Students are never required to include artifacts that reference personal trauma. Reflections focus on academic and social-emotional growth, not personal history.
- **Family dynamics:** If family dynamics are strained, the teacher meets with the student beforehand to plan for potential discomfort and establishes a subtle signal the student can use if they need support.
- **Celebration emphasis:** The conference is framed as a celebration of growth, not a report of deficits. The tone is warm, encouraging, and forward-looking.

## Portfolio Scoring Rubric

Portfolios are evaluated holistically at the end of each quarter using the following rubric. This rubric assesses the portfolio as a curated collection, not individual artifacts within it.

| Dimension | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Completeness** | Portfolio is missing multiple required artifacts; some sections are empty | Portfolio includes most required artifacts but some sections are thin or missing pieces | Portfolio includes all required artifacts across all sections | Portfolio exceeds requirements with additional self-selected artifacts that strengthen the growth story |
| **Growth Evidence** | Portfolio does not demonstrate growth; artifacts are static or random | Portfolio shows some evidence of growth but growth pairs are not clearly identified or compared | Portfolio includes clear growth pairs in multiple domains with explicit before/after comparison | Portfolio tells a compelling growth story across all domains; student articulates specific factors that contributed to growth |
| **Reflection Quality** | Reflections are absent or superficial ("I liked this") | Reflections describe the artifact but do not analyze what it demonstrates about learning | Reflections analyze what each artifact demonstrates, connect to learning goals, and identify next steps | Reflections demonstrate sophisticated metacognition; student connects individual artifacts to broader patterns of growth and personal identity as a learner |
| **Organization** | Portfolio is disorganized; artifacts are difficult to locate; no clear structure | Portfolio has some organizational structure but navigation is inconsistent | Portfolio is well-organized with clear sections, labeled artifacts, and logical sequencing | Portfolio design enhances the growth narrative; organization choices are themselves evidence of thoughtful curation |
| **Presentation** | Student cannot present the portfolio or presents only with significant teacher prompting | Student presents with support; relies on reading reflections rather than narrating; limited eye contact with audience | Student presents confidently; narrates growth story in own words; engages with audience; responds to questions | Student presents with poise and authenticity; engages audience through storytelling; demonstrates ownership and pride; invites meaningful dialogue |

**Scoring Notes:**
- The portfolio score is never averaged into a grade. It is reported separately as a "portfolio quality" indicator.
- Students who receive a 1 or 2 in any dimension receive specific coaching on that dimension before the next portfolio review.
- The emphasis is always on growth: a student who moves from a 1 to a 2 is celebrated for that progress.

## Digital Portfolio Technology Requirements

### Minimum Technology Requirements by Grade Band

| Grade Band | Device | Platform | Connectivity | Accessibility |
|-----------|--------|----------|-------------|---------------|
| K-2 | Shared classroom tablet or teacher device for photography | Teacher-managed folder in school LMS (physical binder is primary) | Not required for student use | N/A (physical format) |
| 3-5 | Shared or 1:1 Chromebook/tablet; digital camera or tablet camera | School LMS with student accounts; simple file organization | Reliable classroom internet; offline capability for drafting | Text-to-speech; adjustable font size; voice recording option |
| 6-8 | 1:1 Chromebook/laptop or shared device with individual accounts | RootGuide portfolio module or approved alternative (Google Sites, Seesaw) | Reliable school internet; home access option for optional work | Full accessibility features: screen reader compatibility, keyboard navigation, captioning for video, alt text for images |
| 9-12 | 1:1 device (school-provided or personal) | RootGuide portfolio module or professional web portfolio platform | Reliable school and home internet | Full accessibility; professional design tools; export capability for applications |

### Data Privacy and Security

- All digital portfolio platforms must comply with FERPA, COPPA (for students under 13), and applicable state privacy laws
- Student portfolios are accessible only to the student, their teacher(s), and their designated family members
- Portfolio data is backed up regularly and retained according to district records retention policy
- When students leave the school, they receive an exported copy of their digital portfolio; the school retains a copy per records policy
- Portfolios are never shared publicly without explicit written consent from the student and their family
- Student photographs in digital portfolios follow the school's photo consent policy

### Technical Support Structure

- The school technology coordinator provides initial setup and troubleshooting
- Teachers receive portfolio technology training during summer professional development and quarterly refresher sessions
- Grade 6-8 students receive a "Digital Portfolio Boot Camp" during the first two weeks of school
- Grade 9-12 students complete a "Professional Portfolio Design" mini-unit in the first month
- A printed quick-reference guide for portfolio technology is available in every classroom
