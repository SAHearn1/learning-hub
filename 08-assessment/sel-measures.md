# SEL Measurement Tools

## Introduction

Social-emotional learning in the RootWork curriculum is not an add-on program -- it is woven into every phase of the 5Rs framework and every moment of garden-based instruction. Because SEL is integral to the curriculum, its measurement must be equally integrated: not a separate battery of tests administered on special occasions, but a continuous, multi-source system of observation, self-reflection, and data collection that honors the complexity of human social-emotional development.

Measuring social-emotional competence raises unique challenges that academic measurement does not. SEL skills are context-dependent: a student may demonstrate strong empathy in a one-on-one garden partnership but struggle with social awareness in a large-group setting. SEL development is culturally situated: what constitutes "appropriate" self-expression, conflict resolution, or relationship-building varies across cultures. And SEL is deeply personal: data about a student's emotional life must be handled with far more sensitivity than data about their reading fluency.

This document describes the RootWork approach to SEL measurement, including the CASEL-aligned framework, observation protocols, student self-assessment tools, regulation tracking instruments, family surveys, data aggregation methods, and the ethical principles that govern all SEL data use.

## CASEL-Aligned Measurement Framework

The Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) identifies five core competencies that form the organizing structure for RootWork SEL measurement. Each competency is mapped to observable indicators within the garden-based, trauma-informed context of the curriculum.

| CASEL Competency | Definition | RootWork Context | Key Observable Indicators |
|-----------------|-----------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| **Self-Awareness** | The ability to recognize one's own emotions, thoughts, and values and understand how they influence behavior | Regulation check-ins, growth journal reflections, TRACE metacognition, garden observation | Accurately names current emotional state; identifies personal strengths and growth areas; recognizes how emotions affect learning; demonstrates growth mindset language |
| **Self-Management** | The ability to regulate one's emotions, thoughts, and behaviors effectively in different situations | Regulation strategy use, goal-setting, sustained engagement during TRACE, garden task completion | Uses regulation strategies independently; sets and monitors personal goals; manages impulses during group work; sustains focus during challenging tasks; transitions between activities smoothly |
| **Social Awareness** | The ability to take the perspective of and empathize with others, including those from diverse backgrounds | Garden collaboration, peer feedback, community projects, cultural food exchanges | Demonstrates empathy during peer interactions; recognizes and respects cultural differences in the garden; considers others' feelings before acting; shows interest in peers' perspectives and experiences |
| **Relationship Skills** | The ability to establish and maintain healthy and supportive relationships | Garden buddy partnerships, collaborative projects, student-led conferences, peer mentoring | Communicates needs and boundaries clearly; cooperates during shared garden tasks; resolves conflicts constructively; seeks help when needed; offers support to peers appropriately |
| **Responsible Decision-Making** | The ability to make caring and constructive choices about personal behavior and social interactions | Garden stewardship, resource sharing, community contribution, ethical reasoning during TRACE | Considers consequences before acting; makes thoughtful choices about garden resource use; balances personal desires with group needs; takes responsibility for mistakes; contributes to classroom and garden community |

### Measurement Schedule

| Competency | Measurement Method | Frequency | Primary Data Source |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Self-Awareness | Observation checklist + student self-assessment | Quarterly (checklist), ongoing (self-assessment embedded in daily check-ins) | Teacher observation, student self-report |
| Self-Management | Regulation tracking data aggregation + goal-tracking review | Daily (regulation data), quarterly (goal review) | Teacher observation, RootGuide regulation dashboard |
| Social Awareness | Observation during collaborative work + peer feedback (grades 6+) | Quarterly observation cycle; peer feedback twice per year (fall, spring) | Teacher observation, peer survey |
| Relationship Skills | Observation during garden partnerships and group work + conflict resolution documentation | Quarterly observation cycle; ongoing conflict documentation | Teacher observation, counselor records |
| Responsible Decision-Making | Garden stewardship rubric + student reflection journal review | Quarterly (rubric), monthly (journal review) | Teacher observation, student reflection |

## Observation Protocols for Each SEL Competency

### General Observation Guidelines

- Observe during natural activities (garden work, TRACE interactions, collaborative tasks), never during contrived assessment situations
- Observe each student across multiple contexts before drawing conclusions -- a student may present differently in the garden than in the classroom
- Record observations as specific, factual descriptions of behavior, not interpretive judgments
- Observe strengths as carefully as you observe challenges
- Never observe or record during moments of active dysregulation; these are not fair representations of a student's competence

### Self-Awareness Observation Protocol

**When to Observe:** During Root phase check-ins, growth journal writing, TRACE Articulate step, and portfolio reflection activities.

**What to Look For:**

| Indicator | Beginning | Approaching | Meeting | Extending |
|----------|-----------|-------------|---------|-----------|
| Emotion identification | Does not yet label emotional states; may use "fine" or "good" for all states | Labels basic emotions (happy, sad, mad) with prompting | Identifies a range of emotions accurately and independently, including nuanced states (frustrated, overwhelmed, excited, anxious) | Identifies emotions in real time and articulates their connection to context ("I'm feeling anxious because this problem is harder than what we did yesterday") |
| Strengths recognition | Does not identify personal strengths; may deflect compliments | Identifies one or two strengths when asked directly | Articulates multiple strengths across academic, social, and garden domains | Connects strengths to identity and goal-setting; leverages strengths to support growth areas |
| Growth mindset awareness | Uses fixed language about self without awareness | Shows awareness of fixed language when it is pointed out | Catches own fixed-mindset language and self-corrects | Reflects on mindset patterns in journal writing; identifies triggers for fixed-mindset thinking |

**Recording Format:** Checklist with date, context, specific behavior observed, and level (1-4). Enter into RootGuide quarterly.

### Self-Management Observation Protocol

**When to Observe:** During transitions between activities, during challenging TRACE tasks, during regulation strategy use, and during goal-monitoring check-ins.

**What to Look For:**

| Indicator | Beginning | Approaching | Meeting | Extending |
|----------|-----------|-------------|---------|-----------|
| Regulation strategy use | Does not yet use strategies independently; relies on adult co-regulation | Uses a strategy when offered by an adult; limited personal strategy repertoire | Independently selects and uses strategies from a personal toolkit; matches strategy to arousal level | Has an extensive, flexible strategy repertoire; modifies strategies based on context; shares strategies with peers |
| Goal pursuit | Does not yet set or track personal goals | Sets goals with adult support; may forget goals between sessions | Sets realistic goals independently; monitors progress using growth journal or checklist | Sets ambitious but achievable goals; adjusts goals based on progress data; reflects on the goal-setting process itself |
| Impulse management | Frequently acts on impulse; difficulty waiting turns or sharing materials | Manages impulses in low-stress situations; struggles when excited or frustrated | Manages impulses consistently across most situations; uses self-talk or physical strategies to pause before acting | Models self-management for peers; articulates strategies used; maintains composure even during high-emotion moments |

### Social Awareness Observation Protocol

**When to Observe:** During garden buddy partnerships, collaborative group work, community visitors, cultural food activities, and conflict situations.

**What to Look For:**

| Indicator | Beginning | Approaching | Meeting | Extending |
|----------|-----------|-------------|---------|-----------|
| Empathy | Focuses primarily on own experience; may not notice peers' emotional states | Notices when a peer is upset but may not know how to respond | Recognizes peers' emotions and responds with appropriate care (checking in, offering space, getting an adult) | Anticipates others' emotional needs; adjusts own behavior to support peers; demonstrates empathy across cultural differences |
| Perspective-taking | Sees situations only from own viewpoint | Acknowledges that others may see things differently when prompted | Actively considers others' perspectives before forming opinions or making decisions | Seeks out diverse perspectives; articulates how different experiences shape different viewpoints; advocates for including marginalized voices |
| Cultural respect | Participates in cultural activities without engagement | Shows curiosity about cultural differences when they arise | Demonstrates genuine respect for cultural differences; asks respectful questions; shares own cultural knowledge | Actively bridges cultural differences; facilitates cross-cultural understanding; challenges stereotypes when encountered |

### Relationship Skills Observation Protocol

**When to Observe:** During all collaborative activities, garden partnerships, peer feedback sessions, conflict moments, and student-led conferences.

**What to Look For:**

| Indicator | Beginning | Approaching | Meeting | Extending |
|----------|-----------|-------------|---------|-----------|
| Communication | Communicates needs through behavior rather than words; may withdraw or act out | Communicates basic needs verbally when supported; may struggle with assertiveness | Communicates needs, boundaries, and ideas clearly and respectfully; uses "I" statements | Adapts communication style to different audiences and contexts; facilitates communication between peers; mediates misunderstandings |
| Cooperation | Works alongside peers but does not yet coordinate efforts; may dominate or withdraw during group tasks | Cooperates during structured tasks with clear roles; may struggle with unstructured collaboration | Cooperates effectively in both structured and unstructured tasks; contributes fairly; adapts to different roles | Takes on diverse roles depending on group need; supports struggling group members; facilitates equitable participation |
| Conflict resolution | Conflicts escalate quickly; may respond with aggression, withdrawal, or seeking adult rescue | Accepts adult-mediated conflict resolution; beginning to use words instead of actions | Resolves minor conflicts independently using learned strategies (I-messages, compromise, taking space); seeks adult help for major conflicts | Mediates conflicts between peers; identifies root causes of conflict; proposes solutions that address everyone's needs |

### Responsible Decision-Making Observation Protocol

**When to Observe:** During garden stewardship activities, resource-sharing situations, group decision-making, and ethical discussions.

**What to Look For:**

| Indicator | Beginning | Approaching | Meeting | Extending |
|----------|-----------|-------------|---------|-----------|
| Consequence consideration | Acts without considering consequences; surprised by outcomes | Considers immediate consequences when prompted; may not consider long-term or others' perspectives | Independently considers both immediate and long-term consequences for self and others before making decisions | Weighs complex factors including community impact, environmental sustainability, and ethical implications |
| Garden stewardship | Needs regular reminders about care for plants, tools, and shared spaces | Follows garden care expectations when reminded; occasional lapses | Consistently demonstrates responsible garden stewardship; notices and addresses needs independently | Takes leadership in garden stewardship; develops and proposes new care systems; mentors others in responsible practices |
| Accountability | Denies or blames others when mistakes happen | Acknowledges mistakes when evidence is clear; may need support to move from acknowledgment to repair | Takes responsibility for mistakes promptly; initiates repair without being asked | Models accountability; helps create a classroom culture where mistakes are learning opportunities; supports peers in taking responsibility |

## Student Self-Assessment Tools by Grade Band

### K-2: Picture-Based Self-Assessment

Young students use visual tools to reflect on their social-emotional experience:

**Daily Feeling Check (used during Root phase):**
- Students point to or place a marker on a visual mood meter showing five faces/colors representing arousal states (very low, low, calm-alert, high, very high)
- Students may optionally name their feeling or point to a feelings chart
- Teacher records the data; students are never questioned or corrected about their self-report

**Quarterly Self-Assessment (completed with teacher support):**
- Uses a "garden growth" visual scale: Seed, Sprout, Growing Plant, Flowering Plant
- Teacher reads each statement; student points to the level that feels right
- Sample statements: "I can tell how I'm feeling" / "I can calm myself down when I'm upset" / "I am kind to my garden buddies" / "I take care of our garden"

### 3-5: Guided Self-Assessment Survey

Students complete a simple written survey with pictorial support:

**Quarterly Self-Assessment Format:**
- 15-20 statements organized by CASEL competency
- Students respond on a 4-point scale: "Not Yet" / "Sometimes" / "Usually" / "Almost Always"
- Each statement includes a brief example for clarity

**Sample Statements by Competency:**

| Competency | Sample Statement |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Self-Awareness | "I can name how I'm feeling even when it's a complicated feeling" |
| Self-Management | "When I feel frustrated during a hard problem, I use a strategy to calm down before giving up" |
| Social Awareness | "I try to understand how my classmates feel, even when their experience is different from mine" |
| Relationship Skills | "When I have a disagreement with a garden buddy, I try to solve it by talking it out" |
| Responsible Decision-Making | "I think about how my choices affect our garden and our class before I act" |

### 6-8: Reflective Self-Assessment

Students complete a more sophisticated self-assessment that includes both rating scales and open-ended reflection:

**Quarterly Self-Assessment Format:**
- 25 statements with a 4-point rating scale (Beginning / Approaching / Meeting / Extending)
- 3 open-ended reflection prompts per competency
- Students identify their top two strengths and top two growth areas across all competencies
- Students set one specific SEL goal for the next quarter

**Sample Open-Ended Prompts:**
- Self-Awareness: "Describe a time this quarter when you noticed an emotion affecting your learning. What did you do?"
- Self-Management: "What regulation strategy worked best for you this quarter? When did it work, and when did it not work?"
- Social Awareness: "Describe a time you saw something from a perspective different from your own. What did you learn?"

### 9-12: Analytical Self-Assessment

High school students complete a comprehensive self-assessment that connects SEL competencies to identity, community, and future goals:

**Semester Self-Assessment Format:**
- Self-rating on all CASEL competencies with evidence citations from portfolio
- Written narrative (300+ words) analyzing SEL growth across the semester
- Connection to personal values, cultural identity, and post-secondary goals
- Identification of SEL strengths they want to leverage in leadership roles
- Action plan for continued SEL development

## Regulation Tracking Instruments

### Daily Regulation Check-In Record

Teachers maintain a daily log for each student using a simple coding system:

```
Student: _______________    Date: _______________

Morning Check-In:    VL  /  L  /  CA  /  H  /  VH     Strategy Used: ____________
Mid-Session Check:   VL  /  L  /  CA  /  H  /  VH     Strategy Used: ____________
End-of-Session:      VL  /  L  /  CA  /  H  /  VH     Strategy Used: ____________

Co-Regulation Episodes: ___  (count)
Recovery Time (if applicable): ___ minutes
Dysregulation Trigger (if identifiable): ________________________________
Notes: _____________________________________________________________
```

**Codes:** VL = Very Low (shutdown/withdrawal), L = Low (lethargic/disengaged), CA = Calm-Alert (optimal learning zone), H = High (agitated/restless), VH = Very High (fight/flight/freeze response)

### Weekly Regulation Summary

At the end of each week, teachers complete a brief summary that captures patterns:

| Weekly Summary Item | Data Source | Purpose |
|--------------------|-------------|---------|
| Most frequent arousal state | Daily check-in aggregation | Identify baseline state and trends |
| Most effective strategies | Strategy use observations | Inform individualized regulation plan |
| Co-regulation need trend | Daily co-regulation count | Track progress toward autonomy |
| Average recovery time | Episode-level data | Monitor regulation capacity growth |
| Environmental factors | Teacher observation and notes | Identify contextual triggers and supports |
| Week-over-week change | Comparison with prior weeks | Detect improvements, regressions, or stability |

### Monthly Regulation Growth Narrative

Once per month, teachers write a brief (3-5 sentence) narrative summarizing each student's regulation growth. This narrative is included in the RootGuide dashboard and informs family progress reports.

**Example Narrative:** "This month, Jaylen showed significant growth in his ability to identify when he's moving from Calm-Alert to High arousal. He independently used the garden breathing station three times this week without prompting -- a first. His average recovery time after dysregulation has decreased from about 12 minutes in September to about 6 minutes now. We're continuing to build his 'calm-down toolkit' with a focus on strategies he can use during TRACE when problems get challenging."

## Teacher Observation Checklists

### Quarterly SEL Observation Checklist

This checklist is completed once per quarter for each student. Teachers observe across multiple sessions and contexts before completing the checklist, which takes approximately 3-5 minutes per student.

**Format:** Each indicator is rated as B (Beginning), A (Approaching), M (Meeting), or E (Extending).

**Self-Awareness Indicators:**
- [ ] Accurately identifies own emotional state during check-ins
- [ ] Recognizes how emotions affect learning behavior
- [ ] Identifies personal strengths and areas for growth
- [ ] Demonstrates growth mindset language about self

**Self-Management Indicators:**
- [ ] Uses regulation strategies independently
- [ ] Manages transitions between activities smoothly
- [ ] Sustains focus during challenging academic tasks
- [ ] Sets and monitors personal goals

**Social Awareness Indicators:**
- [ ] Shows empathy when peers are struggling
- [ ] Considers others' perspectives in discussions
- [ ] Respects cultural differences in the garden community
- [ ] Notices and responds to group emotional climate

**Relationship Skills Indicators:**
- [ ] Communicates needs and boundaries clearly
- [ ] Cooperates during collaborative garden and academic tasks
- [ ] Resolves conflicts constructively
- [ ] Seeks and offers help appropriately

**Responsible Decision-Making Indicators:**
- [ ] Considers consequences before acting
- [ ] Demonstrates responsible garden stewardship
- [ ] Balances personal needs with group needs
- [ ] Takes responsibility for mistakes and initiates repair

## Family Survey Instruments

### Annual Family SEL Survey

Administered twice per year (fall and spring), this survey gathers family perspectives on their child's social-emotional development. It is available in all home languages represented in the school community and in both digital and paper formats.

**Survey Structure:**

| Section | Number of Items | Item Type | Sample Item |
|---------|----------------|-----------|-------------|
| Home SEL Observations | 10 | 4-point rating scale (Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Very Often) | "My child can describe how they are feeling using words" |
| Garden Program Impact | 5 | 4-point rating scale | "My child talks about what they are learning in the garden at home" |
| Regulation at Home | 5 | 4-point rating scale | "My child uses calming strategies when they are upset at home" |
| Family Strengths | 3 | Open-ended | "What strengths does your child bring to your family?" |
| Family Concerns | 2 | Open-ended | "Is there anything about your child's social or emotional development that you would like us to know?" |
| Program Feedback | 3 | Open-ended | "How has the RootWork program affected your child at home?" |

**Administration Guidelines:**
- Surveys are sent home with a personal cover letter from the classroom teacher explaining the purpose
- A pre-paid return envelope or a digital link is provided
- Families who do not return the survey receive a gentle follow-up; participation is voluntary and never pressured
- Survey responses are confidential and used only to support the child's programming
- Results are shared with families during student-led conferences in the context of collaborative goal-setting

## Data Aggregation and Reporting

### Individual Student SEL Profile

Each student's SEL data is aggregated into a profile within the RootGuide platform that includes:

- **Current proficiency levels** for each CASEL competency (from teacher observation checklist)
- **Self-assessment results** compared with teacher assessment (to identify alignment or discrepancy)
- **Regulation trajectory** (daily data aggregated to weekly and monthly trends)
- **Family survey highlights** (key observations from home context)
- **Growth narrative** (teacher-written monthly narrative)
- **Goal status** (current SEL goals and progress toward them)

### Class-Level SEL Summary

Teachers review class-level SEL data quarterly to identify patterns and inform instruction:

| Data Point | Purpose | Action If Concerning |
|-----------|---------|---------------------|
| Class average by competency | Identify which competencies need more instructional focus for the whole group | Increase explicit instruction and practice in that competency area |
| Distribution of proficiency levels | Identify students who may need targeted SEL support | Refer to MTSS process for Tier 2 SEL intervention |
| Regulation climate trend | Monitor whether the classroom emotional climate is improving, stable, or declining | Adjust classroom structures, increase regulation supports, investigate environmental factors |
| Self-assessment/teacher-assessment alignment | Identify students whose self-perception differs significantly from observed behavior | Schedule a supportive conversation to explore the discrepancy; adjust instruction |

### School-Level SEL Report

Administrators receive a quarterly school-level SEL summary that includes:

- Percentage of students at each proficiency level by competency and grade band
- Regulation climate trends across classrooms
- Aggregated family survey themes
- Recommendations for professional development focus areas
- Resource allocation needs for SEL programming

## Using SEL Data to Inform Instruction

SEL data is only valuable if it changes practice. The following decision rules connect SEL data to instructional action:

### Individual-Level Decisions

| Data Pattern | Instructional Response |
|-------------|----------------------|
| Student self-assesses significantly higher than teacher observation | Open a curious, non-judgmental conversation: "I noticed you rated yourself 'Usually' for managing frustration. Can you tell me about a time when that was true?" Explore whether the student needs support recognizing their patterns or whether the teacher's observations are incomplete |
| Student shows strong academic performance but low SEL scores | Investigate whether academic success is masking emotional distress; increase relationship-building; offer leadership opportunities that build social-emotional skills alongside academic ones |
| Student shows strong SEL skills but low academic performance | Rule out skill gaps that are creating frustration and undermining the student's naturally strong SEL; provide targeted academic support; leverage SEL strengths as a pathway to academic engagement |
| Regulation data shows regression after a period of growth | Do not panic or label the student. Check for environmental triggers (home changes, peer conflict, seasonal factors). Increase co-regulation support temporarily. Communicate with family. Document and monitor |
| Student consistently rates low in Social Awareness | Create structured opportunities for perspective-taking (garden buddy partnerships with diverse peers, community interviews, literature discussions about diverse experiences) |

### Class-Level Decisions

| Data Pattern | Instructional Response |
|-------------|----------------------|
| Whole class shows low scores in Relationship Skills | Increase explicit instruction in communication and conflict resolution; introduce or refresh collaborative learning structures with clear roles; increase garden partner work with structured reflection |
| Regulation climate data shows increasing dysregulation across the class | Examine schedule, transitions, and environmental factors; increase the length of Root and Regulate phases; add additional movement and sensory breaks; consult with counselor or administrator |
| Self-Management scores are low at a particular grade band | Review whether expectations are developmentally appropriate; increase explicit self-management instruction and practice; provide more scaffolded goal-setting activities |

## Ethical Considerations in SEL Measurement

The measurement of social-emotional competencies carries ethical responsibilities that academic measurement does not. SEL data touches on the most personal dimensions of a child's life -- their emotions, their relationships, their family functioning, their trauma history. The following principles are non-negotiable in the RootWork system.

### 1. SEL Data Is Never Punitive

SEL data is used exclusively to support students. It is never used to discipline, restrict access to activities, assign detention, or justify exclusion. A student who scores "Beginning" in Self-Management is not a behavior problem -- they are a student who needs more support. Low SEL scores trigger increased support and relationship investment, never consequences.

### 2. Confidentiality Is Paramount

SEL data is shared only among educators with a direct instructional relationship with the student, the student's family, and the student themselves (in age-appropriate ways). SEL data is never displayed publicly, discussed in staff meetings where non-relevant personnel are present, or shared with outside agencies without explicit written family consent.

### 3. Cultural Humility in Interpretation

SEL expression varies significantly across cultures. A student who does not make eye contact may be demonstrating cultural respect, not avoiding engagement. A student who defers to authority rather than self-advocating may be following cultural norms, not lacking assertiveness. SEL observation must be interpreted through a culturally humble lens, and families must be consulted when cultural factors may be influencing SEL presentation.

### 4. Student Consent and Voice

Students have the right to understand what SEL data is being collected about them, why, and how it will be used. At every grade band, students are told (in age-appropriate language) that their teachers notice and care about how they feel and get along with others, and that this information helps teachers support them better. At grades 6 and above, students have the right to review their own SEL data and offer their perspective on its accuracy.

### 5. Avoiding Pathologizing Normal Development

Children and adolescents are still developing social-emotional competencies. A kindergartener who cannot yet resolve conflicts independently is not deficient -- they are five years old. A middle schooler who struggles with impulse control during emotionally charged situations is developing normally. SEL measurement must be anchored in developmental expectations, not adult standards.

### 6. Trauma Sensitivity in Data Collection

Some SEL assessment questions can trigger trauma responses. Questions about family relationships, emotional regulation at home, or past experiences must be phrased carefully and administered in safe, private settings. If a student becomes dysregulated during SEL self-assessment, the assessment is stopped immediately and the student is offered regulation support. The assessment can be completed another day or skipped entirely without penalty.

### 7. Data Security

Digital SEL data is stored with the same or greater security protections as academic data. Access is role-based and auditable. SEL data is never included in data sets shared for research purposes without explicit family consent and IRB approval. When students transition between schools, SEL data is transferred only with family consent and is accompanied by contextual narrative that prevents misinterpretation.

## Conclusion

Measuring social-emotional learning is an act of care, not surveillance. When done well, it helps educators see each student more clearly, respond more effectively, and create learning environments where both academic growth and emotional well-being can flourish. The garden reminds us that healthy growth requires healthy soil -- and in the RootWork curriculum, SEL measurement is how we tend the soil of the classroom community. We observe not to judge, but to understand. We measure not to sort, but to serve.
