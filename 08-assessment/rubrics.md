# Rubrics

## Rubric Design Principles for Trauma-Informed Contexts

Rubrics in the RootWork curriculum are not instruments of judgment -- they are maps that help students understand where they are and where they are headed. In a trauma-informed context, rubric design carries particular weight because students who have experienced adversity are often hypervigilant to criticism, prone to shame spirals when they perceive failure, and conditioned to interpret evaluation as a threat rather than a support.

The following principles govern all rubric design in this curriculum:

### 1. Growth-Oriented Language

Every descriptor at every level is written in language that conveys forward movement. The lowest level is never described as "failing" or "poor" -- it is described as "beginning" or "emerging," language that implies a trajectory rather than a destination. Descriptors focus on what the student IS doing, not what they are NOT doing.

**Example:**
- Deficit language (avoid): "Student cannot explain their reasoning"
- Growth language (use): "Student is developing the language to express their thinking; currently communicates through gestures, drawings, or single words"

### 2. Observable Behaviors Only

Rubric descriptors describe observable actions, not inferred traits. A rubric never assesses "effort," "motivation," or "attitude" -- these are internal states that cannot be reliably observed and that carry particular risk in trauma-informed settings where a student's apparent "lack of effort" may actually reflect a freeze response, dissociation, or hypervigilance.

### 3. Strengths at Every Level

Each level of the rubric describes genuine competencies, even at the Beginning level. A student at level 1 is doing something -- observing, attempting, exploring -- and the rubric names what they are doing, not merely what they have not yet achieved. This ensures that every student can see themselves as a learner with real capabilities.

### 4. Clear, Concrete Descriptors

Rubric language avoids vague qualifiers like "good," "adequate," or "excellent." Instead, descriptors use specific, observable criteria that both teachers and students can identify without ambiguity. "Explains thinking in complete sentences using at least two academic vocabulary terms" is more useful than "provides a good explanation."

### 5. Student-Accessible Versions

Every rubric has two versions: a teacher version with full professional language and a student-friendly version with simplified language, visual supports (for K-2), and examples. Students should be able to self-assess using the rubric without teacher interpretation.

### 6. Culturally Neutral Criteria

Rubric criteria assess knowledge and skill, not cultural norms around communication style, eye contact, self-expression, or assertiveness. A student who demonstrates strong reasoning through quiet, methodical work scores the same as a student who demonstrates the same reasoning through animated verbal explanation.

## Academic Performance Rubrics

### Mathematical Reasoning Rubric

This rubric is used to assess student mathematical thinking during garden-based math activities, TRACE protocol math interactions, and math performance tasks.

| Dimension | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Problem Comprehension** | Engages with the problem context (garden scenario) but does not yet identify the mathematical question | Identifies the mathematical question with support; may focus on surface features rather than mathematical structure | Independently identifies the mathematical question and relevant information; distinguishes between needed and extraneous information | Identifies the mathematical structure underlying the problem; recognizes connections to previously solved problems; poses related questions |
| **Strategy Selection** | Attempts a strategy (counting, drawing, guessing) but may not connect the strategy to the problem structure | Selects a viable strategy with prompting; may need help getting started | Independently selects an appropriate strategy and explains why it fits the problem; adjusts strategy when initial approach is unproductive | Considers multiple strategies before selecting the most efficient; explains trade-offs between approaches; creates novel strategies for unfamiliar problems |
| **Execution and Accuracy** | Applies the chosen strategy with errors in computation or procedure; may lose track of the problem during execution | Applies the strategy with minor errors; computation is mostly accurate; may need support to complete multi-step problems | Executes the strategy accurately and completely; computation is reliable; carries work through to a solution | Executes with precision and efficiency; recognizes and corrects errors independently during execution; work is systematic and well-organized |
| **Communication of Reasoning** | Provides an answer without explanation; may use gestures or drawings to show some thinking | Explains some steps of the reasoning but explanation has gaps; uses everyday language rather than mathematical vocabulary | Explains reasoning clearly and completely using appropriate mathematical vocabulary; explanation would allow another student to follow the same approach | Communicates reasoning with precision, using mathematical notation and vocabulary fluently; anticipates potential confusion and addresses it; justifies each step |
| **Verification and Reflection** | Does not check the answer; accepts the first result | Checks the answer when prompted; can identify if the answer is reasonable | Independently checks the answer using a different method; evaluates whether the answer makes sense in the garden context | Develops a habit of verification; explains why verification matters; identifies implications of the solution and extends to new questions |

**Garden Context Example (Grade 3-5):** "Our garden bed is 12 feet long and 4 feet wide. We want to plant lettuce seedlings 6 inches apart in rows that are 1 foot apart. How many seedlings do we need?"

### Scientific Investigation Rubric

This rubric assesses student scientific thinking during garden-based investigations, lab activities, and science performance tasks.

| Dimension | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Questioning** | Makes observations in the garden but does not yet formulate questions | Asks questions about garden observations; questions may be too broad or not testable ("Why do plants grow?") | Asks focused, testable questions based on specific garden observations ("Does the amount of sunlight affect how fast basil grows?") | Asks refined questions that build on prior investigations; identifies variables and considers confounding factors in the question itself |
| **Investigation Design** | Participates in teacher-designed investigations; follows procedures with support | Contributes ideas to investigation design; identifies what to measure but may not control variables | Designs a fair investigation with identified independent, dependent, and controlled variables; plans data collection method | Designs sophisticated investigations that account for multiple variables, replication, and potential sources of error; modifies design based on preliminary observations |
| **Data Collection and Analysis** | Collects observations using senses; records with drawings or simple descriptions | Collects quantitative and qualitative data with some organizational structure; creates simple data displays | Collects accurate, well-organized data; creates appropriate data displays (tables, graphs); identifies patterns in data | Collects precise data with appropriate units and repeated trials; selects the most effective display for the data type; uses statistical reasoning to analyze patterns |
| **Evidence-Based Reasoning** | Shares what happened but does not connect observations to the question | Connects some observations to the question but reasoning has logical gaps | Uses specific data as evidence to answer the question; distinguishes between observation and inference | Constructs evidence-based arguments that address alternative explanations; acknowledges limitations of the evidence; connects findings to broader scientific concepts |
| **Communication** | Shares findings through drawings, gestures, or brief statements | Communicates findings in a structured format (oral report, poster) with key elements present | Communicates findings clearly using scientific conventions (introduction, methods, results, discussion); uses appropriate scientific vocabulary | Communicates findings in a format suitable for a scientific audience; writing is clear, well-organized, and uses domain-specific language precisely |

### Written Communication Rubric

This rubric is used across all subject areas when written expression is the mode of demonstration.

| Dimension | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Ideas and Content** | Communicates a single idea or observation, often through drawing with some labeling or brief text | Communicates a main idea with some supporting details; ideas may be loosely connected | Develops a clear main idea with relevant, specific details from garden experience or academic content; ideas are logically connected | Develops a nuanced, original main idea with rich, well-selected details; makes insightful connections between ideas; engages the reader's thinking |
| **Organization** | Ideas are presented in no particular order; beginning and end may not be distinct | Has a recognizable beginning, middle, and end; some ideas are grouped logically | Uses a clear organizational structure appropriate to the genre; transitions connect ideas; structure supports the reader's understanding | Organizational structure enhances the writing's purpose; transitions are varied and effective; structure feels purposeful and crafted |
| **Voice and Word Choice** | Uses familiar, everyday words; voice is not yet distinct | Shows some awareness of audience; begins to select words for clarity; moments of distinctive voice emerge | Uses precise vocabulary including academic and garden-specific terms; voice is consistent and appropriate to purpose; word choice is deliberate | Voice is authentic and engaging; word choice is vivid, precise, and varied; language choices create tone and mood; academic vocabulary is used naturally |
| **Conventions** | Uses some letters and spaces; spelling reflects early phonetic understanding; punctuation is emerging | Spells most high-frequency words correctly; uses basic punctuation (periods, capitals); errors do not significantly impede understanding | Demonstrates grade-level control of spelling, punctuation, grammar, and formatting; errors are minor and do not impede understanding | Demonstrates strong command of conventions; uses punctuation and grammar for stylistic effect; formatting enhances readability |

**Note:** Conventions are assessed at grade level. A first-grader who uses inventive spelling with clear phonetic logic and a second-grader who spells all high-frequency words correctly may both receive a 3 at their respective grade levels.

### Oral Presentation Rubric

This rubric assesses student presentations during TRACE sharing, student-led conferences, garden exhibitions, and other oral communication contexts.

| Dimension | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Content Knowledge** | Shares limited information; may read directly from notes or artifacts | Shares relevant information with some gaps; relies heavily on notes but adds some original narration | Demonstrates solid understanding of content; speaks primarily from knowledge rather than notes; addresses all key points | Demonstrates deep, flexible understanding; speaks with authority; seamlessly integrates details, examples, and connections |
| **Organization** | Presentation lacks clear structure; ideas are presented in random order | Presentation has a recognizable beginning and end; some ideas are grouped logically | Presentation follows a clear structure; ideas build on each other; transitions are present | Presentation is purposefully structured to engage the audience; narrative arc is compelling; transitions are smooth and intentional |
| **Delivery** | Speaks very quietly or inaudibly; significant difficulty sustaining the presentation | Speaks audibly with prompting; maintains the presentation with some pauses for support | Speaks clearly and audibly; maintains composure throughout; uses natural pacing | Speaks with confidence and expression; adjusts delivery for emphasis; engages listeners through vocal variety and presence |
| **Audience Engagement** | Does not acknowledge the audience; focuses on materials or the floor | Makes occasional eye contact; responds to direct questions with brief answers | Engages the audience through eye contact and direct address; responds to questions with substantive answers | Draws the audience in through storytelling, questions, or interactive elements; anticipates and addresses audience questions; invites dialogue |

**Trauma-Informed Scoring Note:** Delivery and audience engagement are assessed relative to the student's baseline, not against an absolute standard. A student who typically cannot speak above a whisper and who delivers a presentation in a soft but audible voice has demonstrated significant growth and may receive a 3 even if their volume is below what another student might produce. The rubric measures growth and effort, not personality or cultural communication norms.

## Garden Stewardship Rubric

This rubric is unique to the RootWork curriculum and assesses student engagement with the garden as a living laboratory and community resource.

| Dimension | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Care and Responsibility** | Participates in garden tasks when directed; may need reminders about gentle handling of plants and tools | Follows garden care routines with occasional reminders; handles plants and tools appropriately most of the time | Independently follows garden care routines; handles plants and tools with consistent care; notices when tasks need attention without being told | Takes initiative in garden care beyond assigned tasks; anticipates needs (watering, weeding, pest monitoring); models careful stewardship for peers |
| **Scientific Observation** | Notices obvious features of plants and garden (color, size) when prompted | Makes observations about plants and garden changes without prompting; observations are general ("The plant got bigger") | Makes detailed, specific observations using measurement and scientific vocabulary ("The tomato plant grew 3 inches this week and has 4 new flower buds") | Makes systematic observations over time; notices patterns and anomalies; connects observations to scientific concepts; asks investigable questions based on observations |
| **Collaboration** | Works alongside peers in the garden; may need support with sharing tools and space | Cooperates with peers during garden tasks; shares tools and space when reminded | Collaborates productively with peers; contributes to group garden tasks; communicates respectfully about shared work | Leads collaborative garden projects; mentors peers in garden skills; facilitates group decision-making about garden management |
| **Ecological Understanding** | Experiences the garden as a collection of individual plants | Recognizes some relationships in the garden (bees pollinate, worms help soil) | Understands the garden as an interconnected system; explains relationships between soil, water, sun, plants, insects, and decomposers | Applies ecological thinking to garden management decisions; considers sustainability; connects garden ecology to broader environmental systems |
| **Cultural Connection** | Participates in garden activities without connecting to personal or cultural food traditions | Shares personal food preferences related to garden crops | Connects garden activities to family food traditions; shares cultural knowledge about plants and growing | Actively integrates cultural gardening knowledge into the garden community; facilitates cross-cultural food exchanges; investigates food justice issues |

## TRACE Protocol Rubric

This rubric assesses the quality of student engagement with the TRACE protocol specifically. It is used during TRACE observations for reasoning move tracking (see Progress Monitoring).

| TRACE Step | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|------------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Think** ("What do you notice?") | Waits silently; does not offer observations without direct prompting | Offers one brief observation when prompted ("It's big") | Independently offers multiple relevant, detailed observations | Offers observations that generate questions and drive inquiry; notices features others miss |
| **Reason** ("What's your plan?") | Looks to teacher or peers for direction; does not propose a strategy | Proposes a strategy from a limited set when given options | Proposes a viable strategy independently and explains the reasoning behind the choice | Considers multiple strategies, evaluates trade-offs, and selects the most promising; adapts the plan as new information emerges |
| **Articulate** ("Walk me through your thinking") | Gives only a final answer; cannot yet narrate the thinking process | Describes some steps with prompting; uses everyday rather than academic language | Explains the full thinking process clearly, using academic vocabulary; explanation is followable by a peer | Explains thinking with precision, anticipates misunderstandings, connects to prior learning, and invites alternative perspectives |
| **Check** ("How do you know?") | Accepts first answer without verification; looks to teacher for confirmation | Checks work when reminded; uses the same method to recheck rather than a different strategy | Independently verifies using a different method; catches and corrects own errors before seeking teacher input | Develops systematic verification routines; explains why checking matters; identifies potential sources of error proactively |
| **Extend** ("What else could you do with this?") | Views the task as complete once the answer is found; does not look for connections | Makes a connection when teacher suggests one | Independently connects to other contexts, real-world applications, or prior learning | Poses new problems inspired by the original; identifies generalizable patterns; teaches the concept to a peer; investigates further questions |

## Growth Mindset Rubric

This rubric assesses the development of a growth mindset orientation, which is foundational to the Restore phase of the 5Rs. It is used during quarterly SEL assessments and portfolio reviews.

| Dimension | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Response to Challenge** | Avoids challenging tasks; may shut down, refuse, or become dysregulated when work is difficult | Attempts challenging tasks but gives up quickly when a strategy does not work; may express frustration verbally ("I can't do this") | Persists through challenging tasks; tries alternative strategies when stuck; accepts that difficulty is part of learning | Seeks out challenging tasks; views difficulty as an opportunity to grow; sustains engagement through extended productive struggle |
| **Response to Error** | Errors trigger shame, withdrawal, or dysregulation; may destroy work with errors; avoids tasks where errors are likely | Tolerates errors when supported by teacher; beginning to accept that errors happen but still experiences discomfort | Views errors as useful information; can analyze what went wrong without self-blame; willingly shares errors as learning opportunities | Actively seeks to learn from errors; uses error analysis as a thinking tool; celebrates errors as evidence of risk-taking; helps peers reframe their errors |
| **Self-Talk and Language** | Uses fixed-mindset language ("I'm stupid," "I can't do math," "I'll never get it") | Mixes fixed and growth language; uses growth language when prompted but defaults to fixed language under stress | Consistently uses growth language ("I haven't figured this out yet," "My brain is growing," "This is hard and I'm learning") | Models growth language for peers; reframes fixed statements from others; articulates the connection between effort, strategy, and growth |
| **Effort Attribution** | Attributes success to luck or external factors; attributes failure to fixed ability | Inconsistently attributes outcomes to effort; may attribute success to effort but failure to ability | Consistently attributes outcomes to effort and strategy use; understands that ability grows through practice | Articulates a sophisticated understanding of the relationship between effort, strategy, practice, and growth; applies this understanding across domains |

**Trauma-Informed Note:** Students who have experienced chronic adversity may exhibit fixed-mindset responses not because they lack a growth mindset but because their nervous system is in a protective mode. A student who shuts down at the first sign of difficulty may be experiencing a trauma response, not a character flaw. This rubric is used to track growth over time, never to label a student. Low scores trigger increased regulation support and relationship strengthening, not behavioral consequences.

## Error Analysis Rubric

This rubric assesses student capacity to engage productively with errors -- their own and others' -- which is a core skill in the Restore phase.

| Dimension | Beginning (1) | Approaching (2) | Meeting (3) | Extending (4) |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| **Error Identification** | Does not recognize errors in own work; may recognize errors in others' work when explicitly pointed out | Recognizes errors when teacher indicates the location ("Something went wrong in this step -- can you find it?") | Independently identifies errors in own and others' work by systematically checking each step | Proactively scans for errors before finishing; identifies both computational errors and conceptual misunderstandings; distinguishes between error types |
| **Error Diagnosis** | Cannot yet explain why the error occurred | Identifies what went wrong ("I added instead of multiplied") but not why | Explains both what went wrong and why ("I added instead of multiplied because I misread the word 'times' as 'more than'") | Traces the error to its root cause, including conceptual misunderstandings, procedural missteps, and reading comprehension issues; identifies patterns across errors |
| **Error Correction** | Starts over from scratch rather than correcting the error; or waits for teacher to provide the correction | Corrects the specific error when shown where it is, but may introduce new errors in the process | Corrects the error and re-verifies the entire solution to ensure the correction is complete and accurate | Corrects the error, explains the correction, and identifies what to watch for in future problems to prevent the same error |
| **Emotional Response to Error** | Errors cause significant emotional distress (shame, anger, withdrawal, work destruction) | Experiences discomfort but recovers with teacher support within a few minutes; does not destroy work | Manages emotional response independently; treats errors as neutral information; recovers quickly and returns to the task | Responds to errors with curiosity rather than distress; expresses interest in understanding the error; normalizes errors for peers |

## Grade-Band Adaptations for All Rubrics

All rubrics in this document are written at a general level. The following adaptations ensure that expectations are developmentally appropriate across grade bands.

### K-2 Adaptations

- **Student-facing version:** Rubrics are presented as picture scales (e.g., seed, sprout, growing plant, flowering plant) rather than numbered levels
- **Language:** Descriptors use the simplest possible language ("I try," "I keep trying," "I can do it," "I can help others")
- **Assessment method:** Teachers assess through observation and conversation, not by asking students to read and apply the rubric themselves
- **Frequency:** Rubrics are used for quarterly portfolio review and student-led conference preparation; daily assessment uses simpler checklists

**K-2 Student-Friendly Rubric Example (Growth Mindset -- Response to Challenge):**

| Seed (1) | Sprout (2) | Growing (3) | Blooming (4) |
|----------|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| When it's hard, I stop | When it's hard, I try but I might stop | When it's hard, I keep trying a new way | When it's hard, I keep going and I help others too |

### 3-5 Adaptations

- **Student-facing version:** Rubrics use numbered levels with student-friendly language and one garden-context example per level
- **Language:** Descriptors include academic vocabulary that has been explicitly taught
- **Assessment method:** Students begin to self-assess using the rubric with teacher guidance; teacher assessment remains primary
- **Frequency:** Rubrics are used for performance task scoring, portfolio review, and student self-assessment (monthly)

### 6-8 Adaptations

- **Student-facing version:** Full rubric language is accessible to students; examples are drawn from current grade-band work
- **Language:** Descriptors use full academic vocabulary; students are expected to understand and apply the rubric independently
- **Assessment method:** Students self-assess and provide peer feedback using rubrics; teacher assessment and student self-assessment are compared and discussed
- **Frequency:** Rubrics are used for all performance tasks, portfolio review, peer feedback, and student self-assessment (ongoing)

### 9-12 Adaptations

- **Student-facing version:** Students use the full teacher version of rubrics; they also participate in rubric co-creation for capstone projects
- **Language:** Descriptors include discipline-specific professional language
- **Assessment method:** Students self-assess, peer-assess, and conference with teachers to calibrate understanding; students articulate their own quality criteria
- **Frequency:** Rubrics are used for all formal assessments; students apply rubric thinking to evaluate sources, arguments, and community projects beyond the classroom

## Using Rubrics Effectively in Instruction

### Before the Task

- Share the rubric with students before they begin the task, not after
- Review what each level looks like using exemplars from previous student work (with permission) or teacher-created models
- Invite students to identify which level they are currently at and set a goal for the task
- Explicitly connect rubric language to regulation: "If you notice yourself getting frustrated during this task, that's a sign you're working in the hard part -- and that's where growth happens"

### During the Task

- Use rubric language in real-time feedback: "I notice you're at 'Meeting' for strategy selection -- you chose a strategy and explained why. Would you like to try a second strategy to see if you can reach 'Extending'?"
- Avoid using rubric levels as threats or compliance tools ("If you don't try harder, you'll stay at a 1")
- Offer regulation support when students are working at the edge of their current level

### After the Task

- Students self-assess first, then teacher assesses, then they compare and discuss
- Focus the conversation on the gap between current performance and the next level, not between current performance and the top level
- Always ask: "What's one thing you could try next time to move toward the next level?"
- Record rubric data in the RootGuide platform for progress monitoring and portfolio documentation
