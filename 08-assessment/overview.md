# Assessment Philosophy Overview

## Introduction

Assessment in the RootWork curriculum is rooted in a single conviction: the purpose of assessment is to help children grow, not to sort them into categories. In a trauma-informed, garden-based learning environment, assessment must be as carefully tended as the garden itself -- designed to reveal what students know and can do, responsive to the full range of human expression, and always oriented toward the next step in learning rather than a final judgment. Just as a gardener reads the soil, monitors moisture, and observes leaf color to understand what a plant needs, RootWork educators use assessment to understand each student's current state and determine the conditions that will support continued growth.

This philosophy emerges from the intersection of three imperatives. First, trauma-informed practice demands that assessment never re-traumatize. Students who have experienced adversity are often hypervigilant to evaluation, having learned that being "tested" leads to shame, punishment, or loss. Second, rigorous academic standards require that we know with precision what students understand and where they need support. Third, garden-based pedagogy reminds us that growth is seasonal, nonlinear, and deeply individual -- yet always moving toward fruition when conditions are right.

## Guiding Principles

### 1. Assessment Serves Learning, Not Sorting

Every assessment in the RootWork system exists to answer one question: "What does this student need next?" Assessments are never used to rank students against one another, to justify exclusion from enrichment activities, or to determine a student's fixed ability level. When assessment data reveals a gap, the response is always an instructional adjustment -- not a label.

This principle has practical implications. Teachers do not post scores publicly. Assessments are returned with narrative feedback rather than letter grades. Data conversations among educators focus on instructional response, not student deficit.

### 2. Multiple Modes of Expression

Students demonstrate understanding in many ways: through language, gesture, drawing, building, movement, and demonstration. A student who cannot yet write a paragraph about photosynthesis may be able to diagram the process, explain it verbally to a peer, or demonstrate it using garden materials. The RootWork assessment system honors all of these expressions as valid evidence of learning.

| Mode of Expression | Example Evidence | Best For |
|-------------------|-----------------|----------|
| Written | Journal entries, lab reports, essays | Students with strong literacy skills |
| Oral | TRACE protocol responses, presentations, interviews | Verbal processors; students with writing challenges |
| Visual | Diagrams, garden maps, data displays, photographs | Spatial thinkers; English language learners |
| Kinesthetic | Garden demonstrations, model building, experiments | Hands-on learners; students who regulate through movement |
| Digital | Multimedia projects, data dashboards, video explanations | Technology-oriented learners; older grade bands |
| Collaborative | Group projects, peer teaching, community presentations | Students who think best in social contexts |

### 3. Growth Over Time

A single data point is a photograph; a collection of data points across time is a story. The RootWork assessment system prioritizes longitudinal evidence of growth over isolated performance snapshots. Portfolios, growth journals, and repeated measures across a school year reveal trajectories that no single test can capture. A student who moves from "Beginning" to "Approaching" has demonstrated meaningful growth that deserves recognition, even if they have not yet reached "Meeting."

This principle directly addresses the reality that trauma recovery is nonlinear. A student may demonstrate strong understanding one week and regress the next following a triggering event. Longitudinal assessment allows educators to see past temporary setbacks and recognize the larger growth arc.

### 4. Whole Child Assessment

Academic performance is inseparable from social-emotional well-being, regulation capacity, and sense of belonging. The RootWork assessment system tracks all four domains simultaneously, recognizing that a student who is dysregulated cannot demonstrate their full academic capacity, and a student who feels unsafe will not take the intellectual risks that deep learning requires.

| Domain | What We Assess | Primary Tools |
|--------|---------------|---------------|
| Academic Knowledge | Content understanding across subjects | Curriculum-based measures, performance tasks, TRACE observations |
| Reasoning and Thinking | Quality of cognitive engagement | TRACE rubric, reasoning move tracking, error analysis protocols |
| Social-Emotional Competency | Self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, responsible decision-making | SEL observation checklists, student self-assessments, family surveys |
| Regulation Capacity | Ability to identify, monitor, and manage arousal states | Daily regulation check-in data, co-regulation needs tracking, autonomy progression |

### 5. Trauma-Sensitive Assessment Practices

Assessment itself can be a trigger for students who have experienced adversity. The following practices ensure that assessment supports safety rather than undermining it:

- **Predictability:** Students always know when assessment is happening and what form it will take. There are no surprise tests.
- **Choice:** Where possible, students choose how to demonstrate understanding (mode, timing, topic).
- **Privacy:** Assessment results are shared only with the student and their caregivers, never displayed publicly.
- **Regulation Access:** Students may use regulation tools (fidgets, movement, breathing exercises) during any assessment.
- **Retake Opportunity:** Any assessment can be retaken after additional learning. The most recent evidence of understanding is what counts.
- **Strengths Orientation:** Feedback always begins with what the student demonstrated successfully before addressing areas for growth.
- **Relationship Preservation:** Assessment never damages the student-teacher relationship. A low score is an instructional signal, not a character judgment.

### 6. Culturally Responsive Assessment

Assessment tasks, contexts, and language must be accessible to students from all cultural backgrounds. The garden provides a powerful equalizing context -- growing food is a universal human activity -- but educators must still attend to the ways that assessment can privilege certain cultural norms:

- Tasks use garden and food contexts familiar across cultures rather than culturally narrow scenarios
- Academic language is explicitly taught before it is assessed
- Family and community knowledge is recognized as valid evidence of learning
- Assessment materials are available in students' home languages when possible
- Performance tasks allow students to draw on cultural gardening traditions and food practices

## Assessment Types

The RootWork curriculum uses three types of assessment, each serving a distinct purpose within the instructional cycle.

### Diagnostic Assessment

Diagnostic assessment answers the question: "Where is each student right now?" It occurs at the beginning of a unit, a school year, or when a new student enters the program. Diagnostic data informs initial instructional planning, grouping decisions, and differentiation strategies.

**Diagnostic Tools in the RootWork System:**

| Tool | Frequency | Domains Assessed | Data Generated |
|------|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| Garden Knowledge Survey | Start of year | Prior gardening experience, cultural food practices, science vocabulary | Qualitative profile of background knowledge |
| Academic Screener | Start of year, mid-year | Grade-level literacy and numeracy | Performance level relative to standards |
| Regulation Baseline | First two weeks | Baseline arousal patterns, co-regulation needs, effective strategies | Individualized regulation profile |
| SEL Self-Assessment | Start of year | Self-perceived social-emotional strengths and challenges | Student voice data for goal setting |
| Family Interview | Start of year | Home context, cultural assets, family priorities, child strengths | Relationship-building data; cultural asset inventory |

### Formative Assessment

Formative assessment answers the question: "How is learning progressing, and what should I adjust?" It occurs continuously -- embedded in every lesson, every TRACE interaction, every garden session. Formative assessment is the heartbeat of the RootWork instructional system.

Formative assessment is never graded. It is information for the teacher and the student, not a judgment of worth. The TRACE protocol itself is a formative assessment engine: every time a teacher asks "What do you notice?" or "How do you know?", they are gathering evidence of student thinking.

**Key Formative Assessment Practices:**
- TRACE protocol observation and documentation
- Growth journal review (weekly)
- Exit tickets with drawing and writing options
- Fist-to-five regulation and understanding checks
- Observation checklists during garden work
- Whiteboard responses during direct instruction
- Partner sharing with teacher listen-ins

### Summative Assessment

Summative assessment answers the question: "What has this student learned over this unit or period?" It occurs at the end of instructional units, at quarterly reporting periods, and at the end of the school year. In the RootWork system, summative assessment takes the form of performance tasks and portfolio reviews rather than traditional tests.

**Summative Assessment Formats:**

| Format | Description | Frequency |
|--------|------------|-----------|
| Performance Task | Authentic garden-based task requiring application of knowledge and skills | End of each unit (approximately monthly) |
| Portfolio Review | Curated collection of student work demonstrating growth across domains | Quarterly |
| Student-Led Conference | Student presents portfolio to family, narrating their growth and goals | Twice per year (fall, spring) |
| Garden Exhibition | Students present a garden project to an authentic audience | Once per year (spring) |
| Capstone Project (9-12 only) | Extended research or service project connecting garden learning to community impact | Annual |

## Standards-Based Reporting System

The RootWork curriculum uses a four-level proficiency scale for all standards-based reporting. This scale replaces traditional letter grades with descriptive levels that communicate both current performance and growth trajectory.

### Proficiency Levels

| Level | Label | Description | What It Means for Instruction |
|-------|-------|-------------|-------------------------------|
| 4 | **Extending** | Student demonstrates understanding beyond grade-level expectations; applies knowledge in novel contexts; teaches or mentors others | Provide enrichment, leadership opportunities, and cross-grade mentoring roles |
| 3 | **Meeting** | Student demonstrates solid understanding of grade-level expectations; applies knowledge in familiar and some unfamiliar contexts | Continue current instructional approach; offer extension opportunities |
| 2 | **Approaching** | Student demonstrates partial understanding of grade-level expectations; applies knowledge with support in familiar contexts | Provide targeted reteaching, additional practice, and scaffolded tasks |
| 1 | **Beginning** | Student demonstrates emerging understanding; requires substantial support to engage with grade-level content | Provide intensive intervention, simplified entry points, and co-regulation support |

### Proficiency Level Clarifications

**Extending (4) does not mean "perfect."** It means the student has moved beyond grade-level expectations to apply learning in creative, independent, or mentoring contexts. A student does not need to earn 100% to receive a 4 -- they need to demonstrate depth and transfer.

**Beginning (1) is not a failure.** It is a starting point. Every student begins somewhere, and "Beginning" communicates that the student is on the path, with clear instructional supports identified. In a trauma-informed context, the language of "Beginning" is chosen deliberately to convey a forward trajectory.

**Levels describe current status, not permanent identity.** A student may be "Meeting" in one standard and "Approaching" in another within the same subject. Levels are reassessed regularly and always represent the most recent evidence.

### Reporting Schedule

| Report | Timing | Content | Audience |
|--------|--------|---------|----------|
| Progress Update | Every 4-5 weeks | Narrative summary of growth, current proficiency levels, next steps | Families, student |
| Quarterly Report Card | End of each quarter | Proficiency levels for all assessed standards, SEL summary, regulation growth narrative | Families, student, cumulative file |
| Student-Led Conference Report | Fall and Spring | Student-selected portfolio evidence, student-narrated growth story, collaborative goal setting | Family, student, teacher |
| Annual Summary | End of year | Year-long growth trajectory, transition recommendations, summer learning suggestions | Families, receiving teacher, cumulative file |

## Grading and Reporting Integration

### Translating Proficiency to Traditional Requirements

Some districts require traditional letter grades for transcripts or report cards. When this is the case, the RootWork system provides the following translation framework:

| Proficiency Level | Grade Equivalent | Percentage Equivalent |
|-------------------|------------------|-----------------------|
| Extending (4) | A | 90-100% |
| Meeting (3) | B | 80-89% |
| Approaching (2) | C | 70-79% |
| Beginning (1) | D or I (Incomplete) | Below 70% |

**Important:** When translation is required, the narrative progress report still accompanies the grade. The letter grade is never sent home without context.

### What Is NOT Assessed for Grades

The following are never factored into proficiency-level determinations:

- **Compliance behaviors:** Homework completion, on-time arrival, uniform compliance, and similar behavioral expectations are reported separately, never as part of academic proficiency
- **Regulation challenges:** A student who needs frequent regulation breaks is not penalized academically for time away from instruction
- **Participation quantity:** The quality of thinking matters, not the frequency of hand-raising
- **Speed of learning:** A student who needs three attempts to reach "Meeting" earns the same level as a student who reached it on the first attempt
- **Handwriting or neatness:** Unless the standard specifically addresses presentation, the content of student thinking is what is assessed

### Integration with IEP and 504 Plans

For students with Individualized Education Programs or 504 Plans, assessment and reporting are aligned with the accommodations and modifications specified in those documents:

- IEP goals are mapped to RootWork proficiency standards where applicable
- Modified standards are clearly identified on report cards
- Accommodations (extended time, alternative format, reduced quantity) are provided for all assessments
- Progress toward IEP goals is reported on the same schedule as general education progress reports
- Student-led conferences are adapted to include IEP goal review when appropriate

## Alignment with the 5Rs Framework

Assessment is not a separate phase of the RootWork experience -- it is woven through every phase of the 5Rs:

| 5Rs Phase | Assessment Function | Example |
|-----------|--------------------|---------|
| **Root** | Assess regulation state and readiness to learn | Morning check-in using the mood meter; observation of body language during grounding activity |
| **Regulate** | Monitor regulation strategy effectiveness | Track which strategies each student uses; note co-regulation needs; document autonomy growth |
| **Reflect** | Assess academic understanding through TRACE | Observe reasoning moves; document thinking quality; record engagement mode participation |
| **Restore** | Assess response to error and capacity for productive struggle | Observe how students respond to mistakes; document growth in error tolerance; track help-seeking patterns |
| **Reconnect** | Assess transfer, application, and community connection | Evaluate performance tasks; review portfolio selections; observe community exhibition presentations |

## Conclusion

The RootWork assessment philosophy asks educators to hold a creative tension: to be rigorous in understanding what students know while being gentle in how that understanding is gathered and communicated. The garden teaches us that growth cannot be forced -- but it can be observed, supported, and celebrated. Assessment in this curriculum is the practice of careful observation in service of each student's unfolding potential.
