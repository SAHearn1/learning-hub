# Foreword

## The Quiet Crisis in Our Classrooms

There is a crisis unfolding in American classrooms, and it is not the one that dominates headlines about test scores, teacher shortages, or school safety. It is quieter than that, more pervasive, and far more consequential. It is the crisis of unaddressed childhood trauma -- and it is reshaping the landscape of education in ways that most schools are neither equipped to recognize nor prepared to address.

The numbers are staggering. According to the landmark Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) study initiated by Dr. Vincent Felitti and Dr. Robert Anda at Kaiser Permanente and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, more than 60 percent of adults in the United States report having experienced at least one adverse childhood experience before the age of eighteen. Nearly one in six adults reports four or more ACEs -- a threshold associated with dramatically increased risk for chronic disease, mental illness, substance use disorders, and early death. These are not rare events happening to a small, unfortunate subset of the population. They are the defining experiences of a majority of Americans.

For children, the picture is equally sobering. Data from the National Survey of Children's Health indicates that nearly half of all children in the United States have experienced at least one ACE. In communities marked by concentrated poverty, systemic racism, housing instability, and limited access to healthcare, the prevalence is significantly higher. Children in under-resourced communities face disproportionate exposure to adverse experiences, not because of any deficit in their families or cultures, but because of the structural inequities that concentrate harm along predictable lines of race, class, and geography.

## What Trauma Does to Learning

The implications for education are profound. When a child experiences chronic, unpredictable stress -- what developmental scientists call toxic stress -- the architecture of the developing brain is altered in measurable ways. The stress response system, designed for short-term survival, becomes chronically activated. Cortisol floods neural pathways meant for learning, memory consolidation, and executive function. The prefrontal cortex, responsible for planning, impulse control, working memory, and flexible thinking -- the very capacities that schools demand most -- is functionally impaired.

This is not a behavioral problem. It is a neurobiological reality.

Children operating in a state of chronic hyperarousal or dissociative shutdown are not choosing to be inattentive, defiant, or withdrawn. Their nervous systems are doing exactly what they were designed to do: scanning for threat, conserving energy, and prioritizing survival over learning. The child who cannot sit still, who explodes at a seemingly minor provocation, who stares blankly during instruction, who refuses to attempt a task for fear of failure -- these are not signs of willful disobedience. They are the predictable expressions of a nervous system shaped by adversity.

And yet, the dominant response in too many schools remains punitive. Zero-tolerance policies, behavior charts that publicly rank children's compliance, exclusionary discipline practices, the removal of recess as punishment, rigid seating arrangements, and reward-and-consequence systems that assume all children enter the classroom with the same capacity for self-regulation. These approaches do not merely fail to help trauma-impacted children. They actively re-traumatize them -- replicating the very dynamics of unpredictability, powerlessness, and shame that characterize adverse childhood experiences.

## The Gap Between Knowledge and Practice

The research community has known for decades that punitive approaches to behavior management are ineffective for children impacted by trauma. The neuroscience is clear. The developmental psychology is clear. The epidemiology is clear. And yet, a vast gap persists between what the evidence tells us and how schools actually operate.

This gap is not primarily a knowledge gap. Most educators, when presented with the research on ACEs and toxic stress, respond with immediate recognition. They see their students in the data. They understand, intuitively if not technically, that something deeper is driving the behaviors that consume their classroom management energy. The gap is an implementation gap -- a shortage of practical, structured, evidence-informed tools that translate trauma-informed principles into daily classroom practice.

Educators are told to "be trauma-informed" but given little guidance on what that looks like during a forty-five-minute math lesson with twenty-eight students, three of whom are in active crisis. They are encouraged to "build relationships" but offered no framework for how relationship-building integrates with academic instruction rather than competing with it. They are asked to "regulate before you educate" but provided no curriculum that makes regulation an embedded, daily practice rather than an emergency intervention.

## Bridging the Gap: Why This Curriculum Exists

*From Garden to Growth* was written to close that gap.

This curriculum represents the convergence of two powerful bodies of evidence: the science of trauma-informed education and the research on therapeutic horticulture. It is built on the premise that healing-centered education requires more than good intentions and professional development workshops. It requires a structured, developmentally sequenced, standards-aligned curriculum that weaves trauma-informed practices into the fabric of daily instruction -- and that gives children a tangible, sensory-rich, purpose-driven context in which to practice the skills of self-regulation, reflection, and connection.

The garden provides that context.

Nature-based learning is not a trend or a luxury. A growing body of research demonstrates that exposure to natural environments reduces cortisol levels, improves attention and executive function, strengthens social bonds, and provides the kind of multisensory engagement that supports neural integration. Therapeutic horticulture -- the intentional use of gardening activities to achieve specific therapeutic or rehabilitative goals -- has been shown to reduce symptoms of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress in both children and adults. When garden-based learning is combined with trauma-informed pedagogy, the result is an approach that addresses the whole child: body, mind, and relational self.

The 5Rs Framework -- Root, Regulate, Reflect, Restore, Reconnect -- provides the pedagogical structure that makes this integration coherent and replicable. Each phase of the framework corresponds to a developmental need, a theoretical foundation, and a set of garden-based practices that bring abstract concepts to life through embodied experience. The framework is not an add-on to existing instruction. It is a lens through which any lesson, any subject area, any moment of the school day can be understood and approached with intention.

## A Call to Courage

This curriculum asks something of every educator who picks it up: the courage to believe that the children in your care are not broken, that the systems meant to serve them can be redesigned, and that a garden -- with all its imperfection, seasonality, and quiet persistence -- might just be the most radical classroom you have ever entered.

The evidence is clear. The need is urgent. The children are waiting.

Let us begin.
