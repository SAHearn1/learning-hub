# Trauma-Informed Pedagogy

## From Awareness to Practice

Trauma-informed education has evolved significantly over the past two decades. Early efforts focused on awareness -- helping educators understand what ACEs are and how they affect children. While awareness is necessary, it is insufficient. Knowing that a student has experienced trauma does not, by itself, change instructional practice.

The FG2G curriculum moves beyond awareness to **operationalize** trauma-informed principles at the level of daily instruction. This document describes the six core principles of trauma-informed pedagogy and how each is embedded in the 5Rs Framework.

## The Six Core Principles

The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA, 2014) identifies six principles for a trauma-informed approach. The FG2G curriculum translates each into specific instructional practices:

### 1. Safety

**Principle**: Physical and psychological safety is the foundation. The environment must feel safe, and interactions must promote a sense of safety.

**FG2G Implementation**:

| Component | How Safety Is Created |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **Root phase** | Every session begins with a grounding check-in that establishes predictability and signals relational safety |
| **Living Learning Lab design** | Zones are designed for sensory comfort, clear boundaries, and visual predictability |
| **Regulation pods** | Dedicated spaces where students can retreat without judgment or consequence |
| **Educator role** | "Non-anxious presence" -- calm, warm body language; avoiding shame-inducing language |
| **Transition rituals** | Consistent routines that reduce the anxiety of uncertainty |

**Educator Language Models**:
- Instead of: "Why are you late?" --> "Welcome. We're glad you're here."
- Instead of: "What's wrong with you today?" --> "How are you feeling? What do you need?"
- Instead of: "Stop that behavior right now." --> "I can see you're having a hard time. Let's figure this out together."

### 2. Trustworthiness and Transparency

**Principle**: Operations and decisions are conducted with transparency. The goal is to build and maintain trust.

**FG2G Implementation**:
- **Predictable session structure**: Students always know what to expect (Root --> Regulate --> Reflect --> Restore --> Reconnect).
- **Visible schedules**: The day's activities are posted at the Living Learning Lab entrance.
- **Transparent transitions**: Phase transitions are communicated explicitly: "It looks like you might need some regulation time before we continue. Want to visit the sensory garden?"
- **Honest feedback**: Errors are discussed openly using the TRACE protocol, not hidden or minimized.
- **Consistent adults**: Students work with the same educators whenever possible, building relational continuity.

### 3. Peer Support

**Principle**: Peer support and mutual self-help are key vehicles for establishing safety and hope.

**FG2G Implementation**:
- **Grounding partnerships** (grades 3+): Paired students support each other during Root phase check-ins.
- **Collaborative garden work**: Garden tasks are structured for cooperation, not competition.
- **Peer regulation support** (grades 6+): Trained student peers can offer co-regulation strategies.
- **Error normalization**: When one student's error is analyzed in the Restore phase, peers learn that errors are universal, not personal.
- **Community harvest**: The Reconnect phase includes shared celebrations of growth that strengthen group bonds.

### 4. Collaboration and Mutuality

**Principle**: There is recognition that healing happens in relationships and in the meaningful sharing of power.

**FG2G Implementation**:
- **Student voice in garden design**: Students participate in decisions about what to plant, how to arrange beds, and how to use harvest.
- **Choice within structure**: Every phase offers choices -- which grounding exercise, which garden task, which problem to solve, how to present learning.
- **Co-created expectations**: Garden norms are developed collaboratively with students, not imposed unilaterally.
- **Educator as learning partner**: In the Reflect phase, the educator models thinking alongside the student rather than delivering answers.
- **TRACE protocol**: Structured to be a dialogue, not an interrogation.

### 5. Empowerment, Voice, and Choice

**Principle**: Individuals' strengths are recognized, built upon, and validated. Organizations recognize that the experience of having control taken away is central to trauma; therefore, restoring power is central to recovery.

**FG2G Implementation**:
- **Self-assessment**: Students learn to assess their own regulation levels rather than being told how they feel.
- **Growth documentation**: Students track their own progress through portfolios, garden journals, and growth charts.
- **Reasoning move deployment**: Students are taught to identify and choose which reasoning move to apply (DECOMPOSE, COMPARE, QUESTION, VERIFY, GENERALIZE, TRANSFORM, CONNECT).
- **Student-directed learning** (grades 9-12): Older students direct their own phase transitions and select learning objectives.
- **Garden stewardship**: Students are responsible for living things, providing authentic agency and visible impact.

### 6. Cultural, Historical, and Gender Issues

**Principle**: The organization moves past cultural stereotypes and biases, offers culturally responsive services, and addresses historical trauma.

**FG2G Implementation**:
- **Culturally relevant crops**: Garden plantings reflect the cultural heritage and foodways of the student population.
- **Multicultural garden traditions**: Lessons incorporate agricultural practices from diverse cultural traditions (Three Sisters planting, terrace farming, companion planting from multiple traditions).
- **Multilingual signage**: Plant labels and garden instructions available in students' home languages.
- **Community knowledge**: Family and community members are invited as garden experts, honoring non-academic forms of knowledge.
- **Food justice connections**: The Reconnect phase connects garden learning to issues of food access, environmental justice, and community empowerment.

## Trauma-Informed vs. Trauma-Specific

An important distinction in the FG2G approach:

| Approach | Definition | FG2G Role |
|----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Trauma-informed** | Universal practices that create safety and reduce re-traumatization for all students | The 5Rs Framework structure, garden design, educator practices |
| **Trauma-specific** | Targeted clinical interventions for students with identified trauma | Referral to school counselors, mental health professionals |

The FG2G curriculum is **trauma-informed**, not trauma-specific. It creates the conditions that support all students -- including those with trauma histories -- without requiring educators to diagnose or treat trauma. Students who need trauma-specific interventions should be referred to qualified professionals; the curriculum's role is to ensure that the educational environment does not add to the harm.

## The Educator's Role: Non-Anxious Presence

The concept of the "non-anxious presence" (drawn from Edwin Friedman's work on leadership) is central to trauma-informed pedagogy in the FG2G curriculum:

### What It Means
- Remaining calm and warm regardless of student behavior
- Not absorbing or mirroring the student's dysregulation
- Maintaining emotional boundaries while staying relationally connected
- Communicating through body language that the situation is manageable

### What It Requires
- **Self-regulation**: Educators must manage their own stress responses. An educator in fight-or-flight cannot co-regulate a student.
- **Training**: Understanding dysregulation as a nervous system response, not a personal affront.
- **Support systems**: Access to supervision, peer consultation, and their own wellness practices.
- **Organizational support**: Manageable caseloads, planning time, and administrative backing.

### What It Is Not
- It is not emotional detachment or indifference.
- It is not permissiveness or absence of expectations.
- It is not ignoring problematic behavior.
- It is not suppressing genuine emotions.

It is the capacity to hold space for a student's dysregulation without becoming dysregulated yourself -- the adult equivalent of the garden's steady soil, providing the stable conditions from which growth can emerge.

## Secondary Traumatic Stress

Educators working with trauma-affected students are at risk for secondary traumatic stress (STS) -- the emotional toll of working closely with individuals who have experienced trauma. The FG2G curriculum addresses this through:

- **Professional development modules** on recognizing and managing STS
- **Peer support structures** (professional learning communities, peer observation partnerships)
- **Administrative commitment** to manageable workloads and adequate planning time
- **The garden itself**: Garden-based instruction provides the same parasympathetic benefits to educators that it provides to students
- **Explicit permission** to use regulation strategies themselves -- modeling these practices normalizes them for students
