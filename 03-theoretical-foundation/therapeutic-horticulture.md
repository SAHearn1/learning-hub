# Therapeutic Horticulture

## Definitions and Distinctions

Therapeutic horticulture exists on a continuum of garden-based interventions:

| Practice | Definition | Practitioner | Setting |
|----------|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Horticultural Therapy** | Clinical intervention using garden activities to meet specific therapeutic goals | Registered Horticultural Therapist (HTR) | Clinical, rehabilitation |
| **Therapeutic Horticulture** | Garden activities designed to promote well-being without formal clinical goals | Trained facilitator or educator | Educational, community |
| **Social and Therapeutic Horticulture** | Garden-based programs focused on social inclusion and community building | Program coordinators | Community gardens, schools |
| **Garden-Based Education** | Using garden environments to teach academic content | Trained educator | Schools |

The FG2G curriculum operates primarily in the **therapeutic horticulture** and **garden-based education** space, using garden activities to promote emotional well-being and academic learning simultaneously. It does not claim to be horticultural therapy, which requires clinical credentialing, but it is informed by horticultural therapy research and principles.

## The Evidence Base

### Physiological Benefits

Research demonstrates measurable physiological changes associated with garden-based activities:

**Cortisol Reduction**
- Van Den Berg and Custers (2011) demonstrated that 30 minutes of gardening reduced salivary cortisol levels significantly more than 30 minutes of indoor reading in a controlled experiment.
- Detweiler et al. (2012) found that therapeutic garden use in a veterans' facility was associated with reduced cortisol levels and decreased use of PRN (as-needed) psychiatric medications.

**Cardiovascular Benefits**
- Park et al. (2009) measured blood pressure and heart rate during garden activities and found reductions consistent with parasympathetic activation.
- Regular garden participation is associated with lower resting heart rate and improved heart rate variability (HRV), a marker of autonomic nervous system flexibility.

**Immune Function**
- Li et al. (2010) documented increased natural killer (NK) cell activity following time spent in natural environments -- a finding with implications for students whose chronic stress has suppressed immune function.

### Psychological Benefits

**Mood and Affect**
- Gonzalez et al. (2010) conducted a randomized controlled trial of therapeutic horticulture for clinical depression, finding significant improvements in depression severity scores after 12 weeks of garden participation.
- Clatworthy, Hinds, and Camic (2013) reviewed the literature on gardening and mental health, finding consistent associations between garden activities and improved mood, reduced anxiety, and enhanced self-esteem.

**Attention and Cognition**
- Kaplan's Attention Restoration Theory (1995) provides a theoretical framework for understanding why natural environments (including gardens) improve cognitive function. Natural settings provide "soft fascination" -- engaging attention without taxing directed attention, allowing the executive function system to recover.
- Faber Taylor and Kuo (2009) demonstrated that exposure to green outdoor environments reduced symptoms of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in children -- directly relevant to trauma-affected students who often present with ADHD-like symptoms.

**Self-Efficacy and Agency**
- Sempik, Hine, and Wilcox (2010) reported that participants in therapeutic horticulture programs consistently described increased feelings of accomplishment, self-worth, and control -- precisely the outcomes needed for students whose trauma has undermined their sense of agency.
- The tangible, visible nature of garden work provides a feedback loop that builds self-efficacy: plant a seed, tend it, watch it grow. The causal relationship between effort and outcome is concrete and undeniable.

### Educational Benefits

**Academic Achievement**
- Williams and Dixon (2013) conducted a meta-analysis of garden-based education programs and found positive effects on science achievement, with garden-based students consistently outperforming control groups on science assessments.
- Blair (2009) reviewed garden-based education programs and found improvements in science knowledge, nutritional knowledge, and positive attitudes toward vegetables and fruits.
- Klemmer, Waliczek, and Zajicek (2005) found that students in garden-based science programs scored significantly higher on science achievement tests than students in traditional instruction.

**Engagement and Attendance**
- Garden-based programs are consistently associated with improved attendance and reduced behavioral incidents (Ozer, 2007).
- Students describe garden-based learning as more engaging, relevant, and enjoyable than traditional classroom instruction (Block et al., 2012).

**Social Development**
- Collaborative garden activities promote prosocial behavior, cooperation, and conflict resolution skills (Robinson & Zajicek, 2005).
- Students who participate in garden programs demonstrate improved social skills and peer relationships (Waliczek, Logan, & Zajicek, 2003).

## Mechanisms of Action

Why does garden-based activity produce these effects? Several mechanisms have been proposed:

### 1. Biophilia and Evolutionary Psychology

E.O. Wilson's (1984) biophilia hypothesis suggests that humans have an innate affinity for living systems and natural environments -- a preference shaped by millions of years of evolution in natural settings. Gardens activate this deep-seated connection, producing calming effects that are not dependent on conscious processing.

### 2. Attention Restoration (Kaplan, 1995)

Garden environments provide four qualities that promote cognitive recovery:
- **Being away**: Physical and psychological distance from stressful demands
- **Fascination**: Effortless, involuntary attention (watching a ladybug, noticing a new sprout)
- **Extent**: A sense of a larger world beyond immediate concerns
- **Compatibility**: A match between the environment's demands and the individual's inclinations

### 3. Stress Recovery (Ulrich, 1991)

Ulrich's psychoevolutionary theory of stress recovery proposes that natural environments trigger rapid, automatic recovery from stress through visual processing. Natural scenes (including gardens) produce measurable reductions in sympathetic nervous system activation within minutes.

### 4. Embodied Cognition

Garden activities engage the body in learning -- digging, planting, measuring, carrying, watering. Research in embodied cognition demonstrates that physical engagement enhances cognitive processing, memory formation, and conceptual understanding (Barsalou, 2008). For trauma-affected students, whose bodies carry the imprint of their experiences, physically grounded learning can be particularly powerful.

### 5. Microbial Exposure (Mycobacterium vaccae)

Emerging research suggests that contact with soil microbes, particularly *Mycobacterium vaccae*, may have antidepressant and anxiolytic effects by stimulating serotonin production in the brain (Lowry et al., 2007). While this research is still developing, it provides a biological mechanism for the mood-enhancing effects of direct soil contact.

## Application to the FG2G Curriculum

The therapeutic horticulture evidence base informs specific curriculum design decisions:

### Living Learning Lab as Therapeutic Environment

The garden is not merely a teaching venue -- it is a **therapeutic intervention environment**. Every design element serves both a pedagogical and a therapeutic purpose:

| Garden Element | Pedagogical Purpose | Therapeutic Purpose |
|---------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Raised beds** | Measurement, observation, data collection | Structured, boundaried space that creates predictability |
| **Sensory garden** | Science (plant biology, sensory systems) | Parasympathetic activation through aromatic and tactile stimulation |
| **Regulation pods** | Self-assessment, metacognition | Safe retreat space for co-regulation and self-regulation |
| **Composting station** | Ecology, decomposition, nutrient cycles | Metaphor for the Restore phase -- waste becomes nourishment |
| **Harvest area** | Measurement, nutrition, cooking | Reconnect phase -- tangible evidence of growth; community sharing |

### Seasonal Alignment

The curriculum's seasonal planning aligns garden activities with the school year and the natural growth cycle, providing a living metaphor for student development:

- **Fall**: Soil preparation, planting (Root phase emphasis -- establishing foundations)
- **Winter**: Indoor germination, planning, design (Reflect phase emphasis -- intellectual cultivation)
- **Spring**: Active growing, tending, problem-solving (Full 5Rs cycle in action)
- **Summer**: Harvest, celebration, planning ahead (Reconnect phase emphasis -- integration and legacy)

### Dosage and Frequency

Research suggests that therapeutic horticulture benefits are dose-dependent. The FG2G curriculum recommends:

- **Minimum**: Two garden sessions per week, 45-60 minutes each
- **Optimal**: Daily garden integration, even if brief (15-20 minutes for grounding activities)
- **Seasonal variation**: Increased outdoor time during growing season; indoor garden activities (seed starting, food preparation, garden design) during off-season
