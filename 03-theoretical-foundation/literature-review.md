# Literature Review

## Overview

This literature review synthesizes research across the four domains that inform the FG2G curriculum: adverse childhood experiences and neurodevelopment, trauma-informed educational practice, therapeutic horticulture and garden-based education, and cognitive science applied to instruction. The review focuses on studies with direct implications for curriculum design and classroom practice.

## Domain 1: Adverse Childhood Experiences and Neurodevelopment

### Foundational Studies

**Felitti, V. J., Anda, R. F., et al. (1998).** The original ACE study established the dose-response relationship between childhood adversity and long-term health outcomes. The study's significance for education lies in demonstrating that adverse experiences are not isolated events -- they accumulate, interact, and reshape the developing brain in ways that directly impair learning capacity.

**Shonkoff, J. P., et al. (2012).** The Harvard Center on the Developing Child distinguished between three types of stress responses in children: positive stress (brief, manageable), tolerable stress (more severe but buffered by supportive relationships), and toxic stress (prolonged activation without adequate adult support). Toxic stress -- the type associated with ACEs -- disrupts brain architecture, the stress response system, and gene expression. This framework directly informs the FG2G curriculum's emphasis on adult co-regulation as a buffer against toxic stress.

**National Scientific Council on the Developing Child (2014).** This working paper documented how excessive stress disrupts brain architecture, with particular emphasis on the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala. The council's concept of "serve and return" interactions -- reciprocal exchanges between children and caregivers that build neural connections -- is reflected in the FG2G curriculum's TRACE protocol, which structures learning interactions as dialogic exchanges rather than one-way instruction.

### Brain Development and Plasticity

**Perry, B. D. (2006).** Perry's neurosequential model demonstrates that brain development proceeds from the bottom up -- brainstem and midbrain (regulation) must be addressed before cortex (cognition) can be effectively engaged. This principle is the neurobiological rationale for the 5Rs sequence: Root and Regulate (lower brain) before Reflect (higher brain).

**Teicher, M. H., et al. (2016).** Neuroimaging studies have documented structural and functional brain differences in individuals who experienced childhood maltreatment, including reduced cortical thickness, altered white matter connectivity, and hyperactivation of threat-detection circuits. These differences are not permanent deficits -- they represent adaptations that can be modified through experience, supporting the FG2G curriculum's emphasis on creating consistent, safe learning environments as a form of neural re-patterning.

## Domain 2: Trauma-Informed Educational Practice

### Foundational Frameworks

**SAMHSA (2014).** The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration's six principles of trauma-informed care (safety, trustworthiness, peer support, collaboration, empowerment, cultural responsiveness) provide the organizational framework adopted by the FG2G curriculum. The FG2G contribution is translating these organizational principles into instructional practices.

**Cole, S. F., et al. (2005).** *Helping Traumatized Children Learn* was the first comprehensive guide to trauma-sensitive schools, published by Massachusetts Advocates for Children. Its flexible framework approach -- emphasizing schoolwide culture rather than individual student interventions -- is reflected in the FG2G curriculum's universal design (all students receive the 5Rs structure, not just identified students).

**Sporleder, J., & Forbes, H. T. (2016).** Drawing on the experience of Lincoln High School in Walla Walla, Washington, this work documented measurable improvements in suspension rates, attendance, and academic outcomes following adoption of trauma-informed practices. The case study demonstrates that systemic changes in how schools respond to dysregulation produce school-level improvements -- evidence that supports FG2G's framework-level approach.

### Regulation and Co-Regulation

**Porges, S. W. (2011).** Polyvagal theory provides the neurobiological mechanism for understanding why safety must precede learning. The theory identifies three hierarchical autonomic states (ventral vagal/social engagement, sympathetic/fight-flight, dorsal vagal/freeze-collapse) and explains how environmental cues (neuroception) determine which state is activated. The 5Rs Root phase is designed to activate neuroception of safety.

**Siegel, D. J. (1999, 2012).** The window of tolerance model and the concept of "integration" -- linking differentiated aspects of experience into a functional whole -- inform the FG2G curriculum's approach to regulation. Siegel's emphasis on the interpersonal neurobiology of relationships supports the curriculum's co-regulation model: students develop self-regulation capacity through repeated experiences of being regulated by a calm, attuned adult.

**Bath, H. (2008).** "The Three Pillars of Trauma-Informed Care" identified safety, connections, and managing emotions as the essential elements of trauma-responsive environments. These map directly to the Root (safety), Reconnect (connections), and Regulate (managing emotions) phases of the 5Rs.

## Domain 3: Therapeutic Horticulture and Garden-Based Education

### Therapeutic Mechanisms

**Kaplan, S. (1995).** Attention Restoration Theory proposes that natural environments restore directed attention -- the cognitive resource that is depleted by sustained mental effort and, relevantly, by the hypervigilance associated with trauma. Gardens provide "soft fascination" that engages involuntary attention while allowing directed attention to recover.

**Ulrich, R. S. (1991).** The psychoevolutionary stress recovery theory demonstrates that exposure to natural environments produces rapid, measurable reductions in sympathetic nervous system activation. This provides the physiological rationale for using the garden as a regulation environment.

**Van Den Berg, A. E., & Custers, M. H. G. (2011).** In a controlled experiment, 30 minutes of gardening produced significantly greater cortisol reduction than 30 minutes of indoor reading. The effect was accompanied by improvements in positive affect. This study is particularly relevant because it demonstrates that the active component is gardening itself, not simply being outdoors.

### Educational Outcomes

**Williams, D. R., & Dixon, P. S. (2013).** This meta-analysis of garden-based learning programs found consistent positive effects on science achievement, with effect sizes ranging from moderate to large. The analysis also found positive effects on attitudes toward school, vegetable consumption, and social skills. Notably, effects were strongest for students from disadvantaged backgrounds -- the population the FG2G curriculum primarily serves.

**Klemmer, C. D., Waliczek, T. M., & Zajicek, J. M. (2005).** A quasi-experimental study found that elementary students who participated in garden-based science instruction scored significantly higher on science achievement tests than students who received traditional instruction. The study controlled for teacher effects and prior achievement, strengthening the causal inference.

**Blair, D. (2009).** This review of garden-based education programs identified improvements in science knowledge, nutritional knowledge, and dietary behavior across 12 studies. The review also documented increased engagement and motivation among participating students.

**Ozer, E. J. (2007).** A longitudinal study of school garden programs found improvements in student engagement, social connectedness, and attitudes toward school, with effects persisting across the school year.

## Domain 4: Cognitive Science Applied to Instruction

### Metacognition

**Flavell, J. H. (1979).** Flavell's foundational work defined metacognition as "knowledge and cognition about cognitive phenomena" -- thinking about thinking. His framework distinguishes between metacognitive knowledge (what you know about your own thinking), metacognitive experiences (awareness during cognitive activities), and metacognitive strategies (deliberate regulation of cognitive processes). The TRACE protocol operationalizes all three dimensions.

**Zimmerman, B. J. (2002).** Self-regulated learning research demonstrates that teaching students to set goals, monitor progress, and adjust strategies produces significant improvements in academic achievement across content areas and age groups. The 5Rs framework structures self-regulation: Root (goal setting), Reflect (monitoring), Restore (adjustment), Reconnect (evaluation).

**Schraw, G., & Moshman, D. (1995).** This review identified metacognitive regulation as a stronger predictor of academic achievement than general intelligence. The implication for the FG2G curriculum is clear: teaching students to think about their thinking is more powerful than teaching them to think harder.

### Growth Mindset and Error

**Dweck, C. S. (2006).** *Mindset* documented the distinction between fixed and growth mindsets and their effects on motivation, persistence, and achievement. For trauma-affected students, the fixed mindset often co-occurs with a shame-based self-concept, making error particularly threatening. The Restore phase of the 5Rs applies growth mindset principles at the point of highest leverage: the moment an error occurs.

**Moser, J. S., et al. (2011).** Neuroimaging research demonstrated that individuals with growth mindsets show greater error-related brain activity (error positivity, or Pe) than those with fixed mindsets -- indicating deeper processing of errors. This finding supports the FG2G curriculum's emphasis on error analysis rather than simple correction.

### Experiential Learning

**Kolb, D. A. (1984).** The experiential learning cycle (concrete experience --> reflective observation --> abstract conceptualization --> active experimentation) provides the theoretical structure for the garden as a learning environment. Garden-based instruction naturally engages all four stages, producing deeper learning than instruction that relies primarily on abstract conceptualization.

## Synthesis: How the Literature Informs the 5Rs

| 5Rs Phase | Primary Literature Base | Key Insight |
|-----------|------------------------|-------------|
| **Root** | Polyvagal theory, ACEs research, attention restoration | Safety is a neurobiological prerequisite for learning; natural environments support neuroception of safety |
| **Regulate** | Window of tolerance, co-regulation research, cortisol studies | Regulation must be co-constructed before it can be self-directed; garden activities reduce physiological stress markers |
| **Reflect** | Metacognition, self-regulated learning, TRACE development | Explicit metacognitive instruction produces larger achievement gains than content instruction alone |
| **Restore** | Growth mindset, error processing, brain plasticity | How errors are framed determines whether students persist or withdraw; errors are neurological growth events |
| **Reconnect** | Experiential learning, situated cognition, community psychology | Learning transfers when it is applied in authentic contexts; community connection is protective against adversity |

## Gaps in the Literature

The FG2G curriculum addresses several areas where existing research is limited:

1. **Integration of trauma-informed practice with garden-based education**: Most studies examine these domains separately. The FG2G curriculum is among the first to systematically combine them.
2. **Metacognitive instruction for trauma-affected students**: Research on metacognition has primarily been conducted with general education populations. The TRACE protocol extends metacognitive instruction to students whose executive function has been compromised by adversity.
3. **AI-assisted trauma-informed tutoring**: The RootGuide AI tutor represents a novel application of educational technology, combining adaptive learning with dysregulation detection. No comparable system exists in the published literature.
4. **Longitudinal outcomes of garden-based, trauma-informed education**: While short-term outcomes are well-documented for each domain separately, longitudinal studies of integrated programs are needed.

These gaps represent opportunities for research partnerships between FG2G implementation sites and university researchers.
