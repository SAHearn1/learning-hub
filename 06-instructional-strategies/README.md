# Instructional Strategies

## Overview

The FG2G curriculum employs a coherent set of instructional strategies that integrate trauma-informed practice with rigorous academic instruction. Every strategy in this section is grounded in the 5Rs Framework (Root, Regulate, Reflect, Restore, Reconnect) and activated through the TRACE Protocol (Think, Reason, Articulate, Check, Extend). Educators select and sequence strategies based on student regulation levels, learning objectives, and garden context.

This document serves as the primary reference for educators implementing FG2G instruction across all grade bands (K-2, 3-5, 6-8, 9-12).

---

## 5Rs-Aligned Instructional Strategies

Each phase of the 5Rs Framework calls for distinct instructional approaches. The table below provides a comprehensive mapping.

| 5Rs Phase | Primary Goal | Core Instructional Strategies | Garden Connection |
|-----------|-------------|------------------------------|-------------------|
| **Root** | Establish safety and belonging | Community circle, sensory grounding, naming routines, anchor charts | Garden entry ritual, soil observation, sensory herb station |
| **Regulate** | Support co-regulation and self-regulation | Breathing activities, movement breaks, regulation check-ins, choice boards | Watering rhythm, raking patterns, silent seed sorting |
| **Reflect** | Develop metacognitive awareness | Journaling, think-alouds, garden observation logs, exit tickets | Plant growth documentation, weather journaling, harvest reflection |
| **Restore** | Repair and build academic skills | Direct instruction, guided practice, error analysis, TRACE protocol lessons | Transplanting, pruning, composting, pest management problem-solving |
| **Reconnect** | Transfer learning to life and community | Collaborative projects, community presentations, peer teaching, service learning | Harvest sharing, farmers market, community garden events |

### Phase 1: Root -- Establishing Safety

Instruction during the Root phase prioritizes predictability and sensory engagement. Educators should:

- Begin every session with a consistent opening routine (e.g., "Garden Greeting" circle where each student names one thing they notice in the garden space).
- Use anchor charts that remain visible throughout the session so students can orient themselves.
- Provide sensory grounding stations: a tray of soil, fresh herbs for smelling, textured leaves for tactile engagement.
- Limit verbal demands during this phase; allow nonverbal participation through pointing, drawing, or nodding.
- Monitor for signs of dysregulation (avoidance, agitation, withdrawal) and respond with proximity and calm narration.

### Phase 2: Regulate -- Supporting Regulation

Once safety is established, instruction shifts toward regulation support. Strategies include:

- **Rhythmic garden tasks**: Watering with a steady pour, raking soil in even strokes, and sorting seeds by size all provide sensory regulation through repetitive motor activity.
- **Regulation check-ins**: Use a visual scale (1-5 or color-coded) at least twice per session. Students self-assess and educators respond with appropriate supports.
- **Co-regulation modeling**: Educators narrate their own regulation strategies aloud: "I notice I am feeling rushed, so I am going to slow down my watering pace."
- **Choice within structure**: Offer two or three regulated task options rather than open-ended choices that may overwhelm.

### Phase 3: Reflect -- Building Metacognition

Reflection-phase instruction develops students' capacity to observe their own thinking and learning:

- **Think-alouds**: Educators model reflective thinking during garden observations. "I see the leaves are yellowing. I wonder if that means the plant needs more nitrogen. Let me think about what we learned about soil nutrients."
- **Garden observation logs**: Structured templates guide students to record what they see, what they wonder, and what connections they make.
- **Partner reflection**: Paired sharing with sentence stems such as "I noticed..." and "This reminds me of..." builds reflective language.
- **TRACE journaling**: Students practice the Think and Reason steps in written form before moving to Articulate.

### Phase 4: Restore -- Academic Skill Building

The Restore phase carries the heaviest academic load. Strategies include:

- **Direct instruction with garden context**: Teach content standards using garden phenomena as the primary context (e.g., fractions through harvest measurement, ecosystem concepts through companion planting).
- **Error analysis**: Present common misconceptions related to garden science and guide students through structured analysis using the TRACE protocol.
- **Guided practice with gradual release**: Move from educator modeling to shared practice to independent application within a single garden session.
- **Targeted intervention**: Use formative assessment data to pull small groups for reteaching while other students engage in independent garden tasks.

### Phase 5: Reconnect -- Extending to Community

Reconnect-phase instruction focuses on transfer, application, and community connection:

- **Collaborative projects**: Student teams plan and execute garden-to-table events, community harvest distributions, or garden design proposals.
- **Peer teaching**: Students teach younger students garden skills, reinforcing their own learning through explanation.
- **Community presentations**: Students present garden research findings, growth data, or sustainability proposals to authentic audiences.
- **Service learning integration**: Connect garden harvest to food pantry donations, creating tangible community impact.

## The TRACE Protocol as an Instructional Tool

The TRACE Protocol structures student thinking across all content areas and grade bands. Each step scaffolds the next.

| TRACE Step | Student Action | Educator Prompt | Garden Example |
|------------|---------------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Think** | Pause and observe before responding | "What do you notice? Take 10 seconds to look before you speak." | "Look at both plants. Just observe for now." |
| **Reason** | Apply logic, evidence, or prior knowledge | "What evidence supports your idea? What do you already know?" | "Why might one plant be taller? What variables differ?" |
| **Articulate** | Communicate thinking clearly | "Explain your reasoning to your partner using complete thoughts." | "Tell your partner your hypothesis about the growth difference." |
| **Check** | Verify accuracy and revise | "Does your answer make sense? What could you check?" | "Let's measure both plants and test your prediction." |
| **Extend** | Connect learning to new contexts | "Where else might this idea apply? What new questions arise?" | "How might this apply to our classroom plants or home gardens?" |

### Teaching the TRACE Protocol

1. **Introduce one step at a time** across the first four weeks of implementation. Do not rush to full protocol use.
2. **Use visual cue cards** posted in the garden and classroom showing each step with icons.
3. **Practice with low-stakes garden observations** before applying to academic content.
4. **Celebrate the process**, not just correct answers. Recognize students who demonstrate strong reasoning even when their conclusion needs revision.

## Seven Reasoning Moves

The FG2G curriculum identifies seven reasoning moves that students develop through garden-based learning. These moves are explicitly taught, practiced, and assessed.

| Reasoning Move | Definition | Garden Teaching Context | Grade Band Entry Point |
|---------------|-----------|------------------------|----------------------|
| **Observing** | Gathering sensory information systematically | Detailed plant observation using all senses | K-2 |
| **Comparing** | Identifying similarities and differences | Comparing growth rates, leaf shapes, soil types | K-2 |
| **Sequencing** | Ordering events or steps logically | Documenting plant life cycles, planning planting schedules | 1-3 |
| **Classifying** | Grouping by shared attributes | Sorting seeds, categorizing plants by family | 2-5 |
| **Inferring** | Drawing conclusions from evidence | Diagnosing plant health issues from visible symptoms | 3-8 |
| **Predicting** | Projecting outcomes based on patterns | Forecasting harvest yields based on growth data | 4-9 |
| **Evaluating** | Judging quality and effectiveness of solutions | Assessing companion planting effectiveness, critiquing garden designs | 6-12 |

### Teaching Reasoning Moves Explicitly

- Name the move: "Today we are going to practice inferring. Inferring means drawing a conclusion from evidence we can observe."
- Model the move with a think-aloud in the garden context.
- Provide structured practice with a graphic organizer specific to that reasoning move.
- Debrief by asking students to identify which reasoning move they used and when.

## Scaffolding Strategies: Gradual Release Model

The FG2G gradual release model adapts the classic "I Do, We Do, You Do" framework for the garden context.

| Stage | Educator Role | Student Role | Garden Example |
|-------|-------------|-------------|----------------|
| **Focused Instruction** ("I Do") | Models thinking and skill using TRACE | Observes, listens, takes notes | Educator demonstrates soil pH testing while narrating reasoning |
| **Guided Instruction** ("We Do Together") | Provides prompts, asks guiding questions | Responds to prompts, attempts with support | Educator and students test pH of a second bed together |
| **Collaborative Learning** ("You Do Together") | Monitors, intervenes as needed | Works with peers to apply skill | Student pairs test pH of their assigned beds |
| **Independent Practice** ("You Do Alone") | Assesses, provides feedback | Applies skill independently | Each student tests, records, and interprets pH data |

### Scaffolding for Regulation

In trauma-informed contexts, scaffolding also applies to regulation demands:

- Reduce cognitive load during dysregulated moments by providing pre-completed graphic organizers.
- Offer "regulation scaffolds" such as a calm corner in the garden, noise-reducing headphones, or a sensory tool kit.
- Gradually increase independence in regulation as students build capacity across the year.

## Differentiation Within the Garden Context

The garden provides natural differentiation through varied task complexity, sensory modalities, and pacing.

| Differentiation Type | Strategy | Example |
|---------------------|----------|---------|
| **Content** | Tiered reading materials about the same garden topic | All students study composting; texts range from picture-based to scientific articles |
| **Process** | Multiple pathways to explore the same concept | Students choose to learn about pollination through observation, drawing, reading, or video |
| **Product** | Varied ways to demonstrate learning | Students show understanding of plant life cycles through a diagram, oral presentation, model, or digital slideshow |
| **Environment** | Flexible grouping and workspace | Some students work at raised beds, others at the potting table, others in the shade reading area |
| **Regulation Support** | Tiered regulation scaffolds | Some students self-regulate independently; others receive co-regulation support from an adult |

## Collaborative Learning Structures

| Structure | Description | Best Used During (5Rs Phase) | Group Size |
|-----------|------------|------------------------------|------------|
| **Garden Partners** | Paired students share a garden bed and collaborate on all tasks | Regulate, Restore | 2 |
| **Expert Groups** | Each group specializes in one garden topic, then teaches others | Restore, Reconnect | 3-4 |
| **Harvest Circles** | Whole-class discussion during or after harvest activities | Reflect, Reconnect | Whole class |
| **Design Teams** | Student teams plan garden layouts, experiments, or events | Restore, Reconnect | 4-5 |
| **Regulation Buddies** | Paired students support each other's regulation during garden work | Regulate | 2 |
| **Cross-Age Mentoring** | Older students mentor younger students in garden skills | Reconnect | 2 (cross-grade) |

## Questioning Techniques by Phase

| 5Rs Phase | Question Type | Example Questions |
|-----------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Root** | Invitational, low-demand | "What do you see?" / "Would you like to touch the soil or smell the herbs?" |
| **Regulate** | Regulation-focused | "How is your body feeling right now?" / "What would help you feel settled?" |
| **Reflect** | Metacognitive | "What did you notice about your thinking?" / "What surprised you today?" |
| **Restore** | Analytical, TRACE-aligned | "What evidence supports that?" / "How could you check your reasoning?" |
| **Reconnect** | Transfer and application | "Where else in your life could you use this?" / "Who in our community would benefit from this?" |

## Technology Integration in Instruction

Technology serves instruction in the FG2G curriculum without replacing hands-on garden experiences.

| Technology Tool | Instructional Use | 5Rs Alignment |
|----------------|-------------------|---------------|
| **RootGuide AI Tutor** | Personalized TRACE protocol practice, reasoning move coaching | Restore |
| **Digital garden journals** | Photo documentation, growth tracking, reflection prompts | Reflect |
| **Time-lapse cameras** | Capturing plant growth for observation and data analysis | Reflect, Restore |
| **Soil sensor dashboards** | Real-time data on moisture, pH, and temperature for analysis | Restore |
| **Video reflection tools** | Students record and review their own TRACE protocol explanations | Reflect, Reconnect |
| **Collaborative documents** | Shared garden design plans and research reports | Restore, Reconnect |

### Guidelines for Technology Use

1. Technology should amplify garden-based learning, not replace it. Screens are used after hands-on experience, not instead of it.
2. Ensure equitable access. If digital tools are used, provide alternatives for students without devices at home.
3. Use the RootGuide AI tutor's dysregulation detection to adjust instructional pacing in digital sessions.
4. Limit screen time for K-2 students to no more than 15 minutes per session; increase gradually for older grade bands.

## Strategy Selection Guide

When planning a lesson, educators should consider the following decision flow:

1. **Assess the regulation level** of the group. If regulation is low, begin with Root and Regulate strategies before attempting Restore-phase academic instruction.
2. **Identify the target reasoning move(s)** for the lesson and select activities that practice those moves in the garden context.
3. **Apply the TRACE protocol** to at least one key learning moment in every lesson.
4. **Differentiate** based on student readiness, using the garden's natural variety of tasks to provide appropriate challenge levels.
5. **Close with Reflect or Reconnect** to consolidate learning and prepare for transfer.
