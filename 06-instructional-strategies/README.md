# Instructional Strategies

## Overview

The RootWork curriculum employs a carefully curated set of instructional strategies that honor three simultaneous commitments: trauma-informed practice, rigorous academic engagement, and authentic garden-based learning. Every strategy described here is filtered through the 5Rs Framework, meaning it must (1) maintain emotional safety, (2) support regulation, (3) promote genuine thinking rather than compliance, (4) treat errors as growth opportunities, and (5) connect learning to real-world meaning.

These strategies are not a menu from which educators randomly select. They form an integrated instructional system in which each strategy supports and is supported by the others. A trauma-informed classroom that lacks academic rigor fails students just as surely as a rigorous classroom that ignores emotional safety. The RootWork approach holds both in tension, using the garden as the context that makes this integration natural.

## Core Instructional Approaches

### 1. The TRACE Protocol

The TRACE protocol is the primary instructional routine for the Reflect phase across all grade bands. It structures every academic interaction around five cognitive moves:

| Step | Prompt | Cognitive Purpose | Educator Role |
|------|--------|-------------------|---------------|
| **Think** | "What do you notice?" | Activate observation, prior knowledge, and curiosity | Pose an open-ended question; accept all observations without judgment |
| **Reason** | "What's your plan?" | Develop strategic thinking and hypothesis formation | Ask probing questions; resist the urge to direct |
| **Articulate** | "Walk me through your thinking" | Build academic discourse and metacognitive awareness | Listen actively; paraphrase; ask clarifying questions |
| **Check** | "How do you know?" | Develop self-verification and evidence-based reasoning | Redirect to verification rather than confirming/denying |
| **Extend** | "What else could you do with this?" | Promote transfer, generalization, and creative thinking | Offer connections; invite problem posing |

**Implementation by Grade Band:**

- **K-2:** Use simplified language ("What do you see?" "What do you think?" "Tell me more" "Let's check" "What if?"). Teacher models all five steps explicitly. One step per interaction is sufficient initially.
- **3-5:** Students learn the TRACE acronym and begin self-prompting. Peer partnerships support Articulate and Check steps. All five steps are used routinely.
- **6-8:** Students internalize TRACE and begin applying it independently. Written TRACE reflections in journals. Peer Socratic dialogues use TRACE structure.
- **9-12:** TRACE becomes habitual. Students use it to structure research, design experiments, and evaluate arguments. They teach TRACE to younger students through mentorship.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Rushing through Think to get to the "real" instruction -- thinking time IS instruction
- Accepting a correct answer and skipping to Extend without going through Articulate and Check
- Using TRACE as a rigid script rather than a flexible framework
- Asking Check questions that are really compliance checks ("Did you do it the way I showed you?") rather than genuine verification

### 2. Scaffolded Inquiry

Inquiry-based instruction in the RootWork curriculum follows a gradual release model adapted for trauma-informed contexts:

**Level 1: Structured Inquiry (K-2, early 3-5)**
- Teacher provides the question and the procedure
- Students conduct the investigation and collect data
- Class discusses findings together
- Example: "We're going to find out if seeds need light to grow. Here's how we'll set up our experiment..."

**Level 2: Guided Inquiry (3-5, early 6-8)**
- Teacher provides the question
- Students design the procedure with guidance
- Students collect and analyze data
- Example: "We want to know which soil type is best for growing lettuce. How could we find out?"

**Level 3: Open Inquiry (6-8, 9-12)**
- Students generate the question from observations
- Students design the full investigation
- Students analyze, draw conclusions, and communicate findings
- Example: Student notices yellowing leaves on tomato plants and designs an experiment to determine the cause

**Level 4: Problem Posing (9-12)**
- Students identify a real-world problem through garden/community observation
- Students frame a researchable question
- Students conduct original research and propose solutions
- Example: Student investigates food desert mapping in their community and proposes a community garden intervention

### 3. Differentiated Instruction Through Garden Contexts

The garden naturally supports differentiation because it provides multiple entry points, representations, and levels of complexity within a shared context:

**Content Differentiation:**
| Student Need | Strategy | Example |
|-------------|----------|---------|
| Below grade level | Simplified vocabulary, concrete models, reduced quantity | Count 10 seeds instead of 50; use picture labels |
| On grade level | Standard curriculum with garden context | Measure plant growth in inches; record in data table |
| Above grade level | Extended complexity, abstract connections | Calculate growth rate; predict future height using patterns |
| English Language Learner | Visual supports, bilingual vocabulary, partner work | Garden vocabulary cards in home language; labeled diagrams |
| IEP accommodations | Modified materials, extended time, alternative response modes | Larger seeds for fine motor challenges; oral instead of written responses |

**Process Differentiation:**
- **Tiered Tasks:** All students work on the same concept but at different levels of complexity. Everyone measures plants, but the recording method varies (drawing, table, graph, statistical analysis).
- **Learning Stations:** Garden stations allow students to rotate through activities at their own pace. A measurement station, an observation station, a writing station, and a construction station can all address the same learning objectives.
- **Flexible Grouping:** Students are grouped by interest (what to grow), readiness (skill level), or learning profile (how they learn best) -- not tracked permanently.

**Product Differentiation:**
- **Choice Boards:** Students choose how to demonstrate understanding: written report, oral presentation, poster, model, video, or demonstration.
- **Growth Journals:** Personalized documentation that meets each student where they are.
- **Exhibition Options:** Students select their best work for portfolios and presentations.

### 4. Collaborative Learning Structures

Social learning is central to the RootWork approach, but it must be structured carefully in trauma-informed contexts where peer interactions can be both healing and triggering:

**Think-Pair-Share (Garden Buddy Share):**
1. Think: Individual reflection time (30 seconds to 2 minutes)
2. Pair: Share with a designated garden buddy
3. Share: Selected pairs share with the class
- Trauma adaptation: Always give Think time first. Never cold-call. Use random selection tools (drawing plant names from a pot) rather than hand-raising, which can create performance anxiety.

**Jigsaw:**
1. Each student becomes an "expert" on one aspect (e.g., one garden zone)
2. Experts teach their group
3. Groups synthesize all perspectives
- Trauma adaptation: Ensure every student has a role and contribution. "Expert" status builds competence and belonging.

**Gallery Walk:**
1. Student work is displayed around the room or garden
2. Students rotate, observing and leaving constructive feedback on sticky notes
3. Students return to their own work to read feedback
- Trauma adaptation: Model constructive feedback language explicitly. Use sentence starters: "I notice..." "I wonder..." "This reminds me of..."

**Reciprocal Teaching:**
1. Students take turns leading small-group discussion
2. Roles rotate: Summarizer, Questioner, Clarifier, Predictor
3. Applied to reading scientific texts, garden guides, and seed catalogs
- Trauma adaptation: Roles provide structure and predictability. No student is put on the spot without preparation.

### 5. Engagement Modes

The RootWork curriculum uses five distinct engagement modes during the Reflect phase, each offering a different cognitive pathway:

**Forward Solving:**
- Standard problem-solving approach: given information leads to solution
- "We have a 12-foot by 8-foot garden bed. How many square feet of planting space is that?"
- Appropriate for introducing new concepts and building procedural fluency

**Reverse Engineering:**
- Working backward from a solution to understand the process
- "This garden produced 48 pounds of tomatoes. If each plant produced 6 pounds, how many plants were there?"
- Develops flexible thinking and deeper conceptual understanding

**Error Analysis:**
- Examining a mistake (presented or authentic) to identify and correct the reasoning
- "A student said that 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5. What went wrong in their thinking?"
- Normalizes errors as learning tools; builds analytical skills; reduces shame around mistakes

**Multiple Pathways:**
- Solving the same problem using two or more different strategies
- "Find the area of this L-shaped garden bed. Can you solve it in at least two different ways?"
- Promotes mathematical flexibility; honors diverse thinking styles; builds strategy repertoire

**Problem Posing:**
- Students create their own problems based on garden observations
- "Look at our garden data. Write three questions that could be answered using this data."
- Develops mathematical creativity; deepens understanding; increases student ownership

### 6. Explicit Vocabulary Instruction

Academic vocabulary is taught through a garden-embedded approach:

**Three-Tier Vocabulary Framework:**

| Tier | Description | Garden Example | Instruction Method |
|------|------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Tier 1** | Common words | plant, water, grow, dig | Incidental; used naturally in garden context |
| **Tier 2** | Academic cross-curricular words | observe, compare, analyze, evidence, hypothesis, represent | Explicit instruction; word walls; repeated use across contexts |
| **Tier 3** | Domain-specific words | photosynthesis, germination, transpiration, decomposition, pollination | Direct instruction with garden demonstrations; visual supports |

**Vocabulary Routine:**
1. Teacher provides a student-friendly explanation (not a dictionary definition)
2. Teacher provides or demonstrates a garden-based example
3. Students create their own representation (drawing, gesture, sentence)
4. Students engage with the word in multiple contexts throughout the week
5. Students play vocabulary games (garden charades, word sorts, vocabulary journals)

### 7. Formative Assessment Strategies

Ongoing assessment is embedded in instruction, not separated from it:

| Strategy | Description | When to Use | Trauma-Informed Adaptation |
|----------|------------|-------------|---------------------------|
| **Exit Tickets** | Brief written response to a prompt at session end | End of Reflect phase | Offer drawing option; never grade; use to plan next session |
| **Fist-to-Five** | Students show 0-5 fingers to indicate understanding | Quick check during instruction | Private check (hand close to body); never call out low numbers |
| **Growth Journal Review** | Teacher reads and responds to journal entries | Weekly | Written feedback only; never grade journal entries; respond with encouragement and questions |
| **Observation Checklists** | Teacher observes specific skills during garden activities | During hands-on work | Focus on 4-5 students per session; note strengths first |
| **Whiteboard Response** | Students write answers on personal whiteboards | During math instruction | Erase quickly; celebrate strategies, not just answers |
| **Turn and Talk** | Partner discussions with teacher circulating to listen | During TRACE protocol | Listen without interrupting; note patterns for whole-group instruction |
| **Self-Assessment** | Students evaluate their own understanding using rubrics | End of unit or session | Rubrics use growth language; no letter grades; focus on "next steps" |

### 8. Garden-Based Experiential Learning

The garden is not a supplement -- it is the primary instructional context. Experiential learning follows Kolb's cycle, adapted through the 5Rs:

**Concrete Experience (Root + early Reflect):**
- Students engage directly with the garden: planting, measuring, observing, harvesting
- Sensory engagement activates multiple neural pathways and supports regulation
- Real-world context provides intrinsic motivation

**Reflective Observation (Reflect -- TRACE: Think + Articulate):**
- Students observe and describe what they experienced
- Growth journals, partner sharing, and class discussions
- "What did you notice when we transplanted the seedlings?"

**Abstract Conceptualization (Reflect -- TRACE: Reason + Check):**
- Students connect experiences to academic concepts
- "The seedlings wilted when we moved them to direct sunlight. What scientific concept explains this?"
- Building from concrete to abstract through guided questioning

**Active Experimentation (Reflect -- TRACE: Extend + Reconnect):**
- Students apply concepts to new situations
- "Based on what we learned about transplant shock, what should we do differently next time?"
- Planning, predicting, and testing in the garden

### 9. Culturally Responsive Teaching

The garden is an inherently multicultural space. The RootWork curriculum leverages this:

- **Cultural gardening traditions:** Incorporate Three Sisters planting (Indigenous), companion planting (Asian traditions), kitchen gardens (European), milpa farming (Central American), and other global approaches
- **Heritage crops:** Grow crops that connect to students' cultural backgrounds and family foodways
- **Multilingual vocabulary:** Garden terms displayed in all languages represented in the classroom
- **Family knowledge:** Invite families to share gardening and food preparation traditions
- **Food as culture:** Cooking activities celebrate diverse cuisines using garden produce
- **Counter-narratives:** Challenge the narrative that "some cultures don't garden" by highlighting agricultural traditions from all regions

### 10. Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

All instruction follows UDL principles:

**Multiple Means of Engagement (the WHY):**
- Choice in garden tasks and learning products
- Authentic purpose (growing real food for real people)
- Gradual release of responsibility across grade bands
- Regulation strategies embedded in instruction

**Multiple Means of Representation (the WHAT):**
- Visual models alongside verbal instruction
- Concrete manipulatives before abstract notation
- Real garden specimens paired with diagrams and text
- Video, audio, and hands-on demonstrations
- Bilingual and pictorial supports

**Multiple Means of Action and Expression (the HOW):**
- Drawing, writing, speaking, building, and demonstrating
- Typed, handwritten, or dictated responses
- Individual, partner, and group work options
- Physical and digital portfolio formats
- Performance tasks with flexible response modes

## Strategy Selection Guide by Phase

| 5Rs Phase | Primary Strategies | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Root** | Sensory grounding, check-in routines, transition rituals, garden observation | Keep predictable; never rush; honor student state |
| **Regulate** | Breathing exercises, movement breaks, sensory tools, co-regulation, garden heavy work | Match strategy to arousal level; offer choice; no shame |
| **Reflect** | TRACE protocol, scaffolded inquiry, engagement modes, collaborative structures, garden experiential learning | Maintain rigor while monitoring regulation; differentiate |
| **Restore** | Error analysis, growth mindset reframing, garden metaphors, scaffolded retry, peer support | Never shame; celebrate the error as learning; keep calm tone |
| **Reconnect** | Real-world connections, community projects, family engagement, celebration, garden harvesting | Make learning visible; build identity as learner/grower |
