# Why This Curriculum

## The Crisis at the Intersection of Trauma and Education

### The Prevalence of Adverse Childhood Experiences

The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) study, originally conducted by Felitti and Anda (1998) with over 17,000 participants at Kaiser Permanente, revealed that childhood adversity is not rare -- it is the norm. Subsequent research has confirmed and expanded these findings:

- **64% of U.S. adults** report at least one ACE before age 18 (Centers for Disease Control, 2019).
- **17.3% of children** (approximately 1 in 6) have experienced four or more ACEs -- the threshold associated with dramatically increased risk for chronic health conditions, mental illness, substance use, and reduced educational attainment.
- **Children in poverty**, children of color, and children in the child welfare system experience ACEs at disproportionately higher rates.
- ACEs are cumulative and interactive: the presence of one ACE increases the likelihood of others, creating cascading effects on development.

### How ACEs Affect Learning

The impact of adverse childhood experiences on the brain and nervous system directly undermines the cognitive processes that schools depend on:

| Brain Region / System | Impact of Chronic Stress | Educational Consequence |
|----------------------|-------------------------|------------------------|
| **Prefrontal cortex** | Reduced volume and connectivity | Impaired executive function, planning, and impulse control |
| **Hippocampus** | Cortisol-mediated atrophy | Disrupted memory formation and retrieval |
| **Amygdala** | Hyperactivation and enlargement | Heightened threat detection; neutral stimuli interpreted as dangerous |
| **HPA axis** | Dysregulated stress response | Chronic fight-flight-freeze; inability to return to baseline |
| **Mirror neuron system** | Impaired development | Difficulty with empathy, social learning, and relationship formation |

The result is a student who -- through no fault or choice of their own -- cannot access the cognitive states that traditional instruction requires. Asking a dysregulated student to "pay attention and try harder" is like asking someone running from a bear to solve a math problem. The nervous system has other priorities.

### The Limitations of Current Approaches

Schools have adopted various responses to the challenges posed by childhood adversity:

**Social-Emotional Learning (SEL) Programs**: Valuable but typically delivered as stand-alone lessons (30 minutes weekly) that are disconnected from academic instruction. Students learn about emotions in one context and are expected to apply that learning in a completely different context.

**Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS)**: Effective for many students but relies on extrinsic motivation systems (reward/consequence) that can inadvertently re-traumatize students whose behavior is driven by nervous system dysregulation rather than willful noncompliance.

**Counseling and Mental Health Services**: Essential but insufficient as a sole strategy. Most schools have counselor-to-student ratios exceeding 1:400. Pull-out counseling removes students from instruction and can carry stigma. Healing through talk therapy requires the very executive function and emotional regulation that trauma has impaired.

**Alternative Placements**: When traditional approaches fail, students are often moved to alternative settings -- a response that addresses the school's immediate need but frequently worsens the student's trajectory by disrupting relationships, reducing academic expectations, and concentrating adversity-affected students without proportional increases in support.

**The Common Thread**: All of these approaches treat the student's emotional and psychological needs as separate from -- and often competing with -- their academic needs. They operate on an implicit assumption: deal with the trauma first, then teach. Or: teach regardless of the trauma. Neither works.

## What Is Different About FG2G

The From Garden to Growth curriculum was designed to eliminate the false choice between healing and learning. Its distinguishing features:

### 1. Integration, Not Separation

The 5Rs Framework embeds social-emotional support into the structure of every academic lesson. There is no separate "SEL time." Regulation is assessed at the start of every session (Root phase). Co-regulation strategies are deployed when needed (Regulate phase). Metacognitive reflection is part of every learning interaction (Reflect phase). Errors are analyzed and reframed in real time (Restore phase). And application connects learning to the student's own life and community (Reconnect phase).

### 2. Trauma-Informed at the Instructional Level

Most trauma-informed school initiatives operate at the building level -- changing policies, training staff on ACEs awareness, creating calm corners. FG2G goes further, embedding trauma-informed principles into the minute-by-minute flow of instruction:

- **Predictable sequences** reduce anxiety and build trust.
- **Regulation assessment** catches dysregulation before it escalates.
- **Co-regulation strategies** are built into the lesson flow, not bolted on.
- **Error reframing** prevents the shame response that shuts down learning.
- **Choice and agency** are designed into every phase.

### 3. The Garden as a Therapeutic Learning Environment

The Living Learning Lab is not an extracurricular activity or a reward for good behavior. It is the primary instructional environment -- a space specifically designed to activate the parasympathetic nervous system, provide sensory grounding, and create the conditions under which trauma-affected students can learn.

### 4. Research-Grounded, Practice-Tested

The 5Rs Framework draws on polyvagal theory, window of tolerance research, growth mindset, metacognition, and experiential learning theory. But it was not designed in a university laboratory. It was developed through daily practice with students at Community Exceptional Children's Services -- students with significant trauma histories who had been underserved by traditional approaches.

### 5. Technology That Serves the Framework

The RootGuide AI tutor is not a generic educational technology product with trauma-informed marketing. It was built from the ground up to implement the 5Rs and TRACE protocol, with dysregulation detection that can identify when a student needs co-regulation support before the student themselves may be aware of it.

## Who This Curriculum Is For

### Primary Audience

- **Educators** working with students who have experienced ACEs, particularly in Title I schools, alternative education settings, special education programs, and community-based educational services.
- **School counselors and social workers** seeking to integrate therapeutic approaches with academic instruction.
- **Administrators** looking for evidence-based approaches to improving outcomes for underserved student populations.

### Secondary Audience

- **General education teachers** who recognize that many students in any classroom have experienced adversity.
- **Community-based organizations** providing after-school, summer, or wraparound educational services.
- **Teacher preparation programs** seeking to develop trauma-informed instructional competencies in pre-service educators.
- **Policymakers** evaluating innovative approaches to educational equity.

## The Evidence Base

The FG2G curriculum draws on four converging bodies of evidence:

1. **ACEs and neurodevelopment**: The impact of adverse childhood experiences on brain development, stress response, and learning capacity (Felitti et al., 1998; Shonkoff et al., 2012; National Scientific Council on the Developing Child, 2014).

2. **Trauma-informed education**: Principles and practices for creating school environments that recognize and respond to the impact of trauma (Cole et al., 2005; NCTSN, 2017; Sporleder & Forbes, 2016).

3. **Therapeutic horticulture**: Research demonstrating the psychological, physiological, and educational benefits of garden-based activities (Sempik, Hine & Wilcox, 2010; Detweiler et al., 2012; Williams & Dixon, 2013).

4. **Cognitive science and metacognition**: Research on self-regulated learning, growth mindset, and the development of metacognitive skills in educational settings (Zimmerman, 2002; Dweck, 2006; Flavell, 1979; Schraw & Moshman, 1995).

These bodies of evidence are explored in detail in Section 03 (Theoretical Foundation).

## The Promise

When a trauma-affected student steps into a Living Learning Lab and is met with a predictable grounding routine, a warm check-in, and a garden activity that makes academic content tangible -- something changes. Not because the trauma disappears, but because the learning environment finally matches what their nervous system needs.

The FG2G curriculum does not promise to heal childhood adversity. It promises to stop making it worse -- and to create the conditions under which healing and learning can happen together.
