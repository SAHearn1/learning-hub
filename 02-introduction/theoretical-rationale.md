# Theoretical Rationale

The *From Garden to Growth* curriculum is grounded in a convergence of established theoretical frameworks from neuroscience, developmental psychology, educational research, and therapeutic horticulture. This chapter provides a detailed examination of each theoretical foundation, explains how it informs the design and implementation of the 5Rs Framework, and presents the research evidence supporting garden-based education as a vehicle for trauma-informed practice.

The theories presented here are not decorative citations. They are the load-bearing structures of the curriculum -- the scientific reasoning behind every instructional decision, every garden activity, and every assessment practice. Educators who understand these foundations will implement the curriculum with greater confidence, flexibility, and fidelity.

---

## 1. Polyvagal Theory (Stephen Porges)

### The Theory

Dr. Stephen Porges' Polyvagal Theory, first articulated in 1994 and developed extensively over the subsequent decades, provides a neurobiological framework for understanding how the autonomic nervous system governs our capacity for social engagement, self-regulation, and threat response. The theory identifies three hierarchical states of the autonomic nervous system, each mediated by a distinct neural circuit:

1. **Ventral Vagal State (Social Engagement System):** When the nervous system detects safety, the ventral vagal complex -- the most recently evolved branch of the vagus nerve -- activates the social engagement system. In this state, the muscles of the face, voice, and middle ear are regulated in ways that support eye contact, vocal prosody, and attentive listening. Heart rate is calm and variable. The individual is capable of learning, connection, and flexible thinking. This is the state in which education is possible.

2. **Sympathetic Activation (Fight or Flight):** When the nervous system detects danger, the sympathetic branch takes over. Heart rate increases. Muscles tense. Attention narrows to the perceived threat. Cortisol and adrenaline flood the system. The individual is mobilized for action -- to fight or to flee. In this state, the prefrontal cortex goes partially offline, and the capacity for complex thinking, perspective-taking, and reflective learning is substantially diminished.

3. **Dorsal Vagal State (Shutdown/Freeze):** When the nervous system detects life-threatening danger and determines that neither fight nor flight is possible, the oldest branch of the vagus nerve -- the dorsal vagal complex -- initiates a shutdown response. Heart rate drops. Energy is conserved. The individual may appear numb, dissociated, collapsed, or "checked out." This is the freeze response, and it is the nervous system's last resort for survival.

A critical insight of Polyvagal Theory is the concept of **neuroception** -- the nervous system's subconscious, continuous assessment of safety and danger in the environment. Neuroception occurs below the threshold of conscious awareness. A child does not decide to feel unsafe; their nervous system detects cues of threat (or safety) and shifts the autonomic state accordingly. For children whose early environments were characterized by unpredictability and danger, neuroception may be calibrated to detect threat where none exists, leading to chronic sympathetic activation or dorsal vagal shutdown in the classroom.

### Mapping to the 5Rs Framework

Polyvagal Theory is the primary neurobiological foundation for the **Root** and **Regulate** phases of the 5Rs Framework.

- **Root Phase:** The Root phase is designed to shift the nervous system toward the ventral vagal state by providing cues of safety -- predictable routines, warm vocal tone, regulated adult presence, sensory grounding in the garden environment, and the absence of threat cues such as raised voices, sudden changes, or evaluative pressure. The garden itself serves as a powerful cue of safety: natural environments have been shown to activate the parasympathetic nervous system and promote ventral vagal engagement.

- **Regulate Phase:** The Regulate phase provides explicit practices -- breathing exercises, mindful movement, sensory engagement with soil and plants -- that support the transition from sympathetic or dorsal vagal states back into the ventral vagal window. These practices are not generic relaxation techniques; they are designed to engage the specific neural pathways identified by Polyvagal Theory as mediating the shift toward social engagement.

- **Reconnect Phase:** The social engagement system described by Porges is the neurobiological substrate for the connection, collaboration, and community-building practices of the Reconnect phase. Children can only genuinely connect with peers and adults when their nervous system is in the ventral vagal state -- which is why the Reconnect phase comes after Root and Regulate, not before.

## 2. Window of Tolerance (Daniel Siegel)

### The Theory

Dr. Daniel Siegel, a clinical professor of psychiatry at UCLA and co-director of the Mindful Awareness Research Center, developed the concept of the **Window of Tolerance** as a framework for understanding the optimal zone of autonomic arousal within which an individual can effectively process information, manage emotions, and respond to the demands of their environment.

Within the Window of Tolerance, a person experiences a flow of changing states of arousal -- moments of increased activation and moments of relative calm -- without being pushed into extreme dysregulation. The individual can think clearly, feel their emotions without being overwhelmed by them, and engage in goal-directed behavior.

When arousal exceeds the upper boundary of the window, the individual enters a state of **hyperarousal**: anxiety, panic, hypervigilance, racing thoughts, emotional flooding, or explosive behavior. When arousal drops below the lower boundary, the individual enters **hypoarousal**: emotional numbness, dissociation, cognitive fog, flat affect, or physical collapse.

A central principle of Siegel's work is that the width of the Window of Tolerance is not fixed. It is shaped by experience. Secure attachment, predictable environments, co-regulation with attuned adults, and repeated experiences of manageable stress all widen the window. Chronic adversity, unpredictable threat, and the absence of co-regulating relationships narrow it.

For children impacted by ACEs, the Window of Tolerance may be so narrow that the ordinary demands of the classroom -- waiting in line, transitioning between activities, receiving corrective feedback, encountering a challenging problem -- are sufficient to push them outside the zone of effective functioning. The resulting behaviors -- tantrums, shutdowns, refusals, aggression, dissociation -- are not choices. They are the predictable consequences of a nervous system operating outside its functional range.

### Mapping to the 5Rs Framework

The Window of Tolerance is the conceptual framework that organizes the entire lesson sequence of the 5Rs:

- **Root Phase:** Establishes a baseline of safety that positions the child at the center of their Window of Tolerance before any academic demand is introduced.
- **Regulate Phase:** Provides tools and practices for widening the Window of Tolerance over time and for returning to the window when arousal pushes beyond its boundaries.
- **Reflect Phase:** Engages metacognitive and reflective capacities that are only available within the Window of Tolerance, reinforcing the importance of sequencing reflection after regulation.
- **Restore Phase:** Addresses the inevitable moments when children are pushed outside their window, providing structured pathways back to the zone of effective functioning.
- **Reconnect Phase:** Leverages the expanded capacity for social engagement that comes from operating within the Window of Tolerance to build community, purpose, and belonging.

## 3. Attachment Theory (John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth)

### The Theory

Attachment Theory, originally developed by British psychiatrist Dr. John Bowlby in the 1950s and 1960s and empirically validated by American-Canadian developmental psychologist Dr. Mary Ainsworth through her landmark "Strange Situation" research, describes the innate human need for close emotional bonds with caregiving figures and the profound impact of early attachment experiences on lifelong development.

Bowlby proposed that children are biologically wired to seek proximity to attachment figures, particularly under conditions of distress. The quality of the caregiver's response -- whether it is consistent, sensitive, and attuned or unpredictable, dismissive, or frightening -- shapes the child's **internal working model** of relationships: a set of implicit expectations about whether others are trustworthy, whether the self is worthy of care, and whether the world is fundamentally safe or dangerous.

Ainsworth's research identified three primary attachment patterns:

- **Secure attachment:** The child has experienced consistent, sensitive caregiving and has developed an internal working model of relationships as safe and reliable. These children can use adults as a secure base from which to explore, tolerate manageable distress, and recover from setbacks with adult support.

- **Insecure-avoidant attachment:** The child has experienced caregiving that is emotionally distant, dismissive, or rejecting. The child learns to suppress attachment needs, avoid seeking help, and rely on self-sufficiency as a survival strategy. In the classroom, these children may appear independent but are actually isolated, reluctant to ask for help, and emotionally constricted.

- **Insecure-anxious/ambivalent attachment:** The child has experienced caregiving that is inconsistent -- sometimes available, sometimes not. The child develops heightened attachment behaviors (clinging, anxiety, difficulty separating) as a strategy for maintaining proximity to an unpredictable caregiver. In the classroom, these children may be emotionally volatile, attention-seeking, and deeply sensitive to perceived rejection.

Later research by Dr. Mary Main added a fourth category:

- **Disorganized attachment:** The child has experienced caregiving that is itself a source of fear -- as in cases of abuse, severe neglect, or caregiver mental illness. The child faces an irresolvable paradox: the person they are biologically driven to approach for safety is also the source of danger. This produces disorganized, contradictory behavior -- approaching the caregiver while looking away, freezing mid-movement, or displaying simultaneous approach and avoidance. Children with disorganized attachment are at the highest risk for the behavioral and emotional difficulties that schools find most challenging.

### Mapping to the 5Rs Framework

Attachment Theory informs every phase of the 5Rs Framework, but is particularly central to the **Root**, **Restore**, and **Reconnect** phases:

- **Root Phase:** The predictable routines, warm adult presence, and consistent garden environment of the Root phase function as a "secure base" -- providing the felt safety that allows children to take the risks inherent in learning. For children with insecure attachment, the garden itself can become a transitional attachment object: a reliable, nonjudgmental presence that is always there, always growing, always responsive to care.

- **Restore Phase:** Restorative practices within the framework are modeled on the attachment cycle of disruption and repair. When connection is broken -- through conflict, dysregulation, or misunderstanding -- the Restore phase provides structured opportunities for repair that teach children that relationships can survive rupture. For children whose early attachment experiences taught them that rupture is permanent, this is a transformative learning.

- **Reconnect Phase:** The community-building practices of the Reconnect phase gradually expand the child's relational capacity, moving from the safety of the student-educator dyad to small-group collaboration and eventually to broader community engagement. This expansion mirrors the developmental trajectory of secure attachment: from the safe haven of a primary relationship to the confident exploration of the wider social world.

## 4. Growth Mindset (Carol Dweck)

### The Theory

Dr. Carol Dweck, a professor of psychology at Stanford University, introduced the concept of **mindset** through decades of research on how people's beliefs about their own abilities affect their motivation, effort, and resilience in the face of challenge.

Dweck identified two fundamental mindsets:

- **Fixed mindset:** The belief that intelligence, ability, and character are static traits -- that you are either smart or you are not, talented or you are not, and that effort is a sign of inadequacy rather than a pathway to growth. Individuals with a fixed mindset tend to avoid challenges, give up easily when they encounter difficulty, see effort as pointless, ignore constructive feedback, and feel threatened by the success of others.

- **Growth mindset:** The belief that intelligence, ability, and character can be developed through effort, effective strategies, and support from others. Individuals with a growth mindset embrace challenges, persist through difficulty, see effort as the path to mastery, learn from feedback, and find inspiration in the success of others.

For children impacted by trauma, the fixed mindset is not merely a cognitive belief but a deeply embodied survival adaptation. When a child has repeatedly experienced failure, helplessness, and the inability to change their circumstances, the conclusion that "nothing I do matters" is not irrational -- it is an accurate description of their lived experience. Asking a trauma-impacted child to "just believe in yourself" without providing concrete, embodied experiences of growth is not growth mindset pedagogy. It is empty optimism.

### Mapping to the 5Rs Framework

Growth Mindset theory informs the **Reflect** and **Reconnect** phases of the 5Rs Framework:

- **Reflect Phase:** The garden provides the concrete, embodied evidence of growth that abstract encouragement cannot. When a child observes that the seed they planted has germinated, that the wilting plant they watered has recovered, or that the garden bed they prepared is now producing food, they are not being told that growth is possible. They are seeing it. The Reflect phase asks children to notice, name, and document these experiences of growth -- in the garden and in themselves.

- **Reconnect Phase:** Growth mindset research emphasizes the role of community in sustaining effort and redefining success. The Reconnect phase creates contexts in which children share their growth stories, celebrate each other's progress, and redefine success as a process rather than a destination. The garden harvest -- the tangible product of sustained collective effort -- becomes a powerful metaphor for what growth mindset looks like in practice.

## 5. Metacognition and Self-Regulated Learning (Barry Zimmerman)

### The Theory

Dr. Barry Zimmerman, a distinguished professor of educational psychology at the City University of New York, developed a comprehensive model of **self-regulated learning (SRL)** that describes how learners actively manage their own cognitive, motivational, and behavioral processes to achieve learning goals.

Zimmerman's model identifies three cyclical phases of self-regulated learning:

1. **Forethought Phase:** The learner sets goals, plans strategies, activates prior knowledge, and engages self-motivational beliefs (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, intrinsic interest). This phase occurs before the learning task begins.

2. **Performance Phase:** The learner engages in the task while monitoring their own progress, applying strategies, maintaining attention, and managing their emotional and motivational states. Self-observation and self-control are central to this phase.

3. **Self-Reflection Phase:** After the task, the learner evaluates their performance, identifies what worked and what did not, attributes outcomes to effort and strategy rather than fixed ability, and adjusts their approach for future learning. This phase generates the self-knowledge that feeds back into the next cycle of forethought.

**Metacognition** -- the ability to think about one's own thinking -- is the engine that drives self-regulated learning. Metacognitive awareness includes knowing what you know and what you do not know, recognizing when you are confused, selecting appropriate strategies, and monitoring whether a strategy is working.

For children impacted by trauma, self-regulated learning is often profoundly disrupted. The executive function impairments caused by toxic stress directly affect the cognitive capacities required for planning, monitoring, and self-reflection. Furthermore, children who have developed a helpless or fixed orientation toward learning may lack the self-efficacy beliefs that fuel the forethought phase.

### Mapping to the 5Rs Framework

Zimmerman's model is the primary theoretical foundation for the **Reflect** phase:

- **Reflect Phase:** The Reflect phase of the 5Rs embeds metacognitive practices into every lesson through garden journaling, self-assessment protocols, thinking-aloud routines, and structured reflection prompts. These practices are scaffolded developmentally: younger children engage in simple observation and naming ("I notice..."), while older students engage in strategy evaluation and causal analysis ("I chose this approach because... and it worked/did not work because...").

- **Root Phase (Forethought):** The Root phase functions as the forethought phase of Zimmerman's cycle, establishing the conditions (safety, regulation, intention-setting) under which effective learning planning can occur.

- **Reconnect Phase (Social Dimensions of SRL):** Zimmerman's later work, along with research by Hadwin and colleagues on socially shared regulation of learning, emphasizes that self-regulation develops in social contexts. The Reconnect phase provides the collaborative learning environment in which children practice co-regulation, shared goal-setting, and collective reflection -- the social scaffolding that supports the internalization of self-regulatory capacities.

## 6. Experiential Learning Theory (David Kolb)

### The Theory

Dr. David Kolb, professor emeritus at Case Western Reserve University, published his influential **Experiential Learning Theory (ELT)** in 1984, drawing on the earlier work of John Dewey, Kurt Lewin, and Jean Piaget. Kolb proposed that learning is a cyclical process involving four stages:

1. **Concrete Experience (CE):** The learner engages directly in an experience -- hands-on, sensory-rich, and immediate. This is the "doing" phase.

2. **Reflective Observation (RO):** The learner steps back from the experience to observe, reflect, and consider what happened from multiple perspectives. This is the "watching and thinking" phase.

3. **Abstract Conceptualization (AC):** The learner draws conclusions, generates theories, and connects the experience to broader principles and concepts. This is the "making sense" phase.

4. **Active Experimentation (AE):** The learner applies the newly formed understanding to a new situation, testing the theory in practice. This is the "trying out" phase.

Kolb argued that effective learning requires movement through all four stages of the cycle. Learners may enter the cycle at any point, but learning is incomplete if any stage is skipped. Different learners may have preferences for different stages (Kolb identified four learning styles based on these preferences), but the goal of education is to develop competence across all four.

Garden-based education is perhaps the most natural context for experiential learning. The garden provides concrete experiences that are genuine rather than simulated, observations that unfold over real time rather than hypothetical scenarios, conceptualizations rooted in tangible phenomena, and opportunities for experimentation with immediate, authentic feedback.

### Mapping to the 5Rs Framework

Kolb's Experiential Learning cycle maps directly onto the 5Rs lesson structure:

| Kolb's Stage | 5Rs Phase | Garden Application |
|-------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Concrete Experience | **Root** and **Regulate** | Sensory engagement with the garden; hands-on planting, harvesting, or tending activities |
| Reflective Observation | **Reflect** | Garden journaling; observation of plant growth; noticing patterns and changes |
| Abstract Conceptualization | **Reflect** and **Restore** | Drawing conclusions about plant science, ecosystems, or one's own learning; connecting garden observations to academic content and self-understanding |
| Active Experimentation | **Reconnect** | Applying learning to new garden projects, community initiatives, or future planning; testing hypotheses in the garden |

The 5Rs Framework adds a critical dimension that Kolb's original model does not explicitly address: the role of nervous system regulation and relational safety as prerequisites for effective experiential learning. A child in a state of sympathetic hyperarousal cannot engage meaningfully in reflective observation. A child in dorsal vagal shutdown cannot participate in active experimentation. By sequencing Root and Regulate before Reflect, the 5Rs Framework ensures that the neurobiological conditions for experiential learning are in place before the cognitive demands of the cycle are introduced.

## 7. Therapeutic Horticulture Research

### The Evidence Base

Therapeutic horticulture (TH) -- the purposeful use of plants and gardening activities to promote health, well-being, and rehabilitation -- has a research base spanning several decades and multiple disciplines, including occupational therapy, psychiatry, rehabilitation medicine, and public health.

Key findings from the therapeutic horticulture literature include:

**Stress Reduction and Cortisol Regulation:**
- Research by van den Berg and Custers (2011), published in the *Journal of Health Psychology*, demonstrated that gardening for thirty minutes significantly reduced cortisol levels compared to indoor reading, with participants also reporting greater positive mood after gardening.
- Soga, Gaston, and Yamaura (2017), in a meta-analysis published in *Preventive Medicine Reports*, found that gardening was associated with significant reductions in depression, anxiety, and stress across diverse populations.

**Cognitive Function and Attention:**
- Studies on attention restoration theory (Kaplan, 1995) provide a theoretical framework for understanding why natural environments, including gardens, restore directed attention after periods of cognitive fatigue. Garden activities that engage "soft fascination" -- the effortless attention drawn by natural stimuli -- allow the neural circuits of directed attention to rest and recover.
- Research with children diagnosed with ADHD has shown that time spent in green outdoor settings is associated with improved concentration and reduced symptom severity (Kuo & Taylor, 2004).

**Social-Emotional Outcomes:**
- Ohly et al. (2016), in a systematic review published in *BMC Public Health*, found that school garden programs were associated with improvements in social skills, self-esteem, attitudes toward school, and willingness to try new foods.
- Research on community gardening (Teig et al., 2009) has documented the role of shared garden spaces in building social cohesion, trust, and collective efficacy -- outcomes that align directly with the Reconnect phase of the 5Rs Framework.

**Trauma-Specific Applications:**
- Emerging research on nature-based interventions for trauma, including studies published in *Global Advances in Health and Medicine* and *Ecopsychology*, has documented reductions in post-traumatic stress symptoms, emotional dysregulation, and behavioral difficulties among participants in structured gardening programs.
- Detweiler et al. (2012) found that therapeutic garden programs for veterans with PTSD were associated with reduced medication use, improved sleep, and decreased agitation.
- While the evidence base for therapeutic horticulture specifically with trauma-impacted children is still developing, the convergence of findings from nature exposure research, stress physiology, sensory integration science, and therapeutic horticulture with adult populations provides a strong theoretical and empirical warrant for the approach taken in this curriculum.

### Mapping to the 5Rs Framework

Therapeutic horticulture research informs the design of garden activities across all five phases of the 5Rs:

- **Root:** Grounding practices using soil texture, plant scent, and garden orientation draw on the sensory integration benefits documented in TH research.
- **Regulate:** Breathing exercises paired with plant observation, rhythmic garden tasks (watering, raking, digging), and mindful sensory engagement draw on the cortisol-reducing effects of garden activity.
- **Reflect:** Garden journaling, phenological observation, and growth documentation draw on the attention-restoration and cognitive benefits of nature engagement.
- **Restore:** Quiet time in the garden, compost-as-metaphor practices, and individual tending activities draw on the emotional regulation benefits of TH.
- **Reconnect:** Shared planting, harvest celebrations, and community garden projects draw on the social cohesion outcomes documented in community gardening research.

## 8. Adverse Childhood Experiences Research (Vincent Felitti and Robert Anda)

### The Foundational Study

The ACEs study, published in 1998 in the *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, is one of the largest and most consequential epidemiological investigations ever conducted. The study examined the relationship between childhood adversity and health outcomes across the lifespan in a sample of over 17,000 adult members of the Kaiser Permanente health plan in San Diego, California.

The study's findings were remarkable in several respects:

1. **Prevalence:** ACEs were found to be far more common than previously recognized, with more than half of respondents reporting at least one ACE and more than one-quarter reporting two or more.

2. **Dose-Response Relationship:** The relationship between ACEs and negative outcomes followed a dose-response pattern: as the number of ACEs increased, the risk for a wide range of health and social problems increased in a graded, stepwise fashion. Adults with four or more ACEs were at dramatically increased risk for heart disease, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, depression, substance use disorders, suicide attempts, intimate partner violence, and early death.

3. **Interrelatedness:** ACEs were found to be highly interrelated. The presence of one ACE significantly increased the likelihood of additional ACEs, reflecting the clustered nature of adversity in households and communities.

4. **Universality and Inequality:** While ACEs were found across all demographic groups, subsequent research has consistently demonstrated that their prevalence and cumulative burden are significantly higher among communities impacted by poverty, racism, and systemic disinvestment.

### Implications for Education

The ACEs research has profound implications for education that extend beyond the original health-focused findings:

- **Cognitive development:** The National Scientific Council on the Developing Child has documented the specific mechanisms by which toxic stress impairs brain architecture, with particular effects on the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala -- structures critical for academic learning.
- **Behavior:** Many of the behavioral challenges that schools find most difficult to manage -- aggression, defiance, withdrawal, emotional volatility, attention difficulties -- are predictable expressions of the neurobiological effects of ACEs.
- **Attendance:** Children with higher ACE scores are more likely to experience chronic absenteeism, mediated by physical illness, mental health challenges, and unstable home environments.
- **Achievement:** ACEs are associated with lower academic achievement, higher rates of grade retention, and reduced likelihood of high school graduation, even after controlling for socioeconomic status.
- **School discipline:** Students with higher ACE exposure are disproportionately represented in exclusionary discipline data, creating a pipeline from childhood adversity to school disengagement.

### Mapping to the 5Rs Framework

The ACEs research is the epidemiological foundation for the entire curriculum. Specifically:

- The prevalence data justify the universal design of the curriculum: because ACEs are so common, trauma-informed practices must be embedded in the core instructional approach for all students, not reserved as interventions for identified individuals.
- The dose-response relationship informs the curriculum's tiered approach: while all students benefit from the 5Rs Framework, students with higher ACE exposure may need additional scaffolding, smaller group sizes, and more intensive co-regulation support.
- The interrelatedness of ACEs underscores the importance of the curriculum's holistic approach: addressing regulation, reflection, restoration, and reconnection simultaneously rather than targeting isolated behaviors.

## Theoretical Integration: How the Frameworks Converge

The theories presented in this chapter are not competing frameworks. They are complementary lenses on the same phenomenon: the developing human being's need for safety, regulation, reflection, repair, and connection. The following table summarizes how each theory contributes to each phase of the 5Rs Framework:

| 5Rs Phase | Polyvagal Theory | Window of Tolerance | Attachment Theory | Growth Mindset | Self-Regulated Learning | Experiential Learning | Therapeutic Horticulture | ACEs Research |
|-----------|-----------------|--------------------|--------------------|---------------|------------------------|-----------------------|--------------------------|---------------|
| **Root** | Cues of safety activate ventral vagal state | Establishes baseline within window | Provides secure base for exploration | Creates conditions for effort and risk | Supports forethought and goal-setting | Initiates concrete experience | Sensory grounding reduces cortisol | Universal design addresses high prevalence |
| **Regulate** | Practices shift from sympathetic/dorsal to ventral vagal | Expands window width; returns to window after dysregulation | Co-regulation models attuned caregiving | Effort reframed as regulation practice | Self-monitoring of arousal and attention | Embodied engagement with learning | Rhythmic garden activity regulates ANS | Addresses neurobiological effects of toxic stress |
| **Reflect** | Reflection possible only in ventral vagal state | Metacognition available within window | Secure base enables cognitive risk-taking | Growth documented and named | Self-evaluation and strategy assessment | Reflective observation and conceptualization | Attention restoration supports cognition | Counters learned helplessness with evidence of growth |
| **Restore** | Supports return to ventral vagal after disruption | Provides pathway back into window | Models attachment repair cycle | Failure reframed as information | Self-reflection generates adaptive learning | Feedback integrated into new understanding | Quiet garden time supports emotional recovery | Addresses cumulative stress through structured recovery |
| **Reconnect** | Social engagement system fully available | Full window width supports relational risk | Expanded relational capacity | Community sustains growth orientation | Socially shared regulation of learning | Active experimentation in social context | Community gardening builds social cohesion | Protective factors buffer against ACE effects |

## Research Evidence Supporting Garden-Based Education Outcomes

### Academic Achievement

Multiple studies have documented the positive effects of garden-based education on academic outcomes:

- Williams and Dixon (2013) conducted a systematic review of 48 studies and found consistent evidence that garden-based learning improves science achievement, with moderate to large effect sizes.
- Berezowitz, Bontrager Yoder, and Schoeller (2015) found that students participating in school garden programs demonstrated higher scores on standardized science assessments compared to matched controls.
- Ray, Fisher, and Fisher-Maltese (2016) documented improvements in both mathematics and science achievement among elementary students engaged in garden-integrated curriculum.

### Social-Emotional Development

- Skelly and Bradley (2007) found that school garden participation was associated with improvements in interpersonal skills, self-understanding, and the ability to work cooperatively.
- Block et al. (2012) documented improvements in self-efficacy, teamwork, and attitudes toward school among middle school students in garden programs.
- Wells, Myers, and Henderson (2014) found that children who participated in school gardening showed greater prosocial behavior and emotional regulation compared to non-participating peers.

### Health and Well-Being

- Davis, Spaniol, and Somerset (2015) found that school garden programs increased children's willingness to taste, preference for, and consumption of fruits and vegetables.
- Utter, Denny, and Dyson (2016) documented associations between adolescent involvement in food preparation (including gardening) and improved mental health and well-being.

### Equity and Access

- Ozer (2007) found that school garden programs had particular benefits for students from low-income backgrounds, students of color, and students with limited prior exposure to nature, suggesting that garden-based education may help narrow equity gaps.
- Lautenschlager and Smith (2007) documented the role of youth gardening programs in building community connections and cultural identity among immigrant and refugee youth.

## Conclusion: A Theoretically Grounded Practice

The *From Garden to Growth* curriculum does not ask educators to implement practices based on intuition or trend. Every element of the 5Rs Framework is grounded in established theory, supported by empirical evidence, and designed to address the specific developmental and neurobiological needs of children impacted by adverse childhood experiences.

The convergence of Polyvagal Theory, the Window of Tolerance, Attachment Theory, Growth Mindset, Self-Regulated Learning, Experiential Learning, Therapeutic Horticulture research, and ACEs research creates a theoretical foundation that is both robust and coherent. These are not disparate ideas bolted together. They are interconnected understandings of human development that, together, point toward the same conclusion: children who have experienced adversity need environments that are safe, regulating, reflective, restorative, and connected -- and the garden provides a uniquely powerful context for creating those environments.

The theoretical rationale presented here is not merely academic. It is a practical guide for implementation. When an educator understands *why* the Root phase begins with sensory grounding, they can adapt the specific activity to their context without losing the underlying principle. When an administrator understands *why* regulation must precede reflection, they can support scheduling decisions that honor the framework's sequence. When a community partner understands *why* the garden matters, they can invest with confidence that their contribution serves a purpose grounded in science.

Theory without practice is inert. Practice without theory is blind. This curriculum is both -- and it is designed to put the best of what we know into the hands of the educators who need it most.
