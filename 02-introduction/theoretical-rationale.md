# Theoretical Rationale

## Converging Research Traditions

The FG2G curriculum is not built on a single theory. It emerges from the convergence of five research traditions that, together, provide a comprehensive framework for understanding why garden-based, trauma-informed education works.

```
    ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
    │       POLYVAGAL THEORY          │
    │   (Safety as prerequisite)      │
    └────────────┬────────────────────┘
                 │
    ┌────────────▼────────────────────┐
    │    WINDOW OF TOLERANCE          │
    │   (Optimal arousal zone)        │
    └────────────┬────────────────────┘
                 │
    ┌────────────▼────────────────────┐     ┌──────────────────────────────┐
    │    GROWTH MINDSET &             │     │  THERAPEUTIC HORTICULTURE    │
    │    METACOGNITION                │◄───►│  (The garden environment)    │
    │   (Cognitive engagement)        │     │                              │
    └────────────┬────────────────────┘     └──────────────────────────────┘
                 │
    ┌────────────▼────────────────────┐
    │    EXPERIENTIAL LEARNING        │
    │   (Learning through doing)      │
    └─────────────────────────────────┘
```

Each tradition addresses a critical dimension of the learning process for students who have experienced adversity:

| Research Tradition | Core Insight | FG2G Application |
|-------------------|-------------|------------------|
| **Polyvagal Theory** (Porges, 2011) | The nervous system must detect safety before social engagement and learning can occur | Root and Regulate phases: grounding, safety assessment, co-regulation |
| **Window of Tolerance** (Siegel, 1999) | Learning happens within a specific arousal zone; outside it, students are in survival mode | Regulation level assessment (0-100 scale); transition rules between phases |
| **Growth Mindset** (Dweck, 2006) | How errors are framed determines whether students persist or withdraw | Restore phase: error analysis, reframing, brain growth language |
| **Metacognition** (Zimmerman, 2002; Flavell, 1979) | Teaching students to observe their own thinking is among the most effective interventions | Reflect phase: TRACE protocol, reasoning moves, self-monitoring |
| **Experiential Learning** (Kolb, 1984) | Deep learning requires concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation | The garden as experiential learning environment; Reconnect phase |

## How the Theories Connect

These are not five independent theories applied separately. They interact in a specific sequence that the 5Rs Framework operationalizes:

### Step 1: Establish Safety (Polyvagal Theory)

Stephen Porges' polyvagal theory identifies three hierarchical states of the autonomic nervous system:

1. **Ventral vagal (social engagement)**: Calm, alert, connected -- the state required for learning.
2. **Sympathetic (fight/flight)**: Mobilized for defense -- no capacity for higher-order thinking.
3. **Dorsal vagal (freeze/collapse)**: Shutdown for survival -- the student appears checked out or dissociated.

Students move through these states based on **neuroception** -- the unconscious detection of safety or danger in the environment. Traditional classrooms often trigger neuroception of danger through unpredictability, social evaluation, and demands that overwhelm the student's capacity.

**FG2G Application**: The Root phase creates environmental conditions that signal safety to the nervous system: predictable routines, warm greetings, sensory grounding, and non-evaluative opening interactions. The garden environment itself -- natural light, living things, open space -- activates the ventral vagal system.

### Step 2: Maintain Optimal Arousal (Window of Tolerance)

Daniel Siegel's window of tolerance model describes the zone of arousal within which a person can function effectively:

- **Above the window (hyperarousal)**: Anxiety, agitation, rapid thinking, impulsivity, aggression.
- **Within the window**: Alert, present, able to think clearly, emotionally regulated.
- **Below the window (hypoarousal)**: Numbness, withdrawal, flat affect, dissociation, apparent apathy.

For students who have experienced chronic adversity, the window of tolerance is often narrower than for their peers. Events that other students absorb easily -- a peer's comment, a difficult problem, a change in routine -- can push them outside their window.

**FG2G Application**: The Regulate phase provides tiered co-regulation strategies calibrated to whether the student is above the window (calming strategies: deep breathing, sensory grounding, garden walking) or below it (activating strategies: physical movement, cold water, strong scents). The 0-100 regulation scale provides a shared language for tracking arousal states.

### Step 3: Engage Cognition (Metacognition and Growth Mindset)

Once a student is within their window of tolerance, cognitive engagement becomes possible. Two research traditions inform how the curriculum structures this engagement:

**Metacognition** (thinking about thinking): Flavell (1979) and Zimmerman (2002) demonstrated that explicit instruction in metacognitive strategies -- monitoring comprehension, planning approaches, evaluating outcomes -- produces significant improvements in academic performance across content areas and grade levels. Students who are taught to observe their own thinking become more effective, independent learners.

**Growth Mindset** (Dweck, 2006): Students who believe intelligence is fixed are more likely to avoid challenges, give up after setbacks, and interpret errors as evidence of inability. Students who believe intelligence is malleable persist longer, embrace challenges, and treat errors as learning opportunities. For trauma-affected students, the fixed mindset often reinforces a shame-based self-concept: *I failed because I am broken.*

**FG2G Application**: The Reflect phase deploys the TRACE protocol (Think, Reason, Articulate, Check, Extend) to structure metacognitive engagement. The Restore phase operationalizes growth mindset by reframing errors as "brain growth moments" and providing systematic error analysis rather than simple correction.

### Step 4: Ground in Experience (Experiential Learning)

Kolb's (1984) experiential learning cycle describes four stages: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. Learning is deepest when all four stages are engaged.

The garden is an ideal experiential learning environment because:

- **Concrete experience**: Students handle real materials, observe real processes, and produce real outcomes.
- **Reflective observation**: Students document growth, compare expectations to results, and identify patterns.
- **Abstract conceptualization**: Students connect observations to academic concepts (photosynthesis, measurement, ecosystem dynamics).
- **Active experimentation**: Students test hypotheses (What happens if we change the soil pH? What if we plant closer together?).

**FG2G Application**: The Reconnect phase ensures that learning transfers beyond the immediate context. Students connect garden-based academic learning to their own lives, communities, and futures -- completing the experiential cycle and establishing relevance.

## The Integrated Framework

The 5Rs Framework is the practical synthesis of these five research traditions:

| 5Rs Phase | Primary Theory | Supporting Theories |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Root** | Polyvagal Theory | Window of Tolerance |
| **Regulate** | Window of Tolerance | Polyvagal Theory |
| **Reflect** | Metacognition | Growth Mindset, Experiential Learning |
| **Restore** | Growth Mindset | Metacognition |
| **Reconnect** | Experiential Learning | All of the above |

This integration means that each phase of the framework has a clear theoretical justification, and transitions between phases follow the logic of the underlying science. We do not ask students to reflect until they are regulated. We do not frame errors as growth opportunities until the student has the metacognitive tools to analyze them. We do not ask for application and transfer until the student has experience to transfer.
