# Introduction: From a Garden to Growth

## A Story That Begins in Soil

The insight that became this curriculum did not arrive in a university lecture hall or during a professional development workshop. It arrived in a garden, on a Tuesday afternoon, with dirt under everyone's fingernails and a child named Marcus kneeling in the soil.

Marcus was eleven years old. He had been suspended three times that semester alone -- once for throwing a chair, once for refusing to leave the hallway, once for what his referral form described as "verbal aggression toward staff." His cumulative file was thick with behavioral incident reports and thin on anything resembling an understanding of who he was. By every metric the school used to sort children, Marcus was a problem to be managed.

On that Tuesday, Marcus's class was in the school garden for what was then an experimental unit -- a loosely structured attempt to integrate science standards with outdoor activity. The lesson plan called for students to transplant seedlings from the greenhouse trays into raised beds. Marcus, predictably, had refused to participate when the class first went outside. He sat on the edge of a raised bed with his hood up, watching.

Twenty minutes in, something shifted. Without being asked, Marcus stood up, walked to the tray of seedlings, and picked one up. He held it for a long time, turning it slowly in his hands, studying the roots. Then he dug a hole -- carefully, methodically, with a focus his teacher said she had never seen from him in six months of classroom instruction. He placed the seedling into the soil, packed the dirt around it gently, and watered it with the kind of deliberate attention that looked, to anyone watching closely, like tenderness.

When his teacher asked him later what he was thinking during that moment, Marcus said: "I was thinking that this little plant doesn't know yet if it's going to make it. It just knows somebody put it somewhere new and it has to try to grow."

That sentence changed everything.

---

## The Moment of Connection

Marcus's words crystallized something that had been forming for years in the work at Community Exceptional Children's Services -- a growing recognition that the conventional tools of education were failing the children who needed them most, and that the answer might not be found in better behavior plans or more rigorous academic interventions but in something far more fundamental: giving children a living context in which to practice the skills of survival, adaptation, and growth.

The connection between Marcus's experience in the garden and the principles of trauma-informed care was immediate and unmistakable. Here was a child whose nervous system was chronically activated by the unpredictability and evaluative pressure of the traditional classroom, and who -- in the sensory-rich, nonjudgmental, purposeful environment of the garden -- had spontaneously moved into a state of calm, focused engagement that no behavioral intervention had been able to produce. Here was a child who had never once, in his teacher's recollection, reflected on his own emotional experience, using a plant as a metaphor to articulate a feeling he did not yet have words for.

The garden had done in twenty minutes what six months of behavior charts, office referrals, and consequence-based systems had not: it had met Marcus where he was, provided sensory regulation without demanding compliance, offered authentic purpose without evaluative pressure, and created a space in which reflection could emerge naturally rather than being forced.

The question that followed was the question that gave birth to this curriculum: *What if this was not an accident? What if the garden could be intentionally designed as a trauma-informed learning environment? What if the principles of Polyvagal Theory, attachment, self-regulation, and experiential learning could be woven into a structured, replicable framework that used the garden as its primary context? What would that look like -- and what would it make possible for children like Marcus?*

## Building the Framework

The development of the 5Rs Framework began not with theory but with observation. Over the following year, staff at Community Exceptional Children's Services conducted informal pilot programs at three school sites, documenting what happened when garden-based activities were introduced with intentional attention to trauma-informed principles. Educators were asked to notice: When did children seem most regulated? When did dysregulation occur? What garden activities seemed to produce the deepest engagement? What conditions made reflection possible? What happened when things went wrong in the garden -- a plant died, a crop failed, a conflict erupted -- and how did children process those experiences?

From these observations, patterns emerged that became the five phases of the framework:

**Root** emerged from the observation that the most effective garden sessions began with a period of sensory grounding -- touching the soil, observing the weather, noticing what had changed since the last visit. Children who began with this grounding practice were visibly calmer and more available for learning than those who were rushed directly into an activity. The Root phase was born from the recognition that safety and presence must be established before anything else can happen.

**Regulate** emerged from the observation that certain garden activities -- particularly rhythmic, repetitive, whole-body tasks like digging, watering, and raking -- had a regulating effect on children's nervous systems that breathing exercises alone could not match. The Regulate phase was designed to harness the sensory and proprioceptive qualities of garden work as tools for nervous system regulation.

**Reflect** emerged from the observation that children who kept garden journals -- drawing, writing, or simply recording observations -- began to develop metacognitive awareness that transferred to academic tasks. The garden provided concrete, observable phenomena (a seed germinating, a leaf changing color, a pest damaging a plant) that made abstract reflective skills tangible and accessible.

**Restore** emerged from the observation that the garden was a uniquely effective space for processing difficulty. When a plant died, children naturally engaged in conversations about loss, care, and what might have gone differently -- conversations that mirrored the restorative practices used in trauma-informed discipline. The compost bin became a powerful metaphor: the idea that what seems like waste or failure can be transformed into something that nourishes future growth.

**Reconnect** emerged from the observation that the harvest -- the moment when children shared the food they had grown with their classmates, families, and community -- produced the most powerful expressions of pride, belonging, and purpose that educators had witnessed. Children who had been isolated and disengaged throughout the year stood taller when they handed a bag of tomatoes to a neighbor. The Reconnect phase was designed to ensure that every learning cycle ended with an experience of contribution, community, and shared meaning.

## The Pilot Programs

Following the initial observation year, Community Exceptional Children's Services launched formal pilot programs at four school sites spanning K--12. The pilots ran for a full academic year and included pre- and post-assessments of social-emotional competencies, academic engagement, disciplinary referrals, and school attendance.

### What We Measured

The pilot programs tracked multiple dimensions of student outcomes:

- **Social-emotional competencies** were assessed using validated instruments measuring self-awareness, self-regulation, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making.
- **Academic engagement** was measured through classroom observation protocols, work completion rates, and teacher ratings of on-task behavior.
- **Disciplinary referrals** were tracked through school information systems, including office referrals, suspensions, and expulsions.
- **School attendance** was monitored for chronic absenteeism patterns.
- **Educator perceptions** were gathered through surveys, interviews, and focus groups conducted at the beginning, middle, and end of the pilot year.
- **Student voice** was captured through structured interviews, garden journal analysis, and student-led focus groups.

### What We Found

The results of the pilot programs exceeded expectations in several areas:

Schools implementing the full 5Rs Framework with garden-based activities reported meaningful reductions in office disciplinary referrals compared to the previous year. The reductions were most pronounced among students with documented histories of adverse childhood experiences -- the population the curriculum was specifically designed to serve.

Teacher surveys indicated substantial improvements in students' ability to identify and communicate their emotional states, use regulation strategies independently, and return to learning after episodes of dysregulation. Multiple educators reported that the 5Rs language -- "I need to root myself," "I'm outside my window," "I need a restore moment" -- had become part of students' everyday vocabulary.

Academic engagement measures showed increases in on-task behavior during garden-based lessons and, importantly, during classroom instruction that followed garden sessions. This transfer effect -- improved regulation and engagement persisting beyond the garden itself -- was one of the most encouraging findings of the pilot.

Attendance patterns improved modestly, with the most notable gains among students who had been chronically absent. Several students and families reported that the garden was a reason to come to school on days when nothing else felt worth the effort.

### What Surprised Us

Several findings from the pilot programs were unexpected:

**Educator transformation.** The most consistent theme in educator feedback was not about student outcomes but about their own experience. Teachers reported that the 5Rs Framework had changed how they understood behavior, how they managed their own stress, and how they related to their most challenging students. Several described the garden as the place where they rediscovered why they had entered education. The curriculum's professional development component was substantially revised based on this finding, with greater emphasis on educator self-regulation and reflective practice.

**The power of failure.** Contrary to the assumption that garden failures (dead plants, pest damage, poor harvests) would be discouraging, students consistently reported that these experiences were among the most meaningful of the year. The garden's failures became opportunities for the Restore phase -- for processing disappointment, examining what went wrong, and making plans to try again. For children accustomed to environments where failure was punished rather than explored, this was transformative.

**Cross-age connection.** At schools where multiple grade bands participated, spontaneous mentoring relationships developed between older and younger students. High school students teaching kindergartners how to plant seeds was not in any lesson plan, but it became one of the most powerful expressions of the Reconnect phase. The curriculum now includes explicit guidance for cross-age garden mentoring based on this observation.

**Family engagement beyond expectations.** Family participation in garden events exceeded projections at every pilot site. Parents and caregivers who had not attended a single school function in years came to harvest celebrations. Several families started gardens at home. The garden, it turned out, was a less threatening point of entry for families who had been alienated by the traditional school environment.

## Voices from the Field

The quantitative data from the pilot programs tell one story. The voices of the people who lived the experience tell another -- one that no data table can capture.

### Educator Voices

> "I have been teaching for fourteen years, and I have never seen anything change a child's trajectory the way the garden has. I had a student who hadn't completed a single assignment all year. The first time she harvested a pepper she grew herself, she came inside and asked me for a pencil. She wanted to write about it. She wanted to write. I almost cried."
> -- Elementary teacher, pilot site

> "The 5Rs gave me a language for what I was already trying to do but didn't know how to name. I used to say 'calm down' fifty times a day. Now I say 'let's root ourselves' and the kids actually know what to do. That's the difference between a philosophy and a practice."
> -- Middle school teacher, pilot site

> "I'll be honest -- I was skeptical. I thought this was going to be another initiative that lasted a year and disappeared. But the garden is still here. The kids won't let it go. They take ownership of it in a way I've never seen with any other school program."
> -- High school teacher, pilot site

### Student Voices

> "The garden is the only place at school where nobody tells me I'm doing it wrong. The plant doesn't care if I have an IEP."
> -- 5th grade student

> "When I'm in the garden, my brain gets quiet. Not in a bad way. In a good way. Like I can actually think."
> -- 7th grade student

> "I didn't think I could grow anything. I didn't think I could do anything that mattered. But I grew tomatoes and I gave them to my grandma and she cooked with them. That was the best day of my whole year."
> -- 3rd grade student

> "In class, when I mess up, I get a referral. In the garden, when something doesn't grow, we figure out why and try again. I wish all of school was like the garden."
> -- 9th grade student

> "My counselor says I have trauma. I don't really like that word. But when I'm in the garden, I feel like whatever it is I have, it's not in charge. I'm in charge."
> -- 11th grade student

## The Vision: Healing-Centered Education That Honors the Whole Child

This curriculum was not written to add another program to an already overburdened school system. It was written to change the fundamental question that schools ask about children who are struggling.

The current question, in too many schools, is: *How do we make this child comply?*

The question this curriculum asks instead is: *What does this child need in order to grow?*

The answer -- grounded in neuroscience, developmental psychology, attachment theory, and the quiet wisdom of the garden -- is that children need safety before they can take risks. They need regulation before they can reflect. They need repair before they can reconnect. They need to feel their own agency before they can contribute to a community. And they need all of this not as an add-on to academic instruction but as the very foundation upon which academic instruction is built.

Healing-centered education, as conceived in this curriculum, is not therapy. It is not the abdication of academic standards. It is not the lowering of expectations. It is the recognition that for children who carry the weight of adverse childhood experiences, learning cannot happen until the conditions for learning have been created -- and that creating those conditions is itself a form of teaching.

The garden is the classroom where these conditions come alive. The soil is the first lesson. The seed is the first act of faith. The harvest is the evidence that effort, patience, and care produce something real.

*From Garden to Growth* is an invitation to every educator, every school, and every community that believes children deserve more than management. They deserve growth. And growth, as any gardener knows, begins with what we put into the soil.

## How This Curriculum Came to Be: A Timeline

**Year 1 -- Observation and Discovery**
Informal garden-based activities introduced at Community Exceptional Children's Services program sites. Staff observe patterns of regulation, engagement, and reflection in garden settings. The connection between garden-based learning and trauma-informed principles is identified.

**Year 2 -- Framework Development**
The 5Rs Framework (Root, Regulate, Reflect, Restore, Reconnect) is articulated based on field observations and theoretical foundations. Initial lesson plans are drafted for pilot testing. Advisory board formed with educators, researchers, families, and community partners.

**Year 3 -- Pilot Implementation**
Formal pilot programs launched at four school sites spanning K--12. Pre- and post-assessments administered. Educator training conducted. Garden infrastructure established. Data collection protocols implemented.

**Year 4 -- Analysis and Revision**
Pilot data analyzed. Educator and student feedback incorporated. Lessons revised based on field experience. Assessment tools refined. Professional development materials expanded. Cross-grade and family engagement components added based on pilot findings.

**Year 5 -- Publication and Dissemination**
Comprehensive curriculum completed and published as the first edition of *From Garden to Growth*. Digital platform (RootWork Learning Hub) launched. Professional development credentialing pathways established. Community partnership models documented. Ongoing research agenda initiated.
