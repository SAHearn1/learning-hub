# Garden Management

## Overview

Effective garden management is essential to sustaining the Living Learning Lab as a functional educational environment. Unlike decorative landscapes, an educational garden must balance horticultural best practices with instructional goals, student accessibility, and trauma-informed design principles. This guide provides educators and garden coordinators with the knowledge to maintain a productive, safe, and pedagogically rich growing environment.

## Roles and Responsibilities

### Garden Coordinator
Each Living Learning Lab should designate a garden coordinator (educator, paraprofessional, or community volunteer) responsible for:

- Overall garden health and maintenance between instructional sessions
- Irrigation system monitoring and adjustment
- Pest and disease scouting
- Seasonal planting schedules
- Supply and tool inventory
- Coordination with maintenance staff and community volunteers

### Educators
Classroom teachers integrate garden tasks into instruction. Their role includes:

- Aligning garden activities with academic standards and 5Rs phases
- Supervising student activities during garden sessions
- Recording student observations and data collection
- Connecting garden phenomena to classroom content

### Students
Students take active ownership of garden responsibilities at developmentally appropriate levels:

- **K-2**: Watering assigned plants, observing and drawing changes, helping with harvest
- **3-5**: Planting, weeding, measuring growth, recording data, composting
- **6-8**: Independent plot management, pest identification, experiment design, data analysis
- **9-12**: Garden planning, mentoring younger students, community outreach, enterprise management

## Soil Management

### Soil Testing
- Conduct comprehensive soil tests annually (spring) through the local cooperative extension service
- Test for pH, nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), organic matter, and lead
- Use soil test results as a science lesson: students interpret data, calculate amendments, and track changes over time

### Soil Amendment
- Add 2-4 inches of finished compost to all beds annually
- Adjust pH using lime (to raise) or sulfur (to lower) based on test results
- Apply organic fertilizers based on crop needs and test recommendations
- Practice no-till or minimal-till methods to preserve soil structure and microbiology

### Cover Cropping
- Plant cover crops (crimson clover, winter rye, field peas) during fallow periods
- Use cover crops to teach nitrogen fixation, soil ecology, and agricultural science
- Terminate cover crops 2-3 weeks before spring planting by cutting and laying as mulch

## Water Management

### Irrigation Systems
- **Drip irrigation**: Preferred for production beds; reduces water waste, prevents foliar disease, and provides consistent moisture
- **Timer-controlled**: Set irrigation for early morning (6-8 AM) to minimize evaporation and fungal issues
- **Backup watering**: Hose bibs and watering cans for supplemental hand-watering and student-led watering activities

### Water Conservation
- Mulch all beds with 2-3 inches of organic mulch (straw, wood chips, or shredded leaves)
- Install rain barrels where legally permitted; use collected water for irrigation
- Monitor soil moisture before watering; teach students to use the "finger test" (insert finger 2 inches into soil)
- Track water usage as a math and environmental science data collection activity

## Planting Guidelines

### Crop Selection Criteria
Select crops based on multiple factors:

1. **Academic alignment**: Choose crops that connect to current curriculum standards
2. **Growing season**: Match crops to your USDA hardiness zone and local frost dates
3. **Student interest**: Include crops students are excited to grow and eat
4. **Cultural relevance**: Represent the cultural backgrounds of students in the garden's plant selection
5. **Harvest timing**: Align harvest with the school calendar (avoid crops that mature only during summer break)
6. **Success rate**: Include reliable, fast-growing crops alongside more challenging varieties

### Recommended Crops by Category

**Fast-growing (harvest in 30-60 days):**
- Radishes, lettuce, arugula, spinach, green onions, bush beans

**Medium-season (harvest in 60-90 days):**
- Tomatoes (transplants), peppers (transplants), cucumbers, squash, herbs (basil, cilantro, dill)

**Long-season and perennial:**
- Sweet potatoes, winter squash, fruit trees, berry bushes, perennial herbs (rosemary, thyme, oregano)

**Sensory and therapeutic:**
- Lavender, lemon balm, mint, chamomile, sunflowers, lamb's ear

### Planting Methods
- **Direct seeding**: Large seeds (beans, squash, sunflowers) and root crops (radishes, carrots)
- **Transplanting**: Tomatoes, peppers, herbs, and flowers started indoors or purchased as seedlings
- **Succession planting**: Plant new rounds of fast-growing crops every 2-3 weeks for continuous harvest
- **Companion planting**: Use beneficial plant combinations (tomatoes + basil, beans + corn + squash) to teach ecological relationships

## Pest and Disease Management

### Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Approach
The garden uses an IPM hierarchy that minimizes chemical inputs:

1. **Prevention**: Healthy soil, proper spacing, crop rotation, resistant varieties
2. **Monitoring**: Regular scouting for pests and diseases; students learn to identify common issues
3. **Mechanical control**: Hand-picking pests, removing diseased leaves, using row covers and barriers
4. **Biological control**: Encourage beneficial insects (ladybugs, lacewings, parasitic wasps) through companion planting and habitat
5. **Organic intervention**: As a last resort, use OMRI-listed organic products applied by trained adults only

### Common Pest Identification
Teach students to identify and respond to:
- Aphids (honeydew, curled leaves) -- spray with water, introduce ladybugs
- Tomato hornworms (large caterpillars, defoliation) -- hand-pick, relocate
- Squash bugs (bronze eggs on leaf undersides) -- remove eggs, trap adults
- Whiteflies (tiny white insects on leaf undersides) -- yellow sticky traps

### Disease Prevention
- Water at the base of plants, not on foliage
- Ensure adequate spacing for air circulation
- Remove and dispose of diseased plant material (do not compost)
- Rotate crop families annually: nightshades, cucurbits, legumes, brassicas

## Composting Program

### Three-Bin System
1. **Bin 1 (Active)**: Currently receiving new materials
2. **Bin 2 (Cooking)**: Full bin decomposing; turn weekly
3. **Bin 3 (Finished)**: Ready to use as soil amendment

### What to Compost
- Garden trimmings, spent plants, weeds (without seeds)
- Fruit and vegetable scraps from the harvest preparation area
- Shredded paper, cardboard, dried leaves
- Coffee grounds from the staff room

### What NOT to Compost
- Meat, dairy, or oily foods
- Diseased plant material
- Pet waste
- Treated or painted wood

### Educational Integration
Composting connects to multiple academic standards:
- **Science**: Decomposition, nutrient cycling, microorganism biology, temperature measurement
- **Math**: Volume calculation, temperature graphing, ratio of greens to browns (1:3)
- **Language Arts**: Process writing, observation journals, scientific reporting

## Tool Management

### Essential Tool Inventory
| Tool | Quantity | Size Options |
|------|----------|-------------|
| Hand trowels | 1 per student | Child and adult sizes |
| Hand cultivators | 10-15 | Child and adult sizes |
| Digging shovels | 4-6 | Short and full handles |
| Garden rakes | 4-6 | Short and full handles |
| Watering cans | 6-10 | 1-2 gallon capacity |
| Pruning shears | 4-6 | Adult use only |
| Wheelbarrow | 1-2 | Standard |
| Harvest baskets | 6-10 | Varied sizes |
| Measuring tools | Class set | Tape measures, rulers |

### Tool Safety and Storage
- All tools stored in a locked, organized area when not in use
- Tools inspected weekly for damage; replace broken or worn items immediately
- Students receive tool safety orientation before first garden session
- Sharp tools (pruning shears, shovels) used under direct adult supervision
- Tool cleanup and return is part of every garden session's closing routine

## Record Keeping

### Garden Log
Maintain a garden log documenting:
- Planting dates, varieties, and locations
- Harvest dates and yields
- Weather observations and irrigation records
- Pest and disease observations and interventions
- Soil test results and amendments applied
- Student data collection and experiments

### Student Data Collection
Students maintain individual or group data sheets recording:
- Plant growth measurements (height, leaf count, fruit count)
- Weather data (temperature, rainfall, sunlight hours)
- Harvest weights and counts
- Experiment observations and results

This data feeds directly into math and science instruction, providing authentic datasets for analysis, graphing, and interpretation.
