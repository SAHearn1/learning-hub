# Facilitator Guide

## Purpose of This Guide

This guide prepares facilitators to lead RootWork professional development sessions with fidelity, safety, and authenticity. Facilitating trauma-informed PD requires a distinct skill set -- one that goes beyond content delivery to encompass emotional attunement, group facilitation, and the ability to model the very practices being taught. The facilitator embodies the 5Rs; participants learn as much from how the session is led as from what is covered.

## Facilitator Qualifications

### Required Qualifications

To facilitate RootWork professional development sessions, an individual must meet the following criteria:

- **RootWork Master Educator Credential** (completion of the full three-year PD trajectory) or equivalent experience approved by the RootWork program director
- **Minimum 3 years of classroom teaching experience** implementing trauma-informed, garden-based practices
- **Completion of the RootWork Facilitator Training** (additional 16-hour training focused on adult learning, group facilitation, and trauma-sensitive PD design)
- **Current personal regulation practice** demonstrated through self-assessment and coach verification
- **Cultural responsiveness training** (minimum 8 hours, within the past 3 years)

### Recommended Qualifications

- Advanced degree in education, counseling, social work, or a related field
- Experience facilitating adult professional development (minimum 40 hours)
- Training in restorative practices, motivational interviewing, or coaching
- Certification in a mindfulness or regulation modality (yoga, MBSR, trauma-sensitive yoga)
- Active involvement in a school or community garden

### Facilitator Dispositions

Beyond qualifications, effective RootWork facilitators demonstrate the following dispositions:

- **Humility:** Willingness to learn alongside participants rather than positioning oneself as the sole expert
- **Vulnerability:** Capacity to share personal experiences with regulation, struggle, and growth
- **Attunement:** Ability to read the emotional temperature of a group and adjust in real time
- **Cultural Humility:** Ongoing commitment to examining one's own biases and centering diverse perspectives
- **Groundedness:** Personal regulation capacity sufficient to hold space for others' distress without becoming dysregulated

## Creating Safe PD Spaces

### The Parallel Process

The single most important principle in RootWork facilitation is the **parallel process**: the PD space must mirror the classroom space we are asking educators to create for students. If we want educators to create psychologically safe, regulation-supportive, culturally affirming classrooms, then we must create psychologically safe, regulation-supportive, culturally affirming PD environments.

This means:

- **Every PD session follows the 5Rs structure.** Participants experience Root, Regulate, Reflect, Restore, and Reconnect as learners before they facilitate these phases for students.
- **Facilitators model regulation.** When facilitators notice their own stress rising, they name it: "I'm noticing I feel a bit activated right now. I'm going to take a grounding breath." This normalization is powerful.
- **Choice is always honored.** Participants may pass on sharing, step out if needed, or choose alternative activities. There is no forced participation.
- **Imperfection is celebrated.** Facilitators make mistakes publicly and model the Restore process: "That didn't go as I planned. Let me try again."

### Physical Environment

- Arrange seating in a circle or small clusters (avoid lecture-style rows)
- Include natural elements: plants, natural light, garden specimens, nature sounds
- Provide sensory tools on tables (fidgets, smooth stones, textured fabric)
- Post the 5Rs framework visually in the room
- Ensure accessibility for all participants (mobility, sensory needs, dietary needs during breaks)
- Maintain a comfortable temperature and adequate ventilation
- Designate a "regulation corner" where participants can step away if needed

### Establishing Group Agreements

At the beginning of every PD cohort, co-create group agreements with participants. Suggested starting points:

1. **Confidentiality:** "What's shared here stays here; what's learned here leaves here."
2. **Brave Space:** We aim for a brave space rather than a safe space -- a place where discomfort is welcomed as a sign of growth, while harm is actively avoided.
3. **Right to Pass:** Everyone has the right to pass on sharing without explanation.
4. **Assume Positive Intent:** We extend good faith to one another while also acknowledging impact.
5. **One Mic:** One person speaks at a time; we listen to understand, not to respond.
6. **Name It to Tame It:** We practice naming our emotions and experiences as part of our regulation work.
7. **Step Up / Step Back:** Those who tend to speak more practice listening; those who tend to stay quiet are invited (not pressured) to share.

## Managing Disclosure

### The Reality of Educator Trauma

Educators are human beings with their own histories. When we teach about trauma, adverse childhood experiences, and dysregulation, some participants will recognize their own stories. This is not a complication -- it is an expected and important part of the work. However, facilitators must be prepared to hold this space skillfully.

### Principles for Managing Disclosure

1. **Normalize without minimizing.** When an educator shares a personal trauma experience, the facilitator's response should communicate: "Thank you for sharing. You are not alone, and your experience matters." Avoid rushing to fix, advise, or redirect.

2. **Maintain appropriate boundaries.** PD sessions are not therapy. The facilitator's role is to create a space where educators can connect their personal understanding to their professional practice, not to process trauma in depth. If deeper processing is needed, the facilitator should offer a private follow-up and connect the educator with appropriate support resources.

3. **Prepare the group.** Before any session that may evoke personal responses (especially Sessions 1, 5, and 6), the facilitator should:
   - Remind participants of group agreements, especially confidentiality and right to pass
   - Explicitly state: "This content may bring up personal memories or feelings. That is a normal response. You are welcome to step out at any time, and support is available."
   - Provide a written list of support resources (EAP, counseling services, crisis hotlines)

4. **Watch for signs of overwhelm.** If a participant becomes visibly distressed (tears, withdrawal, agitation, dissociation), the facilitator should:
   - Offer a brief regulation strategy ("Would you like to take a grounding breath with me?")
   - Provide the option to step out with a co-facilitator or support person
   - Check in privately during the next break
   - Never require the person to "explain" or "process" in front of the group

5. **Debrief with co-facilitators.** After sessions that involve disclosure, facilitators should debrief with their co-facilitator or supervisor to process their own responses and plan any follow-up support.

### Support Resources to Have Available

- Employee Assistance Program (EAP) contact information
- Local counseling and mental health services
- Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741)
- National Suicide Prevention Lifeline (988)
- RootWork program coordinator contact for follow-up support

## Facilitation Techniques

### The 5Rs Facilitation Cycle

Every PD session follows this structure, which should feel seamless rather than formulaic:

1. **Root (Opening):** Ground the group. Use sensory activities, garden-based grounding, or a brief mindfulness exercise to bring participants into the present moment. This is not filler -- it is the foundation.

2. **Regulate (Check-In):** Take the group's emotional temperature. Use the Regulation Thermometer, a brief pair-share, or a body scan. This tells the facilitator what the group needs and models the practice of checking in before pushing forward.

3. **Reflect (Core Learning):** Deliver content through interactive, participatory methods. Minimize lecture; maximize engagement through discussion, hands-on activities, video analysis, and collaborative tasks.

4. **Restore (Working Through Challenges):** Create space for struggle, confusion, resistance, and honest conversation. This is where the real learning often happens. Do not rush this phase.

5. **Reconnect (Application):** Bridge from the PD space to the classroom. Every session ends with concrete action planning and a closing ritual.

### Active Facilitation Strategies

- **Think-Pair-Share:** After presenting new content, give educators 1 minute to think individually, 2 minutes to share with a partner, and then invite a few pairs to share with the whole group.
- **Gallery Walk:** Post charts or artifacts around the room. Educators rotate, read, and add comments or questions using sticky notes. Debrief as a whole group.
- **Fishbowl:** A small group practices a strategy in the center while the rest observe. Structured feedback follows.
- **Jigsaw:** Each small group masters one piece of content and then teaches it to the rest.
- **Case Study Analysis:** Small groups analyze realistic classroom scenarios and propose responses using the 5Rs and TRACE protocol.
- **Video Analysis:** Watch classroom clips and use structured observation tools to identify effective practices and growth areas.
- **Role Play:** Educators practice facilitation moves with peers before using them with students. Always debrief afterward.
- **Reflective Journaling:** Silent, individual writing time is built into every session. Journals are private unless the educator chooses to share.

### Pacing and Energy Management

- Alternate between high-energy and reflective activities
- Build in movement every 30-45 minutes (garden walks, standing discussions, movement breaks)
- Watch the group's energy and adjust: if the room feels flat, add movement; if the room feels charged, add grounding
- Honor break times -- do not cut them short
- End on time; respect educators' personal time

## Handling Resistance

### Understanding Resistance

Resistance in professional development is a normal and often healthy response. It may indicate:

- **Cognitive dissonance:** The new framework challenges deeply held beliefs about teaching, discipline, or student behavior
- **Overwhelm:** The educator feels unable to add one more thing to their plate
- **Past experience:** Previous PD has been unhelpful, top-down, or disconnected from classroom reality
- **Vulnerability avoidance:** The personal nature of trauma-informed work feels threatening
- **Legitimate concern:** The educator identifies a real problem with the approach that deserves engagement

### Response Strategies

1. **Validate before redirecting.** "I hear you saying this feels like a lot. You're right -- this is a significant shift. Let's talk about what feels most manageable to start with."

2. **Seek to understand.** Ask open-ended questions: "What's behind that concern?" or "Help me understand what you're experiencing." Resistance often carries important information.

3. **Offer choice and agency.** Resistance often decreases when educators feel they have control. Offer multiple entry points: "You don't have to implement everything at once. Which piece resonates most with your students right now?"

4. **Use the Restore phase.** The Restore phase of PD is specifically designed for working through challenges. Normalize resistance as part of the learning process, just as we normalize errors for students.

5. **Connect to student impact.** Many educators are moved by concrete evidence of student benefit. Share data, student testimonials, or classroom video that demonstrates the impact of trauma-informed, garden-based practice.

6. **Avoid argumentation.** If a participant is entrenched, further debate will only strengthen resistance. Acknowledge the disagreement, offer to continue the conversation one-on-one, and move forward.

7. **Reflect on your own practice.** Sometimes resistance is a signal that the facilitation needs adjustment. Is the content too theoretical? Is there enough practical application? Are diverse perspectives being honored?

## Supporting Implementation Fidelity

### What Fidelity Means in RootWork

Implementation fidelity does not mean rigid adherence to a script. In the RootWork model, fidelity means:

- Consistent use of the 5Rs structure as an organizing framework for instruction
- Regular integration of garden-based learning activities aligned to content standards
- Use of the TRACE protocol to scaffold student thinking
- Trauma-informed classroom practices that prioritize regulation over compliance
- Ongoing use of the RootGuide platform for data-informed instruction

### Fidelity Indicators

| Domain | High Fidelity | Developing | Low Fidelity |
|--------|--------------|------------|--------------|
| 5Rs Structure | All 5 phases visible in most lessons; responsive transitions | 3-4 phases present; transitions sometimes abrupt | 1-2 phases or none; framework not evident |
| Garden Integration | Weekly garden activities aligned to standards | Monthly garden activities; alignment inconsistent | Rare or no garden activities |
| TRACE Protocol | All 5 moves facilitated regularly; reasoning moves named | 2-3 moves used; reasoning moves not explicitly named | TRACE not observed |
| Regulation Practice | Multiple strategies available; co-regulation to self-regulation continuum | Some strategies used; primarily educator-directed | Punitive or compliance-based responses to dysregulation |
| RootGuide Use | Regular data review; platform used for instruction and assessment | Occasional platform use; data reviewed infrequently | Platform not used |

### Supporting Fidelity Through Coaching

Coaches use a non-evaluative, inquiry-based approach to support implementation fidelity:

1. **Observe without judgment.** Collect data on what is happening, not what is missing.
2. **Ask before telling.** "What did you notice about the Regulate phase today?" is more effective than "You forgot the Regulate phase."
3. **Celebrate progress.** Growth is the goal, not perfection.
4. **Co-plan next steps.** The educator and coach collaboratively identify one area for focused improvement.
5. **Follow up.** Return to observe the focused area and provide specific feedback.

## Coaching Protocols

### Pre-Observation Protocol (30 minutes)

1. **Check in:** How is the educator feeling about the upcoming observation? Any specific stressors or successes to note?
2. **Review the lesson plan:** Walk through the 5Rs structure, TRACE integration, and garden connections.
3. **Identify a focus area:** What does the educator want feedback on? This becomes the primary lens for observation.
4. **Agree on data collection:** What specific evidence will the coach collect (scripting, tallying, timing, student response tracking)?

### Observation Protocol (45-60 minutes)

The RootWork Observation Protocol guides data collection across five domains:

- **5Rs Facilitation:** Phase transitions, timing, responsiveness to student cues
- **TRACE Implementation:** Questioning stems used, reasoning moves elicited, student discourse quality
- **Garden Integration:** Connections to garden activities, use of garden metaphors, standards alignment
- **Regulation Practices:** Co-regulation strategies, student self-regulation support, environmental design
- **Student Engagement:** Participation patterns, emotional tone, evidence of learning

Coaches record objective data (direct quotes, timestamps, tallies) rather than evaluative judgments.

### Post-Observation Debrief (45 minutes)

1. **Ground and regulate (5 minutes):** Brief grounding exercise to enter the debrief in a regulated state.
2. **Educator reflection (10 minutes):** "How do you feel the lesson went? What worked? What would you change?" The educator speaks first, always.
3. **Share positive evidence (10 minutes):** Coach shares specific, evidence-based observations of effective practice. Use direct quotes from the educator and students.
4. **Collaborative inquiry (15 minutes):** Focus on the agreed-upon area. Coach shares relevant data and asks inquiry questions: "I noticed X. What was your thinking there?" "What might happen if you tried Y?"
5. **Co-plan next steps (5 minutes):** Identify one specific action step for the educator to try before the next observation. Keep it manageable.

## Observation and Feedback Frameworks

### The "Glow and Grow" Model

Every piece of feedback includes:

- **Glow:** A specific strength observed, with evidence. "When Marcus became dysregulated, you moved beside him and offered the breathing card without any verbal correction. He was back in his window within 2 minutes. That's the Regulate phase in action."
- **Grow:** A specific area for development, framed as a question or invitation. "I noticed the TRACE sequence moved quickly from Think to Articulate. What might happen if you gave students 30 more seconds of silent Think time before inviting them to share?"

### Feedback Sentence Stems

- "I noticed that when you... students responded by..."
- "One thing I saw that aligned with the 5Rs was..."
- "I'm curious about your decision to... What was your thinking?"
- "What if you tried... and see how students respond?"
- "The strongest moment I observed was... because..."

### Calibration

All coaches participate in biannual calibration sessions where they observe the same classroom video and compare their observation notes and feedback. This ensures consistency across the coaching team and prevents drift in observation standards.

## Facilitator Self-Care

Facilitating trauma-informed PD is emotionally demanding work. Facilitators must attend to their own regulation and well-being:

- **Pre-session grounding:** Arrive 30 minutes early to set up the space and complete a personal grounding practice.
- **Co-facilitation:** Whenever possible, facilitate with a partner so that one person can hold space while the other takes a regulation break.
- **Post-session debrief:** After every session, debrief with a co-facilitator, coach, or supervisor. Process what came up, what went well, and what was challenging.
- **Ongoing supervision:** Meet monthly with a clinical supervisor or experienced mentor to process the cumulative impact of holding space for educator trauma.
- **Personal practice:** Maintain a daily regulation practice (mindfulness, movement, journaling, time in the garden).
- **Boundaries:** It is appropriate and necessary to set limits on between-session contact with participants. Refer to appropriate support resources when needs exceed the facilitator's scope.

The garden teaches us that growth requires tending, but the gardener must also rest. Facilitators who neglect their own root systems cannot sustain others.
