# FG2G Facilitator Guide

## The Role of the FG2G Facilitator

The FG2G facilitator is not a traditional teacher delivering content from a script. Rather, the facilitator is a gardener of learning — preparing the soil (environment), planting seeds (introducing concepts), providing nutrients and water (support and scaffolding), pruning when necessary (redirecting), and trusting the growth process (student-led discovery). This guide provides the principles, techniques, and practical wisdom needed to fulfill this role effectively.

> *"The best facilitator, like the best gardener, creates the conditions for growth and then has the wisdom to step back and let nature do its work."*

## Core Facilitation Principles

### 1. Safety Before Content

No learning occurs without neurobiological safety. Before any academic engagement, the facilitator ensures:

- **Physical safety** — The environment is predictable, sensory-friendly, and free from threat
- **Relational safety** — The facilitator is a regulated, non-anxious presence
- **Psychological safety** — Errors are normalized; vulnerability is honored; no student is put on the spot
- **Cultural safety** — All identities, backgrounds, and experiences are respected and represented

### 2. Regulation Before Instruction

Monitor student regulation levels before, during, and after each activity:

| Regulation Level | Facilitator Response |
|---|---|
| 80-100 (Thriving) | Full engagement with challenging content; encourage peer leadership |
| 60-79 (Regulated) | Standard facilitation; maintain pace and engagement |
| 40-59 (Coping) | Slow pace; increase scaffolding; offer choice; check in frequently |
| 20-39 (Struggling) | Simplify tasks; prioritize co-regulation; reduce demands; offer sensory supports |
| 0-19 (Dysregulated) | Redirect to Root phase; prioritize safety; avoid academic demands entirely |

### 4. Garden as Co-Facilitator

The garden is not a prop — it is an active teaching partner. The facilitator learns to read the garden's cues and use them responsively:

- A wilting plant becomes a real-time lesson in stress and recovery
- An unexpected pest provides an authentic problem-solving opportunity
- A bumper harvest creates a natural occasion for celebration and generosity
- A crop failure offers a genuine experience of productive struggle

## Facilitation Techniques by 5Rs Phase

### Root Phase Facilitation

**Goal:** Establish safety and grounding before any academic work begins.

**Techniques:**

| Technique | Description | When to Use |
|---|---|---|
| Threshold greeting | Meet each student at the garden/classroom entrance with a calm, warm presence | Every session opening |
| Sensory grounding | Guide students through a 5-4-3-2-1 sensory activity using garden elements | Transitions, after disruptions |
| Regulation check-in | Use the visual scale (0-100) to assess group and individual regulation | Before academic activities |
| Anchor rituals | Consistent, predictable routines that signal safety | Opening and closing of each session |
| Environmental scan | Quietly observe and adjust environmental factors (noise, temperature, seating) | Ongoing throughout session |

**Language Patterns:**
- "Before we begin, let's take a moment to notice where we are..."
- "Find something in the garden that feels calm to you right now..."
- "Your only job right now is to be here. That's enough."
- "Let's check in with ourselves — where are you on the scale today?"

### Regulate Phase Facilitation

**Goal:** Support students in achieving and maintaining optimal arousal for learning.

**Techniques:**

| Technique | Description | When to Use |
|---|---|---|
| Co-regulation modeling | Visibly demonstrate your own regulation strategies | When students show dysregulation cues |
| Movement integration | Incorporate garden-based physical activity (digging, watering, walking) | When energy is too high or too low |
| Choice architecture | Offer structured choices that give students agency | When students resist engagement |
| Strategic pairing | Partner students based on complementary regulation patterns | Group work activities |
| Pace adjustment | Speed up or slow down based on group energy | Throughout instruction |

**Language Patterns:**
- "I'm noticing my own energy is a bit high right now. I'm going to take three slow breaths."
- "It seems like our group could use some movement. Let's water the garden beds."
- "Would you prefer to work on the plant observations or the soil testing first?"
- "You're working hard to stay with this. I see that effort."

### Reflect Phase Facilitation

**Goal:** Guide critical thinking and inquiry without taking over the reasoning process.

**TRACE Protocol Facilitation Guide:**

| TRACE Step | Facilitator Moves | Common Mistakes to Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Think** | Ask open-ended questions; wait at least 7 seconds for responses | Answering your own questions; rushing silence |
| **Reason** | Prompt for evidence; ask "How do you know?" | Accepting surface-level answers; providing hints too quickly |
| **Articulate** | Encourage students to explain to peers, not just to you | Rephrasing student thinking too much; correcting language prematurely |
| **Check** | Ask "Does that make sense?" and "How could we verify?" | Skipping verification; treating first answers as final |
| **Extend** | Connect to other contexts: "Where else have we seen this?" | Forcing connections that aren't natural; over-extending |

**Seven Reasoning Moves — Facilitation Prompts:**

| Reasoning Move | Facilitator Prompt Examples |
|---|---|
| DECOMPOSE | "What are all the parts of this problem?" / "Let's break this down..." |
| COMPARE | "How is this similar to...?" / "What's different about these two?" |
| QUESTION | "What would you ask if you could ask any question about this?" |
| VERIFY | "How could we test that idea?" / "What evidence supports this?" |
| GENERALIZE | "If this is true here, where else might it be true?" |
| TRANSFORM | "What if we looked at this from a completely different angle?" |
| CONNECT | "How does this relate to what we learned about...?" |

### Restore Phase Facilitation

**Goal:** Guide productive error analysis without triggering shame.

**Key Principle:** The facilitator's emotional response to student errors sets the tone for the entire learning community. Demonstrate genuine curiosity about errors rather than disappointment.

**Techniques:**

| Technique | Description | Language Model |
|---|---|---|
| Error celebration | Respond to errors with interest and appreciation | "Oh, that's a really interesting mistake — let's explore it" |
| Diagnostic questioning | Guide students to identify their own error types | "Walk me through your thinking — where did it start to feel uncertain?" |
| Garden parallel | Connect errors to garden experiences | "Remember when our tomatoes got blight? How did we figure out what happened?" |
| Normalization | Share expert examples of productive failure | "Scientists publish papers about their failed experiments because..." |
| Growth documentation | Help students track their error patterns and improvements | "Let's add this to your growth journal — what did you learn?" |

### Reconnect Phase Facilitation

**Goal:** Support students in connecting learning to community and purpose.

**Techniques:**

| Technique | Description | When to Use |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose bridging | Help students connect activities to meaningful goals | End of units; enterprise project planning |
| Community linking | Facilitate connections between classroom learning and community needs | Partnership activities; service projects |
| Peer mentoring | Support older/more experienced students in guiding others | Cross-grade activities; leadership development |
| Celebration rituals | Create meaningful moments of recognition and connection | End of cycles; harvest celebrations; milestones |
| Legacy building | Help students see their contributions as lasting | Documentation; transition planning; garden plantings |

## Managing Common Facilitation Challenges

### When a Student Becomes Dysregulated During Instruction

```
NOTICE → PAUSE → CO-REGULATE → REDIRECT → RESUME
  │        │         │              │          │
  │        │         │              │          └─ Return to lesson
  │        │         │              │             when student and
  │        │         │              │             group are ready
  │        │         │              │
  │        │         │              └─ Offer Root phase activity
  │        │         │                 (garden grounding, sensory)
  │        │         │
  │        │         └─ Calm voice, slow pace, proximity
  │        │            without pressure
  │        │
  │        └─ Stop instruction briefly; signal safety
  │
  └─ Observe behavioral/physiological cues
```

### When the Group Energy Doesn't Match the Activity

| Mismatch | Response |
|---|---|
| High energy + need for focus | Garden movement break first, then return to focused task |
| Low energy + need for engagement | Begin with hands-on garden activity to activate sensory systems |
| Mixed energy levels | Offer parallel activities at different intensity levels |
| Post-recess dysregulation | Extended Root phase with garden grounding before any academic work |

### When You (the Facilitator) Are Dysregulated

Your own regulation is the most important variable in the room. When you notice your own dysregulation:

1. **Acknowledge internally** — "I notice I'm activated right now"
2. **Buy time** — "Let me take a moment to think about that" or direct students to a brief independent task
3. **Use your strategies** — Practice the same regulation techniques you teach students
4. **Be transparent when appropriate** — "I'm feeling a bit scattered today. Let me regroup."
5. **Seek support** — Tag-team with a co-educator; step into the garden for a brief reset

## Facilitation Self-Assessment Checklist

Use this checklist weekly to reflect on your facilitation practice:

| Domain | Reflection Question | Rating (1-5) |
|---|---|---|
| Safety | Did every student appear to feel safe in today's session? | |
| Regulation | Did I accurately assess and respond to regulation levels? | |
| Dual purpose | Did my activities serve both academic and SEL goals? | |
| TRACE | Did I facilitate inquiry rather than deliver answers? | |
| Error response | Did I respond to errors with curiosity rather than correction? | |
| Garden integration | Did the garden serve as an active learning partner today? | |
| Co-regulation | Did I maintain my own regulation throughout the session? | |
| Student voice | Did students do more talking and thinking than I did? | |
| Differentiation | Did I adapt for diverse regulation levels and learning needs? | |
| Connection | Did the session end with students feeling connected and purposeful? | |

## The Facilitator's Garden Metaphor

Remember: You are not the sun that makes the garden grow. You are the gardener who prepares the soil, provides the water, and removes the weeds. The growth belongs to the plants — and to the students. Your greatest skill is knowing when to tend and when to trust.
