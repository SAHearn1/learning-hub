# Regulate Phase

## Co-Regulation & Stabilization

The Regulate phase activates whenever a student moves outside their window of tolerance -- whether into hyperarousal (anxiety, agitation, fight/flight responses) or hypoarousal (shutdown, withdrawal, dissociation). This phase is not a punishment or a disruption to learning; it is a critical component of the learning process itself.

The Regulate phase draws on Dan Siegel's window of tolerance model and Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory to provide structured co-regulation strategies that help students return to a state of calm alertness where cognitive engagement becomes possible again.

## Purpose

1. **Detect dysregulation** -- Identify when a student has left their window of tolerance through behavioral signals.
2. **Co-regulate** -- Provide relational and environmental supports that help the student's nervous system settle.
3. **Restore readiness** -- Guide the student back to a regulation level where academic engagement is possible.
4. **Build self-regulation capacity** -- Over time, teach students to recognize and manage their own regulation states.

## Dysregulation Signals

Educators and the RootGuide AI tutor monitor for the following signals:

### Hyperarousal Indicators
- Rapid, short responses or talking over others
- Increased errors after a period of success
- Physical agitation (tapping, bouncing, pacing)
- Aggressive language or punctuation (ALL CAPS, exclamation marks)
- Argumentative or oppositional behavior
- Inability to sit still or focus

### Hypoarousal Indicators
- Long pauses or non-responsiveness
- Flat affect or monotone speech
- "I don't know" or "I don't care" to every question
- Head on desk, avoidance of eye contact
- Withdrawal from social interaction
- Dissociative behaviors (staring, seeming "checked out")

### Masked Dysregulation
Some students mask their dysregulation with coping behaviors that appear adaptive:
- Perfectionism and excessive erasing/rewriting
- People-pleasing and agreeing without understanding
- Humor deflection to avoid the task
- Off-topic conversations to steer away from challenging content

## Co-Regulation Strategies

### Tier 1: In-Context Supports (No Phase Transition Required)
These light-touch interventions can be offered within the current learning activity:

- **Acknowledgment**: "This is challenging work. That's okay."
- **Normalization**: "Lots of students find this tricky the first time."
- **Reframe**: "Your brain is building new connections right now -- that's what the struggle feels like."
- **Pace adjustment**: Slow down, simplify the current question, or offer a brief pause.

### Tier 2: Active Regulation Interventions
When Tier 1 supports are insufficient (regulation level drops below 40):

- **Breathing exercises**: "Let's take three deep breaths together. In through the nose like you're smelling a flower, out through the mouth like you're blowing a dandelion."
- **Movement break**: Walk to the garden, water a plant, stretch, or do gentle bilateral movements.
- **Sensory engagement**: Hold a smooth stone, smell rosemary or lavender from the garden, squeeze a stress ball.
- **Regulation Pod visit**: Transition to the Rootwork Regulation Pod for a more structured calming environment (see Rootwork Regulation Pods documentation).

### Tier 3: Calm Corner / Extended Regulation
When a student requires more than a brief intervention:

- Transition to the designated calm corner or regulation space
- Student selects from a menu of calming activities (drawing, listening to music, guided meditation, garden time)
- No academic demands during this period
- Educator checks in periodically without pressure
- Student signals when ready to return

## The Regulate Phase in the AI Tutor

The RootGuide AI tutor monitors for dysregulation signals throughout every interaction. When detected:

1. The system adjusts the regulation level score downward.
2. If the score drops below 40, the AI transitions to the Regulate phase.
3. The AI offers regulation supports in a warm, non-judgmental tone.
4. Academic content pauses until the student indicates readiness to continue.
5. After three or more regulation interventions in a single session, the system flags the session for educator review.

**AI Regulate phase language examples:**
- "I notice this might be getting frustrating. Want to take a breather?"
- "Let's pause for a moment. Take a deep breath with me."
- "Want to visit your calm corner for a minute? The learning will be here when you're ready."
- "It's completely okay to need a break. Your brain works best when you feel settled."

## Building Self-Regulation Skills

The long-term goal of the Regulate phase is to shift from co-regulation (adult-supported) to self-regulation (student-directed). This progression follows a developmental trajectory:

### Stage 1: Co-Regulation (Educator-Led)
The educator identifies dysregulation and initiates regulation strategies. The student learns to associate specific interventions with feeling better.

### Stage 2: Prompted Self-Regulation
The educator helps the student notice their own dysregulation: "What's happening in your body right now?" The student begins to choose from offered strategies.

### Stage 3: Independent Self-Regulation
The student recognizes their own dysregulation signals and initiates self-regulation strategies independently: "I need a minute in the garden" or "Can I do some breathing first?"

### Stage 4: Proactive Regulation
The student anticipates challenging situations and pre-emptively engages regulation strategies before dysregulation occurs.

## Garden-Based Regulation

The garden provides unique regulation opportunities:

- **Rhythmic activities**: Raking, digging, watering -- repetitive motor activities that naturally regulate the nervous system.
- **Sensory engagement**: Textures of soil and leaves, scents of herbs, colors of flowers, sounds of nature.
- **Nurturing activities**: Caring for plants creates a sense of purpose and competence.
- **Physical exertion**: Turning compost, hauling mulch, or building raised beds channels excess energy productively.
- **Quiet observation**: Watching insects, tracing leaf patterns, or sitting in a garden quiet space.

## Transition Out of Regulate

The student transitions back to the Root phase for a fresh grounding check when:

- Regulation level returns above 60
- Student verbally or physically indicates readiness
- Breathing and heart rate have visibly settled
- Student can engage in brief conversation without distress signals

The transition is always student-paced and never forced: "Whenever you're ready, we can check back in and see how you're feeling."

## Documentation

Educators document regulation events to identify patterns:

- Time of day when dysregulation occurs most frequently
- Subjects or activities that trigger dysregulation
- Strategies that are most effective for each student
- Frequency and duration of Regulate phase episodes over time
- Progress along the self-regulation development continuum

This data informs IEP accommodations, classroom modifications, and parent communication.
