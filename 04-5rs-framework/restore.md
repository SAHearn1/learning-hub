# Restore Phase

## Error Recovery & Growth

The Restore phase transforms the experience of making errors from a source of shame into an engine for growth. In traditional educational settings, errors are often met with correction, red marks, or diminished grades -- responses that can trigger a trauma response in students who have experienced adverse childhood experiences. The Restore phase deliberately reframes errors as neurological events: moments when the brain forms new synaptic connections.

This phase operationalizes Carol Dweck's growth mindset research within a trauma-informed context, ensuring that error recovery strengthens both cognitive understanding and emotional resilience.

## Purpose

1. **Normalize error** -- Establish that mistakes are an expected and valuable part of learning, not a reflection of the student's worth or intelligence.
2. **Analyze error** -- Guide the student to identify what went wrong and why, developing diagnostic reasoning skills.
3. **Reframe error** -- Connect the mistake to brain science: "Your brain just grew a new synapse! Let's look at what happened."
4. **Build from error** -- Use the error as a stepping stone to deeper understanding, not just correction.

## Core Principles

### Never Just Say "Wrong"
Every error receives analysis, not just correction. The goal is to understand the reasoning that led to the error, not simply to replace the wrong answer with the right one.

### Separate the Person from the Error
Language matters. Errors belong to the work, not the student:
- **Avoid**: "You made a mistake" or "That's wrong"
- **Use**: "There's something interesting happening in this step" or "Let's look at where the path diverged"

### Errors Are Data
Each error provides diagnostic information about the student's understanding. A computational error reveals different needs than a conceptual misunderstanding. The Restore phase uses error analysis to inform subsequent instruction.

### Maintain Emotional Safety
For students with trauma histories, errors can trigger disproportionate shame or fear responses. The Restore phase must be emotionally safe:
- Calm, curious tone
- No time pressure
- No comparison to other students
- Explicit reassurance that errors are expected

## Restore Phase Process

### Step 1: Acknowledge and Reframe
When an error is detected during the Reflect phase, the educator transitions to Restore with a positive reframe:

- "Your brain just built a new connection! Let's explore what happened."
- "Interesting -- this is exactly the kind of mistake that leads to deeper understanding."
- "I'm glad this happened, because now we get to learn something really important."

### Step 2: Locate the Divergence Point
Guide the student to find where their reasoning departed from a valid path:

- "Walk me through your steps. Where does it start to feel uncertain?"
- "Everything looks good up to here. What happened at this step?"
- "Let's go back to the last point where you felt confident."

### Step 3: Analyze the Error Type
Help the student categorize their error:

| Error Type | Description | Example |
|------------|-------------|---------|
| Computational | Correct strategy, arithmetic mistake | 7 x 8 = 54 |
| Procedural | Steps performed in wrong order or incomplete | Forgot to carry in multi-digit addition |
| Conceptual | Fundamental misunderstanding of the concept | Thinking multiplication always makes bigger |
| Strategic | Used wrong approach entirely | Adding when should multiply |
| Reading/Interpretation | Misread the problem or data | Confused "more than" with "less than" |

### Step 4: Self-Correction
Whenever possible, guide the student to correct the error themselves rather than providing the correction:

- "Now that you see where it went off track, can you fix it?"
- "What would happen if you tried a different approach at that step?"
- "Use your VERIFY reasoning move -- check it another way."

### Step 5: Consolidation
After correction, help the student extract a lesson from the error:

- "What would you tell another student to watch out for?"
- "If you saw this problem again, what would you do differently?"
- "Let's add this to your 'growth notes' -- what did your brain learn today?"

## Error Analysis as a Skill

The Restore phase does not only activate when students make their own errors. It is also used proactively as a learning strategy:

### Presented Error Analysis
The educator presents work with deliberate errors (attributed to a fictional student) and asks the learner to find, explain, and fix them:

- "Maya solved this problem. She got 36, but the correct answer is 42. Can you figure out what happened?"
- "Look at this garden measurement data. Something doesn't add up. Can you find the error?"

This approach develops critical analysis skills in a low-stakes context -- the student is analyzing someone else's error, which reduces the emotional charge of making mistakes.

### Error Journals
Students maintain a section in their growth journal for "Brain Growth Moments" where they document:
- The error they made
- What they learned from it
- How they would approach the problem differently
- A similar problem they created and solved correctly

## Garden Connection

The Restore phase has a natural garden metaphor: pruning and composting.

- **Pruning**: Cutting back a plant's dead or overgrown branches helps it redirect energy toward healthy growth. Similarly, identifying and removing errors in thinking allows the student to redirect cognitive energy.
- **Composting**: Garden waste doesn't disappear -- it breaks down and becomes nutrient-rich soil for future growth. Similarly, errors don't vanish -- they decompose into the understanding that feeds future learning.

Students can engage in actual pruning and composting activities as a physical parallel to the cognitive process of error recovery.

## The Restore Phase in the AI Tutor

When the RootGuide AI tutor detects an error during the Reflect phase:

1. The AI never says "wrong" or "incorrect" as a first response.
2. It asks the student to explain their reasoning: "Walk me through how you got that."
3. It identifies whether the error is computational, procedural, conceptual, strategic, or interpretive.
4. It provides a reframe: "Your brain just grew! Let's look at what happened."
5. It guides self-correction through targeted questions rather than providing the answer.
6. After correction, it offers a consolidation prompt: "What did you learn from that?"

## Transition Out of Restore

After the error has been analyzed, corrected, and consolidated, the student returns to the Reflect phase to continue learning. The transition is forward-looking:

- "Great work figuring that out. Ready to keep going?"
- "You just leveled up your understanding. Let's try another one."
- "That brain growth is going to help with the next problem. Let's see."

If the error analysis reveals a deeper conceptual gap, the educator may adjust the difficulty level or approach before continuing in Reflect. If the error process triggers dysregulation, the session transitions to Regulate.
