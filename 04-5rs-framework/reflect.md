# Reflect Phase

## Active Learning & Metacognition

The Reflect phase is where core academic learning takes place. Once a student has been grounded (Root) and is within their window of tolerance, the Reflect phase engages them in rigorous, standards-aligned instruction that develops synthesis-level thinking through the TRACE protocol and explicit reasoning moves.

This phase is not about passive receipt of information. The Reflect phase demands that students think about their thinking, articulate their reasoning, and engage with content at the highest levels of Bloom's taxonomy.

## Purpose

1. **Engage in standards-aligned academic content** -- Mathematics, Science, and Language Arts instruction connected to Georgia Standards of Excellence, Common Core, and NGSS.
2. **Develop metacognitive awareness** -- Teach students to observe, name, and direct their own thinking processes.
3. **Build reasoning capacity** -- Explicitly develop the seven reasoning moves (Decompose, Compare, Question, Verify, Generalize, Transform, Connect).
4. **Maintain trauma-informed practice** -- Ensure that academic rigor never comes at the cost of emotional safety.

## The TRACE Protocol

All learning interactions during the Reflect phase follow the TRACE protocol:

### Think
"What do you notice first?"

The educator or AI tutor presents a problem, text, or phenomenon and asks the student to observe before acting. This activates prior knowledge and develops careful observation habits.

- Present the stimulus without immediately asking for an answer
- Allow processing time (especially important for students with extended time accommodations)
- Encourage multiple observations: "What else do you notice?"

### Reason
"What's your plan of attack?"

Before solving, the student articulates a strategy. This develops executive function and strategic thinking.

- Ask for the approach before the answer
- Accept and explore multiple valid strategies
- Model strategy selection: "I might DECOMPOSE this into smaller parts first. What would you do?"

### Articulate
"Talk me through your thinking."

The student explains their reasoning process in their own words. This is the most critical step -- understanding is demonstrated through explanation, not just correct answers.

- Require verbal or written explanation for every response
- Never accept "I just knew" as sufficient reasoning
- Use prompts: "Can you explain that as if you were teaching it to a friend?"
- For correct answers, still probe: "How did you know to do that?"

### Check
"How do you know you're right?"

The student verifies their own work using an alternative method or logical reasoning. This builds self-verification habits and mathematical proof thinking.

- "Can you check that a different way?"
- "Does your answer make sense in the context of the problem?"
- "What would happen if you plugged your answer back in?"
- Teach estimation as a checking tool

### Extend
"What else could you do with this?"

After solving, the student considers how the concept applies more broadly. This promotes transfer and generalization.

- "What if we changed this number? How would your approach change?"
- "Where might you see this in the garden?"
- "Can you make up a harder version of this problem?"
- "How does this connect to what we learned last week?"

## Engagement Modes

The Reflect phase uses five engagement modes to develop different cognitive skills:

### Forward Solving
Traditional problem presentation where students solve step-by-step with scaffolding. The educator guides with questions rather than direct instruction.

### Reverse Engineering
The answer is provided first; students must reconstruct the reasoning. "The answer is 42. How would someone arrive at this answer?" This develops logical reasoning and process understanding.

### Error Analysis
Students receive work containing deliberate errors (attributed to a fictional student) and must identify, explain, and correct the mistakes. "Alex solved this problem but made an error somewhere. Can you find it and explain what went wrong?"

### Multiple Pathways
After solving a problem one way, students must find an alternative approach. "That's one valid method. Can you think of a completely different way to get to the same answer?" This builds cognitive flexibility.

### Problem Posing
Students create their own problems based on the current concept. "Design a problem that uses the same idea but is slightly harder." This requires deep conceptual understanding and develops mathematical creativity.

## Reasoning Moves in Practice

Educators explicitly name and model reasoning moves during the Reflect phase:

| Reasoning Move | Educator Model | Student Prompt |
|----------------|----------------|----------------|
| DECOMPOSE | "Let me break this into smaller pieces..." | "Can you DECOMPOSE this problem?" |
| COMPARE | "This reminds me of... let me compare..." | "What's similar and different here?" |
| QUESTION | "I wonder what would happen if..." | "What assumptions are we making?" |
| VERIFY | "Let me check this another way..." | "Can you VERIFY that?" |
| GENERALIZE | "I see a pattern forming..." | "What rule can you write?" |
| TRANSFORM | "What if we look at this differently..." | "Can you represent this another way?" |
| CONNECT | "This relates to what we saw in the garden..." | "Where else have you seen this?" |

When students use reasoning moves independently, the educator celebrates: "Great QUESTIONING! That's exactly what a scientist would ask."

## Scaffolding Without Giving Answers

The Reflect phase requires a specific instructional stance: the educator facilitates thinking rather than transmitting knowledge.

**Instead of**: "The answer is 15 because you multiply 3 times 5."
**Use**: "What operation do you think might help here? What clues does the problem give you?"

**Instead of**: "That's wrong, the answer is 24."
**Use**: "Interesting thinking. Walk me through how you got there." (Then transition to Restore phase if needed.)

**Instead of**: "Good job, that's correct!"
**Use**: "You got 24. Before I say anything, how confident are you? How would you check?"

## Bloom's Taxonomy Alignment

The Reflect phase targets the upper levels of Bloom's taxonomy:

- **Remember/Understand**: Used only as warm-up or retrieval practice at the start of the phase.
- **Apply**: Students apply concepts in garden-based or real-world contexts.
- **Analyze**: Students break apart problems, compare approaches, identify patterns.
- **Evaluate**: Students judge the quality of reasoning, critique solutions, assess strategies.
- **Create**: Students design problems, propose solutions, generate questions, create representations.

## Monitoring During Reflect

Even during active learning, the educator continuously monitors for dysregulation:

- Watch for error cascades (sudden increase in mistakes after success)
- Notice body language shifts (slouching, fidgeting, avoidance)
- Listen for negative self-talk ("This is stupid," "I can't do this")
- Track engagement patterns (decreasing response length, off-topic deflection)

If dysregulation is detected, the session transitions to the Regulate phase without shame: "I can see your brain is working really hard. Want to take a quick break?"

## Transition to Next Phase

- **To Restore**: When a student makes an error that requires reframing and deeper analysis.
- **To Regulate**: When dysregulation signals are detected (regulation level drops below 40).
- **To Reconnect**: When the learning objective has been met and mastery is demonstrated.
