# Root Phase

## Grounding & Foundation

The Root phase is the entry point for every learning session in the RootWork Framework. Before any academic content is introduced, students must establish a felt sense of safety and readiness. This phase draws on polyvagal theory and attachment research to ensure that the autonomic nervous system is in a state conducive to learning.

Just as a plant cannot grow without establishing its root system in stable soil, a student cannot engage in higher-order thinking without first feeling grounded, safe, and connected.

## Purpose

The Root phase serves three interconnected functions:

1. **Safety assessment** -- Determine whether the student feels physically and emotionally safe enough to engage in learning.
2. **Readiness check** -- Gauge the student's current regulation level and cognitive availability.
3. **Intention setting** -- Establish a shared purpose for the learning session, giving the student agency over their goals.

## Key Activities

### Grounding Check-In

Every session begins with a brief, non-invasive check-in. The educator or RootGuide tutor opens with a warm, predictable greeting that signals safety:

- "How are you feeling today? Ready to grow some thinking?"
- "What's one thing you noticed on your way here today?"
- "If your energy were a weather pattern right now, what would it be?"

These questions are deliberately open-ended and non-academic. They serve as a regulation barometer, helping the educator assess whether the student is within their window of tolerance.

### Sensory Grounding Exercises

When a student arrives in a heightened or withdrawn state, the Root phase includes brief sensory grounding techniques:

- **5-4-3-2-1 Grounding**: Name 5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you can touch, 2 you smell, 1 you taste.
- **Garden Grounding**: Hold a seed, feel the soil, smell an herb -- use the garden environment to anchor to the present.
- **Breathing Roots**: Breathe in while imagining roots growing down from your feet into the earth; breathe out while imagining the roots spreading wide.

### Transition Rituals

Consistent rituals help students transition from the unpredictability of their day into the structured safety of the learning environment:

- Entering the garden space or learning lab
- Putting on a garden apron or gloves
- Watering a personal plant
- Writing or drawing in a "growth journal"

These rituals create predictability, which is particularly important for students who have experienced trauma and may lack consistent structure in other areas of life.

## Regulation Level Assessment

During the Root phase, the educator or AI tutor assigns an initial regulation level (0-100 scale):

| Level | State | Indicator | Action |
|-------|-------|-----------|--------|
| 81-100 | Optimal | Engaged, responsive, curious | Proceed to Reflect |
| 61-80 | Adequate | Present but slightly distracted | Brief grounding, then Reflect |
| 41-60 | Borderline | Restless, unfocused, flat affect | Extended grounding, reassess |
| 21-40 | Dysregulated | Agitated, withdrawn, avoidant | Move to Regulate phase |
| 0-20 | Crisis | Distressed, dissociated, unsafe | Escalation protocol |

A regulation level above 60 permits transition to the Reflect phase. Below 40 triggers a move to the Regulate phase. Between 40 and 60, the educator uses professional judgment to determine whether extended grounding or co-regulation is needed.

## Garden Connection

The Root phase has a direct physical analog in the garden: preparing the soil. Before planting, gardeners test the soil, remove debris, add compost, and create the conditions for growth. Students engage in parallel activities:

- Testing soil pH (connecting to science content)
- Clearing weeds (a physical metaphor for clearing mental clutter)
- Preparing planting beds (creating space for new ideas)

These activities serve dual purposes: they provide a grounding physical task while naturally introducing academic content in a low-stakes context.

## Grade Band Implementation

### K-2
- Adult-led check-in with picture cards (weather, emoji faces)
- Physical grounding activities (jumping, stretching, touching soil)
- Short transition songs or chants
- Duration: 5-8 minutes

### 3-5
- Student-selected check-in format (verbal, written, drawing)
- Garden-based grounding activities
- Beginning journaling
- Duration: 5-7 minutes

### 6-8
- Self-assessment check-in using regulation scale
- Peer grounding partnerships
- Growth journal entries
- Duration: 3-5 minutes

### 9-12
- Self-directed grounding with minimal scaffolding
- Mindfulness or reflection journaling
- Mentor check-ins with younger students
- Duration: 2-5 minutes (student-paced)

## Educator Role

During the Root phase, the educator's primary role is to be a **non-anxious presence**. This means:

- Maintaining calm, warm body language regardless of student behavior
- Avoiding questions that could trigger shame ("Why are you late?" "What's wrong?")
- Offering choice and control ("Would you like to start with your plant or your journal?")
- Observing without diagnosing -- noting dysregulation signals without labeling the student
- Modeling grounding behaviors themselves

## Transition to Next Phase

The Root phase transitions based on the grounding check outcome:

- **To Reflect**: Student demonstrates readiness (regulation level > 60, engaged eye contact, responsive to questions, expressed willingness to learn).
- **To Regulate**: Student shows signs of dysregulation (regulation level < 40, avoidance, negative self-talk, physical agitation or withdrawal).

The transition is always communicated transparently: "It seems like you might need a moment before we dive in. Want to visit the calm corner?" or "You seem ready to grow! Let's get into it."
